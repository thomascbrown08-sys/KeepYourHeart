Part IV · Loving · Chapter Seventeen

Burdens and Loads

On the two sentences in Galatians 6 that have been quietly running every argument you have ever had about how much of another person is yours.

Paul says two things three verses apart and does not appear to notice a problem.

Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ. (Galatians 6:2)

For each will have to bear his own load. (Galatians 6:5)

Every debate about codependency, enabling, boundaries, over-functioning, rescuing, and whether you are permitted to stop answering the phone at eleven at night is a fight between those two verses. And most of us have picked one, built a life on it, and quietly regarded the other as an inconvenience.

Two different words

The apparent contradiction dissolves the moment you look at the Greek, because Paul is not using the same word twice.

Terms
Barē (v. 2, “burdens”)
Crushing weights. Something heavy enough to break the person under it — an excess, an overload, more than one set of shoulders was built for.
Phortion (v. 5, “load”)
A pack. The word used for a soldier’s kit or a ship’s cargo — what is properly and appropriately yours to carry. It is the same word Jesus uses when he says his own is light.

So the two instructions are not in tension at all. A burden is what will crush you alone. A load is what is properly yours. Help with the first. Carry the second.

Warrant · Interpretation

Galatians 6:2, 6:5 — the two commands, and the distinction between barē and phortion.

The two commands are directly stated. The weight placed on the vocabulary distinction is interpretation, and I should be honest that not every commentator leans on it as hard as this chapter does — some read the difference as stylistic. It is a well-attested and widely held reading, the words do carry those senses, and the resulting framework is genuinely useful. But it is a reading, and if it does not persuade you, the practical conclusions below still stand on the plain fact that Paul commands both without embarrassment.

A burden is what will crush you alone. A load is what is properly yours. Paul uses two different words three verses apart, and every argument you have ever had about how much of somebody else is yours to carry is downstream of confusing them.

The two failures, and the uncomfortable part

Get this wrong in the first direction and you carry loads that were never yours.

This one wears a halo, which is what makes it so hard to see. You handle things for people. You anticipate. You smooth. You make the phone call they should be making, you manage the consequence they should be feeling, you carry the pack that was issued to them.

And the damage is not only to you. It is to them, because a person whose loads are carried by somebody else never develops the shoulders. You have removed the thing that would have grown them, and you have done it out of what genuinely feels like love. It usually is love, mixed with something else.

Get it wrong in the other direction and you refuse to let anyone near your burdens.

This one wears armour rather than a halo. You are fine. It is under control. There is no need to trouble anyone. And we tend to file that under strength or humility, when Galatians 6:2 files it under disobedience — because bearing one another’s burdens is a command, and a command has two ends. Somebody has to be carried, or the instruction cannot be obeyed. Refusing to be the carried one is not modesty. It is opting out of the arrangement.

Now the uncomfortable part, and I include myself.

Most of us do both at once. The same person, in the same week, carrying loads that were never theirs and declining help with a burden that is crushing them. It looks like two opposite problems and it is one, because both are ways of arranging never to need anybody. One puts you in the position of the giver, which is safe. The other keeps you out of the position of the receiver, which is not.

Carrying what was never yours and refusing help with what is — these look like opposite faults and they are the same one. Both are ways of arranging never to need anybody.

The test is the engine, not the behaviour

Which means you cannot sort this by looking at what you did, and this is where most teaching on boundaries goes wrong.

Because Christianity does not make self-protection the highest good. It assumes a legitimate regard for yourself — love your neighbour as yourself takes that for granted as the measure — and it then subordinates it. The one we follow laid down his life, and we are told to love as he loved. Any framework that puts your own preservation at the top of the list is not going to survive contact with the New Testament, and it should not.

So the question is never how much am I carrying. It is whose is it, and did I choose it, and why.

Sacrificial love and compulsive rescue can produce absolutely identical behaviour. Same phone call, same late night, same money. The difference is underneath — which is chapter two, arriving now for the last time.

  • Sacrificial love is chosen, and it is free. It is offered by someone who could have declined and did not. It has an end. It does not require the other person to be grateful, because it was never a transaction.
  • Compulsive rescue is not chosen and is not free. It is done because not doing it is unbearable — because of what their disappointment would mean about you. It has no end, because the thing it is really trying to secure cannot be secured this way. And it quietly generates resentment, which is the reliable tell, because resentment is what a transaction produces when the payment does not come.

Sacrificial love and compulsive rescue can look identical from outside. The tell is resentment — because a gift does not generate an invoice, and a transaction always does.

And notice the diagnostic that gives you. If you are carrying something and quietly keeping score, you have not been giving. You have been lending, at interest, to somebody who never agreed to the terms.

He also went to bed

The obvious objection is that Christ gave everything, so any limit is a compromise.

Except that the same Christ, as we saw in chapter fifteen, left a town with sick people still in it. He withdrew to pray, repeatedly, while need was outstanding. He slept. He did not heal everyone in first-century Palestine, and it was not because he ran out of power.

He gave his life once, deliberately, at the appointed time, in an act he described as something nobody took from him but that he laid down of his own accord. That is not the same thing as being worn away by everyone who wanted a piece of him, and the Gospels are careful to show the difference.

Christ laid down his life freely and at the time he chose. He also went to bed with people still sick in Capernaum. Both are in the same Gospel and neither cancels the other.

You are a part

Underneath all of this is a picture of the church that most of us assent to and few of us inhabit.

Paul says the church is a body and you are a part of it. Not a smaller version of the whole thing. A part — which means limited by design, dependent by design, insufficient by design, and not defective for being so. He says the eye cannot say to the hand I have no need of you, and the striking thing about that sentence is where the error is located: not in the hand for needing help, but in the eye for claiming it does not.

Warrant · Direct

1 Corinthians 12:12–27 — the body, its many members, and the eye that may not say to the hand I have no need of you.

Directly stated, at length. The passage locates the fault in the member who denies needing the others, which is precisely the socially respectable failure of the two in this chapter.

Case · both at once

“G.” is fifty and is the person everyone in her family calls. She manages her brother’s appointments, mediates between her adult children, and has covered her nephew’s rent twice. She would describe none of this as a burden; she would say it is what family is.

Her own marriage has been in trouble for two years. Nobody knows. Not her closest friend, not her sister, not the small group she has attended for a decade. When asked why, she says she does not want to add to anyone’s plate, and she means it sincerely.

G. is carrying four loads that belong to other adults and one burden that is crushing her, alone. She experiences the first as love and the second as consideration. They are the same decision made twice.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

Why standard boundary language sometimes fails with religious clients. Framing limits primarily as self-care or self-protection can read to them as a competing value system rather than a clinical recommendation, and is quietly resisted — because their tradition genuinely does call for self-giving, and they are not wrong about that.

What this chapter does instead. It grounds limits in a distinction the client’s own text supplies (crushing weight versus proper responsibility) and shifts the test from how much to whose, and freely chosen or not. That permits genuinely costly love to remain fully available while identifying the compulsive version as a different phenomenon with a different driver.

Two things directly usable in session. First, resentment as the diagnostic — the client can be taught to read their own resentment as evidence that a transaction was underway rather than a gift, which sidesteps the usual defensiveness. Second, the observation that over-functioning and help-refusal frequently co-occur in the same client and share a driver; treating them as one presentation rather than two is often more productive.

Watch for the client using the load/burden distinction to justify withdrawal from genuine obligation. The chapter forecloses this by insisting that self-protection is not the goal, but the shortcut is available.

In My Toolbox

The clinical companion to this chapter:

  • Parentification — carrying adult loads as a child, which is where the habit is usually learned.
  • Family systems — how a family assigns roles and what happens when someone stops playing theirs.
Practice

Sort the pile. List everything you are currently carrying for other people — all of it, including the small things. Beside each, write one letter: B if it would genuinely crush them alone, L if it is a competent adult’s own pack. Expect the L column to be longer than you would like.

Then run the engine test on the L column only. For each: what would happen if I stopped? and then the real question, what would it mean about me if they were disappointed? That second answer is what you are actually carrying.

Check for the invoice. Where are you keeping score? Name one thing you have done for someone that you are quietly waiting to be recognized for. That is not a gift and it will not stop hurting until it is either given away properly or set down.

If you have never had a setting where honesty was safe, deciding who is a prior question — The Room With No Cost gives a test for it.

The hard one: name one burden and hand it over. Not a general request for prayer. One specific thing, to one specific person, this week, in plain words. If you did chapter twelve’s assignment, you already have a Hopeful; use them.

Journaling.

  1. Which failure is yours — carrying too much, or letting nobody near? Then check honestly whether the answer is both.
  2. Who would you be if you stopped being the person who handles things? Sit with that one; it is usually the real question.
  3. When did you last let someone carry something for you? If you cannot remember, that is the finding.

To bring to your counselor. “I take on other people’s responsibilities and I don’t let anyone help with mine. I think those are the same problem. Can we look at it that way?”
