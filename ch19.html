Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Nineteen

Care That Was Not Care

On control that arrives under the name of concern, and why it is so hard to object to.

Somebody does something to you, and calls it worry about you.

Restricts you, and calls it protecting you. Reads what you did not offer, and calls it paying attention. Requires an account of you and gives nothing back, and calls it help. And every one of those has a word attached that makes objecting to it look like ingratitude.

That is the trap, and it is worth being precise about where the injury actually is. The injury is not only the control. It is the name.

Because control called control can be resisted. You can say no to it, argue with it, name it to somebody else. Control called care removes all three. Object, and you are the person who threw kindness back in someone's face. Say nothing, and you have agreed with the account.

Control named as control can be resisted. Control named as care cannot — because objecting to it makes you the ungrateful one, which is what the name was for.

In My Toolbox

The clinical vocabulary: Coercive control for the pattern itself; Institutional betrayal where the party is an organization that promised protection; and DARVO for what frequently happens when you name it.

God spends a chapter on this

I did not expect to find a whole chapter of Scripture devoted to it, and I want to lay it out properly, because reading it for the first time is a particular experience for anyone who has been on the receiving end.

Ezekiel 34 is God prosecuting the shepherds of Israel — the leadership, the people whose job was the care of everybody else. And he does it in the first person, at length, in detail.

The charges begin with what they took: you eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones, but you do not feed the sheep.

And then he itemizes the failures, and notice that almost every one of them is an omission:

The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought. Ezekiel 34:4

Five things that should have happened and did not. That is the substance of the charge — not that they attacked the flock, but that they occupied the role of care and did not do the work of it while continuing to take the wool.

And then one commission, and it is the phrase that stops people: with force and harshness you have ruled them.

The role was shepherd. The activity was ruling. That gap is the whole indictment, and it is the same gap as the word that does not match the act.

Warrant · Direct

Ezekiel 34:2–6 — the shepherds who feed themselves and not the flock; the five omissions; “with force and harshness you have ruled them.” (ESV)

Directly stated, at length, in the first person. Note the target: this is not a general complaint about wickedness. It is aimed specifically at people occupying a role of care and using it for something else.

And then he does not stop at condemning them

What follows is the part that matters most for anyone reading this.

God does not say I will send better shepherds — or rather, he says that eventually, but first he says something more direct.

Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out… I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak. Ezekiel 34:11, 16

Read that list against the earlier one. It is the same five items, in the same order, reversed from accusation into promise. Every single thing the shepherds failed to do, he says he will do himself.

He is not merely disapproving of them. He is taking the job.

Warrant · Direct

Ezekiel 34:11–16 — “I, I myself will search for my sheep… I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak.” (ESV)

Directly stated. The correspondence between the charge-list in v.4 and the promise-list in v.16 is in the text, item for item.

Every failure God charges the shepherds with in verse four, he promises to perform himself in verse sixteen. Same list, same order, accusation turned into promise.

And there is something practical in that which I did not expect. You do not have to prosecute this. A great deal of energy goes into trying to prove that what was done to you was not what it was called — to a family, a board, a congregation, an agency, usually unsuccessfully. Ezekiel 34 means the case has already been heard by the only party whose verdict is final, and it did not require your testimony to be believed first.

The New Testament keeps the same language

Peter, writing to elders, uses the shepherd word and attaches three conditions that read like a direct inheritance from Ezekiel: not under compulsion, not for shameful gain, and — the one that matters here — not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples.

And Jesus describes the religious leadership of his own day in one sentence that anybody who has been helped expensively will recognize: they tie up heavy burdens and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger.

Set that beside what he says about his own: my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. Same category, opposite weight. The contrast is deliberate and he draws it himself.

Warrant · Direct

1 Peter 5:2–3 — shepherd the flock, not under compulsion, not for shameful gain, not domineering; Matthew 23:4 against Matthew 11:30.

All directly stated. The Matthew pair is a deliberate contrast within the same Gospel and the same vocabulary of burdens.

Two tests you can actually run

Because the whole difficulty is that from inside, and often from outside, the counterfeit looks like the real thing. Two questions separate them reliably.

1. What happens when you improve?

Care is glad when you no longer need it. That is its entire aim; a person who genuinely wants you well is pleased by the evidence that you are.

Control is threatened by exactly the same fact, because your need was the mechanism. Watch what happens when you get better, get further, or stop requiring the thing. If competence produces warmth, it was care. If competence produces coolness, a new concern, or a fresh reason you are not ready — you have your answer, and you did not have to prove anything to anybody to get it.

Care is glad when you no longer need it. Control is threatened by the same fact. Watch what happens when you improve; it answers the question faster than any argument.

2. What did it cost the giver?

Real care costs something — time, money, inconvenience, reputation, the giver's own comfort. Control frequently costs nothing at all and purchases a great deal: information, compliance, standing, the appearance of virtue. When you cannot identify what an act of concern cost the person performing it, that is worth noticing. It does not prove anything on its own. It is a place to look.

The price attached to help

There is a specific version of this that deserves its own paragraph, because it is so common that people assume it is simply how help works.

It is help delivered with condescension attached — assistance that arrives bundled with an explanation of what is wrong with you, usually at a moment when you cannot reply and leave on your own terms. The help may be real. The bill is the tone.

Watch how Jesus does it instead, because the contrast is unusually clean. A blind beggar is shouting at him from the roadside — a man with no standing, no privacy, and an obvious need that anybody present could have named for him. And Jesus asks: what do you want me to do for you?

He asks. A man who was blind, poor, and shouting in the road was asked what he wanted rather than told. Nobody assumed on his behalf, and nobody explained him to himself first.

A blind beggar shouting in the road, and Jesus asked him what he wanted. Not one person in that crowd would have thought the question necessary. That is the difference.

Three guards, and the third is the hard one

Not everyone who helped you badly was a counterfeit. Some help was genuine and clumsy. Some people did the wrong thing out of real love and no skill. A frame this sharp will happily reclassify all of it, and if you let it, you will lose the actual care that was in your history along with the rest.

Ezekiel condemns bad shepherds, not shepherding. The chapter ends with God promising to set a shepherd over them, not with the abolition of the office. Legitimate authority exists, and the argument here is against its counterfeit, not against the thing being counterfeited.

And the one that matters most: this frame can become a way to refuse all correction. If every rebuke can be reclassified as control, and every requirement as domination, then you have made yourself permanently unteachable — and chapter seventeen's lit room needs somebody who can actually receive a correction without it detonating.

The distinction is not tone and it is not comfort. Correction aims at your capability. Control aims at your compliance. A rebuke that leaves you more able is the real thing even when it stings, and a kindness that leaves you smaller is not, however gently it was delivered.

If every correction becomes control, you have made yourself unteachable. The test is not how it felt. It is whether you came out of it more able or more managed.

Case · the improvement

“D.” was helped through a genuinely bad period by someone who drove him to appointments, took his calls at night, and told him more than once how far gone he had been when they found him.

Eighteen months later D. is working, housed, and steady. He has noticed that the phone calls have changed. There is a new worry now — that he is moving too fast, that he does not see how fragile it all is, that he has forgotten what he was like. He cannot point to anything untrue in it, and it arrives with obvious concern.

What D. has not let himself think is that every piece of good news he brings is met with a reason it is premature. He is not being lied to. He is being needed, and the need has begun to require his fragility.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

Why this may present as resistance. A client with a history of control delivered under the language of concern will be primed to detect the same structure in helping relationships, including this one. That is not paranoia and it is not transference in the loose sense; it is a trained discrimination that was accurate for a long time and does not switch off because the current setting is different.

What the chapter provides. Two functional tests — whether the helper is pleased or threatened by the client's improvement, and what the help costs the helper. Both are usable in session and both are things you can invite the client to apply to you, which is frequently more effective than reassurance and costs very little.

The guard the chapter builds in, and it is worth reinforcing: the frame can be used to reclassify all correction as control, producing an unteachable stance. The offered discriminator is outcome rather than affect — correction aims at capability, control aims at compliance — so a client can be held to it without arguing about whether something felt bad.

Note also that naming this pattern accurately can produce a period of anger at people the client had previously defended. That is usually movement rather than deterioration, but it is worth anticipating.

Practice

Read Ezekiel 34 in one sitting. All of it. It takes about five minutes. Mark verse 4 and verse 16 and read them against each other — the charge list and the promise list, item for item.

Run the improvement test. Think of one person or institution whose care you have been uncertain about. Then ask: what happened, or what would happen, when you needed them less? Do not answer from theory; find an actual instance.

Name one word that did not match. One occasion where an act was given a name it did not deserve. Write both: what it was called, and what it was. Seeing the two side by side in your own handwriting does something that thinking about it does not.

Then run the third guard on yourself. Find one correction you received that you have filed as control. Re-examine it honestly against the test: did it aim at your capability or your compliance? Some will survive the re-examination. Some will not, and finding one that does not is the healthiest possible outcome of this chapter.

Journaling.

  1. Which words have been used on you that did not match the act? List them. Most people find the same two or three words recur across decades and different people.
  2. What has help cost you, historically? Not what it gave — what the bill was.
  3. Where are you doing this to someone else? Everyone learns the grammar they were raised in. This is the least comfortable question in the chapter and the most useful.

To bring to your counselor. “I have trouble telling care from control, because the ones I grew up with used the same words. Can we work out what tests actually distinguish them?”
