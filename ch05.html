Part II · Attending · Chapter Five

Before the Face of God

On the loneliest instruction in the skills manual, and what the Christian tradition puts in its place.

In chapter one I said something I now have to pay for.

I said that criticism which leaves a person emptier than it found them is not discernment but vandalism — that you are not permitted to take a practice out of somebody’s hands unless you have something better to put in them. Part II is where that bill comes due. So the critique in these four chapters will be short, and the replacement will be long, and if I fail to hand you something you can actually do, then the whole argument collapses and you should keep doing what you were doing.

Let me start by saying what the skill genuinely gives you, because it is not nothing.

What it does, and why it works

The instruction is roughly: notice what is happening inside you, describe it in plain words, and do not immediately grab hold of it.

What that produces — and this is a real achievement — is a gap. Ordinarily a feeling arrives and you are it instantly; there is no daylight between the wave and you. The practice opens perhaps two seconds of daylight, and in those two seconds something becomes visible that was invisible before: that a feeling is an event rather than a verdict. Dread shows up and announces that everything is ruined. In the gap, you can see that dread has shown up and is making an announcement, and that those are two different facts.

Anyone who tells you this is worthless has not tried it while drowning. It works, the evidence for it is substantial, and a Christian has no business sneering at it.

And the specific instruction to describe before evaluating — the thing usually reported as non-judgmental, which sets off every alarm a Christian has — is mostly a sequencing rule, not a ban on moral judgment. Get the facts down before you pass sentence. Proverbs says that answering before you have heard is folly and shame. We have had that rule for a long time.

The question underneath

So the snag is not in the technique. It is in a question the technique never asks, and you will only notice it if you sit in the practice long enough.

Observed by whom?

The exercise asks you to become two people. One half of you feels; the other half stands slightly back and takes notes. And for most of us on most days that is fine — it is a useful piece of interior furniture.

But ask the obvious question and the floor gets thin. What is the observing half standing on? It is not more stable than the feeling half; it is made of the same material. When you are well, the watcher is calm. When you are not, the watcher is frightened too, or contemptuous, or has quietly changed jobs from observer to prosecutor — and now you are not practising a skill. You are under surveillance, conducted by yourself, in a locked room, with no possibility of appeal.

Self-observation is only restful if the observer is kind. Yours is made of the same weather as the rest of you, which is why on the worst days the practice stops being attention and becomes a stakeout.

Nor is that the deepest issue. The deepest issue is quieter and takes longer to surface: you are alone in there. The entire transaction — the noticing, the naming, the letting go — happens between you and you. Nobody else is present. For a certain kind of suffering that is precisely the problem, because what has gone wrong is not primarily a failure of attention. It is that a person has been unheld for a very long time, and no amount of skilful self-attention supplies being held.

You were never the only one in the room

Now the turn, and I want to make it with a story rather than an argument, because the argument will not do the work.

Somewhere around the nineteenth century BC, a pregnant Egyptian slave named Hagar ran into the desert. She had been used by a household as an instrument, then resented for succeeding at it, then treated harshly enough that a pregnant woman alone in a wilderness looked like the better option. Nobody in the story is on her side. She is not the covenant wife, she is not the heir, she is not even free.

God finds her at a spring. Not a metaphor — the text says the angel of the Lord found her, in the wilderness, by the water on the road.

And afterwards she does something nobody else in the Hebrew Scriptures does. She gives God a name. Not a patriarch, not a prophet, not a king — a foreign slave woman who had run out of options in a desert is the one person in the entire Old Testament who names God, and the name she picks is the God who sees me.

Warrant · Direct

Genesis 16:13 — “So she called the name of the Lord who spoke to her, ‘You are a God of seeing,’ for she said, ‘Truly here I have seen him who looks after me.’” (ESV)

Directly stated. The detail that she is the only person in the Hebrew Scriptures to name God is an observation about the text rather than a claim the text makes, but it is the text that puts the name in her mouth and nowhere else.

The only person in the Old Testament who gives God a name is a pregnant slave with no standing, no protector, and no destination. She calls him the God who sees me. She did not need a technique for being seen. She needed to find out she already was.

Coram Deo

Term
Coram Deo
Latin: before the face of God. The conviction that every moment of a human life is lived in the presence of a God who is personally attending to it — not observed by a system, not logged by a mechanism, but looked at by somebody. The Reformers used it constantly; it is one of the load-bearing ideas of the whole tradition.

Here is the whole difference between the two practices, and it is a difference of one word.

Mindfulness says: observe yourself.

The Christian tradition says: you are observed.

Passive rather than active, and the passive voice is doing enormous work. In the first, attention is a task you perform and can fail at. In the second, attention is a fact about your situation that was true before you woke up and stays true when you are too ill to practise anything. On the days you cannot manage the exercise, the exercise stops. The looking does not.

Psalm 139 is the great text here, and notice the direction the searching runs. David does not say I have examined myself thoroughly. He says you have searched me and known me — you know when I sit down and when I rise up, you discern my thoughts from far away, you are acquainted with all my ways. God’s knowledge of him came first and goes further than his own ever will.

And then, at the end, having spent the whole psalm establishing that God already knows everything, David asks him to search him. Which would be redundant, except that the point was never information. The point is that self-knowledge is safest when it is conducted in company.

Warrant · Direct / Entailed · Cornerstone

Psalm 139:1–6, 23–24 — “O Lord, you have searched me and known me… Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!” (ESV)

Cornerstone for the whole of Part II. Directly stated: God’s knowledge of the person precedes and exceeds their self-knowledge, and the psalm ends by inviting the searching rather than performing it alone. Entailed: that self-examination is therefore meant to be conducted in company rather than in isolation — which is the argument of chapter eight, and the reason this psalm gets marked as load-bearing rather than merely useful.

Calvin builds most of his account of self-knowledge on exactly this footing: that we never see ourselves accurately by looking harder at ourselves, but only by first looking at God, because there is nothing to measure against until then. Which is a claim not just about theology but about optics. You cannot see the shape of a room by staring at the floor.

The part that ought to embarrass us

The tradition has an enormous literature on this. It is called recollection — the deliberate practice of bringing yourself back into conscious awareness of God’s presence during the ordinary hours, again and again, as often as you drift.

The best-loved book on it in any language was written by a French kitchen worker. Nicolas Herman was a soldier and then a footman, and by his own account clumsy at both; he ended up in his forties as a lay brother in a Paris monastery, assigned to the kitchen, which he disliked. He is remembered as Brother Lawrence, and the small collection of his letters and conversations that survives makes one argument: that he had learned to remain in conversation with God while washing up, and that the washing up was not an interruption of the spiritual life but the location of it.

He was a seventeenth-century Carmelite, which is to say a Roman Catholic, and I am naming that rather than smuggling it. I include him for two reasons. The first is that his practice is doctrinally unloaded — there is nothing in it a Protestant needs to strip out. The second is the point I made in chapter one and cannot avoid here: when a person goes looking for practical instruction on attending to God during ordinary work, the best short book available to them was written by a Catholic kitchen brother in 1690, and that is a fact about our shelves, not about his.

Warrant · Historical

Calvin, Institutes I.i.1–2 (knowledge of God and of self as mutually dependent, with knowledge of God first); Brother Lawrence, The Practice of the Presence of God (compiled 1691).

Historical warrant. Calvin is a primary voice of this tradition; Brother Lawrence is outside it and is cited for a practice rather than a doctrine. Neither is Scripture, and the argument above rests on Psalm 139 and Genesis 16 regardless.

The practice

So here is the thing to put in your hand. It is three beats instead of two, and the third beat is the entire difference.

The three beats
  1. Notice. Exactly as you were taught. Something is happening in you. Do not fight it, do not narrate it, just register that it is here. Something has arrived.
  2. Name. Put one plain word on it, and prefer the smaller word. Not my life is falling apart but dread. Not everyone despises me but shame. The description is not yet a verdict, and you are not obliged to decide yet whether it is telling the truth.
  3. Address. Say it to God. Out loud if you can manage it, in four or five words. “Lord, this is dread.” “Father, I am ashamed.” That is the whole third beat. It is not a prayer request, it is not a resolution, and it does not require you to feel anything.

Beats one and two you may already have. Beat three costs about two seconds and changes the character of the other two, because the moment you address it to somebody you have stopped being alone in the room with it. That is not a psychological trick. It is a report on where you actually were the whole time.

Do not expect the feeling to lift. Often it will not, and if you practise this expecting relief you will conclude it does not work and abandon it. What changes first is not the intensity. It is the company.

Noticing is a skill. Naming is a skill. Addressing is a relationship — and it is the only one of the three that changes what the noticing costs you.

Case · the stakeout

“T.” is fifty-two, retired early on medical grounds, and has been practising mindfulness daily for a year and a half. He is extremely good at it. He can identify a rising anxiety within seconds and name it precisely.

He also reports that the practice has begun to feel, in his words, like keeping records for someone who is going to review them. He cannot say who. On bad weeks the observing has an edge to it that he recognizes from his father: not hostile exactly, but never satisfied. He assumed this meant he was doing it wrong and doubled the duration, which made it considerably worse.

T. does not have a technique problem. He has an audience problem — he has been watched for eighteen months by someone he has never identified, and the identification turned out to matter more than any refinement to the method.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What is being added, in translatable terms. This chapter does not replace the observing skill; it adds a third step in which the client directs the observation outward to a personal addressee. Functionally it converts a solitary metacognitive task into a relational one.

Why that may matter clinically. Self-monitoring is not affectively neutral. In clients with harsh internalized figures, the observing stance can acquire the tone of the critical caregiver, at which point the practice reinforces the very schema it was meant to loosen. This is a known failure mode and often presents as “mindfulness makes me worse,” frequently mislabelled as poor technique. The reframe supplies a benign attentional other, which for a religious client is not imagined but believed to be already present.

What to watch. If the client’s God-concept is itself punitive, this step will import the harshness rather than relieve it, and the practice will make things worse in a way that looks identical from outside. If beat three lands badly — if addressing God increases distress rather than steadying it — that is diagnostically important and worth pursuing directly rather than treating as resistance.

Practice

This week. Add beat three to whatever you are already doing. Do not change beats one and two, do not lengthen your sessions, and do not evaluate whether it is working for at least a week.

Recollection — the short version. Pick one ordinary repeated moment: the kettle, the commute, the walk from the car. Once a day, at that moment, say six words to God about what is actually happening in you. Not a devotional. Six words. Brother Lawrence’s whole method, minus three hundred years of kitchen work.

Journaling.

  1. When you observe yourself, what is the tone of the observer? Write the voice down as dialogue. Whose voice is it? Most of us can answer that faster than we would like.
  2. Read Psalm 139 slowly and answer honestly: does being fully known feel like safety or like exposure? Both answers are common and the honest one is more useful than the correct one.
  3. Where does “the God who sees me” land — as comfort or as threat? That answer is the real subject of chapter eight, so do not resolve it now. Just write it down.

To bring to your counselor. “When I observe myself, the observer is not neutral. Can we look at who I think is watching?”
