Lament Finder

Whatever you have got, somebody already prayed it and it was published.

Chapter ten argued that roughly a third of the Psalms are complaints and that the etiquette forbidding them was never in the Bible. This page is that argument made usable at eleven at night, when you do not have the capacity to go looking.

Find the row that is closest. Read the psalm out loud, slowly, all the way to the end — including the parts that do not resolve. You are not looking for a verse to make you feel better. You are looking for someone who has already said it, so that you know it can be said.

How to use this

Start with the first psalm listed; it is the one that fits most people most often. The others are there if the first does not land.

Do not skip to the turn. If the psalm ends in the dark, let it. If it turns and you cannot turn with it, stop where you are — that is a legitimate place to finish, and chapter ten explains why.

If nothing on this page fits, go to Psalm 88. It is the one that never resolves, and it is here for exactly this.

A note on the ones that ask God to break teeth

If you land in Psalm 109 or 69 or 137, you will find requests that are genuinely shocking. Chapter ten deals with these at more length. The short version: the psalmist is handing his rage to the only judge who can be trusted with it, rather than acting on it, and the psalm is what he does instead of the revenge.

How Christians should pray these is a live disagreement and this book takes no position. That there is somewhere to put the fury other than into a person is not in dispute.

If you only take one

“How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?”

Psalm 13:1 (ESV)

Six verses long. Starts in accusation, ends in singing, and does not explain how it got from one to the other. Most of us live somewhere in the middle of it.
