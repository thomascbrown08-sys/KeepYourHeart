How Scripture Is Cited Here

Six tiers, so you always know how much weight a verse is being asked to carry — and a full index, so you can check.

Most Christian writing cites Scripture at one volume. A verse that says the thing outright and a verse that has been stretched to fit both arrive in the same font, with the same authority, and the reader has no way to tell them apart without doing the work themselves.

That is not honest, and it is particularly not honest in a book like this one, which is being handed to people in distress who are in no position to audit anything. So every scriptural claim here is labelled with how hard it is working. You should be able to disagree with me and see exactly where.

The labels also mean something practical: the lower tiers can be removed without the argument collapsing. Where a chapter rests on an illustration or a disputed reading, it says so and names what the argument would still stand on.

The six tiers

Direct

The text says the thing. Nothing is inferred, nothing is stretched, and the claim in the chapter is the claim in the verse. This is the strongest tier and most of the book rests on it.

Cornerstone · Direct / Entailed

Reserved, and used three times in the whole book. Marks a passage the argument structurally depends on, where something is directly stated and something further follows from it. If one of these three is wrong, large parts of the book fall down with it. They are: Philippians 2:12–13 (the engine), Psalm 139 (attending), and 1 John 3:19–20 (assurance under self-accusation).

Entailed

The text does not say the thing, but the thing follows from what it says — usually assembled from a pattern across several passages rather than stated anywhere in those words. Sound, but one inferential step further out than Direct, and the box says which step.

Interpretation

A reading. Defensible, held by serious people, and not the only possible one. Used for illustrations the text did not intend as principles, and for places where lexical or contextual weight is being placed on something that could bear less. Every one of these boxes names what the argument falls back on if you reject the reading.

Contested

Genuinely disputed among faithful Christians, or genuinely uncertain in the text itself. Includes textual questions (which manuscripts have a passage) and interpretive ones (who Romans 7 is describing). The book does not resolve these and says so.

Historical

Not Scripture. The judgment of a voice in the tradition — a theologian, a pastor, a poet. Carries real weight and not scriptural weight. Two sub-grades sit slightly higher and are labelled where used: creedal (Chalcedon) and confessional (Westminster). Literary (Bunyan, Herbert) sits lowest of all and proves nothing on its own.

What is deliberately not here

No tier for “a verse that felt right.” If a passage could not be placed in one of the six, it did not go in the book.

And one rule governs all of them, stated in chapter four and worth repeating: hold what Scripture asserts as firmly as Scripture asserts it, and hold what Scripture leaves open as loosely as Scripture leaves it. Most damage in Christian argument comes from getting those two levels the wrong way round.
