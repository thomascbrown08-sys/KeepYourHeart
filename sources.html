Sources & Further Reading

What this book is built out of, with honest notes on what each one is actually like to read.

Most reading lists are inventories. This one tries to tell you which of these would help you, and which would be a bad idea in a bad week.

Scripture

English Standard Version (ESV) is used for all quotation in the teaching sections, because it stays close to the wording and this book repeatedly asks you to notice particular words — now in Romans 8:1, for in Philippians 2:13, yet in Mark 14:36.

New Living Translation (NLT) is worth having open beside it for the devotional passes, and especially for the Psalms, where its plainness sometimes lands the emotion better than the precision does.

The Protestant tradition on suffering

The strand of writing this book leans on hardest, and the one most people do not know exists.

Timothy Rogers, A Discourse Concerning Trouble of Mind and the Disease of Melancholy (1691). Written by a minister who lost roughly two years of his life to it. Much of the book is addressed not to sufferers but to their friends and pastors, telling them to stop. If you have one person in your life who keeps suggesting you pray harder, this is the book to hand them.

Richard Baxter, The Cure of Melancholy and Overmuch Sorrow, by Faith and Physic. The title carries the argument. Baxter was chronically ill for most of his life and insisted on medicine alongside prayer, and told melancholy sufferers to spend less time in solitary self-examination — startling counsel from a Puritan, and the seed of chapter eight.

William Bridge, A Lifting Up for the Downcast (1648). Thirteen sermons, each addressed to a different reason a person might be discouraged. Unusually practical. Read the chapter that fits and ignore the rest; it was built that way.

The tradition on attention and meditation

Joseph Hall, The Art of Divine Meditation (1606). Short, step-by-step, plainly written. The method in chapter seven is largely a compression of this.

Thomas Watson, A Christian on the Mount (1657) and Richard Baxter, The Saints’ Everlasting Rest (1650), Part IV. Baxter’s section is the most detailed — when to do it, how long, what to do when your mind wanders and when it will not start at all.

Brother Lawrence, The Practice of the Presence of God (compiled 1691). A seventeenth-century Carmelite lay brother, so Roman Catholic, and named as such in chapter five rather than smuggled. Doctrinally unloaded and about ninety minutes to read. The whole argument is that he stayed in conversation with God while washing up.

On the heart and its loves

Thomas Chalmers, The Expulsive Power of a New Affection (1819). One sermon, freely available, perhaps forty minutes. If you read only one item on this page, read this one. The argument that a love is only ever displaced by a better love governs chapters two and thirteen.

Jonathan Edwards, Religious Affections (1746). Difficult, long, and worth it. Written to sort genuine transformation from convincing counterfeits by a man who had watched both. The affection/passion distinction in chapter thirteen is from here. Modern abridgements exist and are not a defeat.

Augustine, City of God XV.22 for the ordo amoris, and Confessions generally — the first book anyone wrote about what it is like to be a person from the inside.

Modern

D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones, Spiritual Depression: Its Causes and Cure (1965). A physician turned preacher. Chapter one, on Psalm 42, is the source of the distinction between listening to yourself and talking to yourself. A caution: some later chapters read more sternly than a person in a bad week will find helpful. Take chapter one and come back for the rest when you are stronger.

Edward T. Welch, When People Are Big and God Is Small. The fear of man, at length and practically. Chapter fourteen is a compressed version of what this book does properly.

Creeds and confessions

The Definition of Chalcedon (451) — four negatives and no mechanism, and the model for every restraint in this book. The Westminster Confession, ch. XVIII on assurance — short, and worth reading if you cannot presently feel what you believe. The Heidelberg Catechism, Q1 — four sentences, and the best answer anyone has given to the question chapter sixteen is about.

Literature

John Bunyan, The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678). The Doubting Castle episode is a few pages and can be read on its own.

John Bunyan, Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666). With a warning. This is Bunyan’s own account of years of religious terror, intrusive blasphemous thoughts, and obsessive fear of having committed an unforgivable sin. For some readers it is the most consoling book ever written — proof that a person can go through exactly this and come out. For a reader currently in scrupulosity it can function as fuel. If chapter seven’s warning box described you, read this one with your clinician or not yet.

George Herbert, The Temple (1633). Love (III) is printed in full in chapter sixteen. The whole collection exists because a dying man told his friend to publish it only if it might help one dejected poor soul.

Clinical

Marsha M. Linehan. The skills themselves belong with a trained clinician and are not reproduced anywhere in this book. Her memoir, Building a Life Worth Living (2020), tells the story that opens chapter one and closes chapter sixteen, in her own words rather than mine.

For anything else clinical — what treatment fits you, what a diagnosis means, what to do this week — the right source is not a book. It is the person you are already working with.

Outside the faith

Three writers who described the condition accurately without proposing this book’s answer, and who are worth reading precisely for that reason.

Ernest Becker, The Denial of Death (1973). Difficult and rewarding. The source of the immortality-project material in chapter sixteen.

David Foster Wallace, This Is Water (2005). Twenty minutes, and one of the most honest things said out loud this century about worship. Read chapter sixteen’s note about him before or after; not instead.

Søren Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety (1844) and The Sickness Unto Death (1849). Genuinely hard going. If you want the ideas without the labour, almost any good secondary treatment will give you the dizziness of freedom and the self that rests transparently in the Power that established it, which is all this book takes from him.

What is deliberately not here

No self-help. No books promising that the affliction lifts if you do the thing correctly. Nothing that treats your suffering as primarily a discipline problem.

And no attempt at a complete DBT bibliography, because this book is not a substitute for the treatment and a reading list would imply otherwise. If you want the skills, get them from someone trained to teach them.

Most of the pre-1900 material on this page is out of copyright and freely available online, often in modernized spelling. You do not need to buy anything to read the seventeenth century.

This book is not treatment. It is written to be read alongside care from a qualified professional, not instead of it. If you are in immediate danger, contact your clinician or, in the US, call or text 988.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) was developed by Marsha M. Linehan. This book discusses and responds to DBT’s aims and assumptions in its own words; it does not reproduce DBT’s skills lists, worksheets, or training materials, which remain the property of their authors and publishers.
