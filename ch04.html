Part I · Foundations · Chapter Four

Both/And Without Losing the Floor

Why the Christian faith can hold more contradictions than any therapy — and why that is a feature.

Here is the sentence at the centre of DBT, and the first time you hear it, it sounds like someone trying to have it both ways.

You are doing the best you can — and you need to do better.

The mind reaches for the escape hatch immediately. Surely one of those is the real one and the other is politeness. Either I am doing my best, in which case demanding more is unfair, or more is genuinely required, in which case I have not been doing my best and the first half was a lie told to soften me up.

Except that anyone who has actually lived inside a hard season knows both halves are true, simultaneously, at full strength, and that hearing only one of them is unbearable in a different way each time. Hear only the first and you are patted on the head and left where you are. Hear only the second and you are crushed. It is the and that makes it survivable.

So the technique is sound. The question this chapter is about is what has to be true of the universe for that and to hold — and whether the philosophy DBT inherited can actually pay for it.

What dialectics is, and what it is not

Terms
Dialectic
Broadly: a way of thinking that works by holding opposing positions together and looking for what emerges, rather than picking a winner. In DBT specifically, it is mostly a practical stance — refusing false either/ors, especially the all-or-nothing ones that dominate severe emotional suffering.
Thesis, antithesis, synthesis
The classical shape: a position, its opposite, and a third thing that resolves them — which then becomes a new position with a new opposite, and so on. The engine never stops.

A common Christian objection runs like this: dialectics teaches that truth is relative, produced by balancing viewpoints rather than revealed, and a Christian cannot hold that alongside a Lord who calls himself the truth.

I want to take that objection seriously and then correct it, because it is aiming at something real but has the wrong target in its sights.

The wrong target first. In the clinic, dialectical is not a theory of truth at all. It is a rescue operation for people whose thinking has collapsed into two settings. The characteristic agony of severe emotional dysregulation is not too much nuance; it is total absence of it — a person is all good or all worthless, a relationship is perfect or over, one bad night means the whole project is finished. Teaching that person to hold two things at once is not moral relativism. It is closer to teaching them to see in more than one colour.

And the specific instruction to describe before you evaluate — the thing usually reported as “non-judgmental” — is not a ban on moral judgment. It is a sequencing rule: get the facts down before you sentence anybody. The book of Proverbs says that answering before you have listened is folly and shame. James says be quick to hear, slow to speak. That is the same rule, older.

Now the real target. Underneath the clinical practice sits an inherited picture of reality — drawn loosely from a nineteenth-century philosophical tradition — in which everything is process, everything is interrelated, and truth is not so much discovered as arrived at, provisionally, on the way to the next synthesis. That is a claim about the universe, and a Christian cannot hold it as stated. So the concern is legitimate. It has just been aimed at the wrong floor of the building.

The problem with a dialectic is not that it holds two things at once. It is that a synthesis with nothing underneath it never lands. It just keeps becoming the next thesis, forever, and a frightened person cannot rest on an escalator.

The turn

Here is what I did not expect when I started working on this, and it reversed the whole argument for me.

Christianity is not less dialectical than DBT. It is far more. It holds tensions that would tear any therapy apart, and it holds them at maximum strength, without softening either side, and it does this for centuries without resolving them — because it can afford to.

Look at what it asks a believer to hold simultaneously.

You are simultaneously righteous and a sinner. Not partly one and partly the other. Not moving from one to the other on a sliding scale. Fully both, at once, right now. The Reformers had a Latin tag for it because it needed a permanent name: simul iustus et peccator. Keller used to compress it into a single line — we are more sinful than we dared believe, more loved than we dared hope. Both clauses at full volume. Neither one is there to soften the other.

The kingdom has arrived and has not arrived. Already and not yet. It is genuinely here, and you are genuinely still waiting, and anyone who collapses that into one side produces either triumphalism or despair.

You must work, and you cannot generate the power. We did this one in chapter two. Paul says work out your salvation with fear and trembling because God is at work in you. He joins them with the word for, which is the most theologically loaded conjunction in the New Testament.

Carry each other’s burdens — and each will carry his own load. Paul says both, in the same chapter, three verses apart, without noticing any difficulty. Every argument about codependency and boundaries you have ever had was about those two verses.

Warrant · Direct

Galatians 6:2, 6:5 — “Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ… For each will have to bear his own load.” (ESV)

Both directly stated, in the same passage, by the same author, with no attempt at reconciliation. Paul does not appear to think he has a problem. Chapter 15 works out what to do with this.

You are entirely forgiven and must go on confessing. John says that claiming to have no sin is self-deception, and in the next breath that God is faithful to forgive. Two verses. Full weight on each.

Warrant · Contested

Romans 7:15–25 — the man who does the thing he hates.

Flagged honestly because this is one of the genuinely unsettled passages in the New Testament. Whether Paul is describing his life as a Christian, his life before Christ, or Israel under the law has been argued by serious people in every direction for centuries. This book does not need to settle it: the simul stands on Galatians 5:17 and on 1 John 1:8–9 regardless. But if you have been handed Romans 7 as though it were obvious, it is not, and you should know that.

Why we can afford it

Every one of those tensions would be intolerable if the two sides were resting on each other. They are not. They are both resting on something that does not move.

And the something is not a proposition. This is the part I think is easy to miss.

The deepest both/and in the Christian faith is not a doctrine at all. It is a person — one man who is fully God and fully human, the two natures neither blurred into a hybrid nor split into a partnership. The church spent four centuries and several ugly councils arriving at the formula, and what it finally produced in 451 was not an explanation. It was a fence: here is the field, do not go over these edges, the mystery lives inside.

Which means the Christian habit of holding two things at once is not a coping mechanism we developed because reality is confusing. It is a habit we learned from the shape of the one we worship.

Warrant · Historical · Creedal

The Definition of Chalcedon (451) — one person, two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.

Creedal warrant, which in this book carries more weight than ordinary historical warrant and less than Scripture. Notice the form: four negatives and no positive mechanism. The church declined to explain how and confined itself to saying where the errors were. That restraint is the model for everything in this chapter.

Christianity does not hold fewer contradictions than DBT. It holds more — and holds them steadier, because it is not balancing them against each other. It has hung them both on the same nail.

The test you can actually use

Which raises the fair objection: if we are allowed this much paradox, what stops “both/and” from becoming a licence to believe anything and dodge every hard question?

Something does stop it, and it is simple enough to run in about ten seconds.

A genuine paradox raises the stakes on both sides. A compromise lowers them on both.

Run it. Simul iustus et peccator takes your sin more seriously than you do — and takes your acceptance more seriously than you do. Both intensified. Nothing discounted. That is a paradox.

Now run it on a fudge. Well, God is loving, but he is also just, so probably things land somewhere in the middle. Notice what happened: the love got smaller and the justice got smaller, and everyone went home relieved. That is not a both/and. That is an average, and an average is what you get when you have decided in advance that the tension is unbearable.

One limit on the test, and it is not optional. This test sorts claims that are already warranted; it does not warrant anything. A both/and is admissible only where Scripture actually asserts both halves — and the reason all the examples above work is that each one is two things the Bible says, not two things that felt true together. Run the test on a pair Scripture does not assert and you will get a confident result and a false one. Paradox is a way of holding what has been given to you. It is not a licence to manufacture.

The test works on your own life too, which is where it earns its keep. I am genuinely loved and what I did on Thursday was genuinely wrong — both intensified, nothing softened, and the second one is safe to look at precisely because the first one is not in question. Compare the fudge: it wasn’t that bad and I suppose God understands. Both discounted. Nothing faced. Nothing healed.

A real both/and makes both sides heavier. A fudge makes both sides lighter and calls it peace. If the tension went away and nothing cost you anything, you did not resolve it. You just turned the volume down.

Doing it on purpose, in public

Let me demonstrate the discipline on live ammunition, since a chapter about holding tensions ought to be willing to hold one.

Christians have argued for centuries about how God’s sovereignty relates to human responsibility — whether and how a person’s genuine choices fit together with a God who genuinely rules everything. It is a real disagreement between sincere believers, and this book is not going to adjudicate it.

What is worth doing is naming what each side is protecting, because in most long-running Christian disagreements both parties are guarding something Scripture actually says.

  • Those who press hardest on sovereignty are protecting this: that salvation is God’s work from beginning to end, that grace does not finally depend on the strength of your grip, and that no one has grounds to boast.
  • Those who press hardest on responsibility are protecting this: that God is not the author of evil, that your choices are truly yours and truly matter, and that the invitation is sincerely offered to everyone who hears it.

Every item in both lists is asserted somewhere in Scripture. What Scripture does not do — and this is the thing worth noticing — is supply the mechanism that harmonizes them. It asserts both and moves on, exactly the way Paul asserts both halves of Galatians 6 and does not appear to feel the difficulty.

Which is not a dodge. It is a rule: hold what Scripture asserts as firmly as Scripture asserts it, and hold what Scripture leaves open as loosely as Scripture leaves it. Most of the damage done in Christian argument comes from getting those two levels the wrong way round — gripping a mechanism like a creed, or holding a creed like an opinion.

What this does on a bad Tuesday

All of which sounds abstract until it is about eleven at night and you have just done the thing you promised yourself you would not do again.

The all-or-nothing mind has two moves available. Minimize it — it was not that bad, everyone does it, I was tired. Or absolutize it — this proves what I have always suspected, the whole project is a fraud, there is no point continuing. And notice that both moves are attempts to escape a tension that has become physically uncomfortable to hold.

The third option is to hold both at full strength, and it is only available if something underneath is holding you.

That was genuinely wrong. It mattered. It hurt someone, or it hurt me, and I am not going to file it down. And I am not one inch less loved at eleven-fifteen than I was at ten.

You cannot say the second sentence to yourself as a technique; it is not a mantra and it will not work as one. You can only say it because it is true, and it is true because somebody settled it, and it was settled a very long time before Tuesday.

Case · the switch

“D.” is twenty-six and describes his weeks in binary. A week is either “on track” or “a write-off,” and the transition between them takes about ninety seconds and is usually triggered by something small — a skipped run, a snapped remark at his brother.

His therapist has been working on the all-or-nothing pattern for months with real progress in every area but one. When D. fails morally — when he is unkind, or dishonest — the switch throws instantly and no amount of nuance helps, because in that domain he has understood nuance to be the same thing as excusing himself. Being fair to himself feels like lying.

What D. has never been offered is a third position: that the wrongness can be named at full size without the verdict on him changing. He has only ever been given the choice between condemning himself and letting himself off, and he is too honest to take the second one.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

Two things here may be practically useful regardless of what you make of the theology.

First, a religiously serious client will sometimes resist dialectical work specifically in the moral domain, because holding two perspectives on their own wrongdoing feels like self-justification. That resistance is conscientious rather than oppositional, and it does not usually yield to the argument that they are being too hard on themselves — which they will hear as a request to lower the standard.

Second, the framework in this chapter offers a route through that is not available in purely clinical terms: the wrongness stays at full size while the client’s standing stays fixed, because the standing was never contingent on the behaviour. It is a way of getting the second perspective without discounting the first, which is precisely what a scrupulous client refuses to do.

A phrase that translates: “We are not going to make it smaller. We are going to put it somewhere it can be held.”

Practice

Journaling — running the test.

  1. Write down a tension you are currently living inside as an either/or. The sentence usually starts “either I’m … or I’m …” Get the actual wording down.
  2. Try writing it as an and instead, with both halves at full strength and nothing shaved off either one.
  3. Run the test on what you wrote. Did both sides get heavier, or did both get lighter? If lighter, you wrote a fudge. Go back and put the weight back on.
  4. Ask the honest question underneath: what would have to be true for me to hold both of these without flinching? That answer is the floor you are actually standing on, and it is worth knowing what it is.

Try this out loud for one week. After any failure, say two sentences in this order, and do not let yourself stop after the first: “That was genuinely wrong.” and “I am not loved any less than I was an hour ago.” Most of us can say one of those easily and find the other physically difficult. Which one is hard for you is worth knowing.

To bring to your counselor. “When it comes to moral failure, holding two views feels like lying to myself. Can we work on that specifically?”
