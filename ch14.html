Part IV · Loving · Chapter Fourteen

Truth in Love

On saying the thing — and on why knowing how has never been the problem.

You have rehearsed it in the car. You have the sentence. It is a good sentence, fair and clear and not unkind, and you have said it perfectly seven times to the windscreen.

And then you go in and you do not say it.

Or you say a smaller version of it, wrapped in so much apology that the other person reasonably concludes it was not important. Or you say nothing for four more months and then say all of it at once, badly, at the wrong moment, and spend a week repairing the damage.

This chapter is about the gap between the car and the room.

The skills are good and you should learn them

DBT’s interpersonal material is, in my view, the most immediately usable thing in the whole treatment. How to make a request. How to refuse one. How to do either while keeping the relationship intact and without leaving your own self-respect on the floor.

And it is worth saying plainly: a great many adults were simply never taught this. Not because they are damaged — because nobody sat them down. If you grew up in a house where wants were not stated and refusals were not permitted, you did not fail to learn these skills. They were never offered.

Scripting a request is not manipulation. It is preparation, and preparation is a courtesy to the other person as much as to you. A person who has thought about what they actually want is easier to say yes or no to than a person who has not.

Two things at once, again

Paul gives the whole of Christian speech in four words. Speaking the truth in love.

And by now you will recognize the shape, because chapter four was about it: two things held at full strength, neither one softening the other.

Drop either and you get a recognizable and very common failure.

Truth without love is the person who tells you exactly what they think and calls it integrity. They are frequently accurate. They are also doing damage, and the accuracy is what makes the damage stick.

Love without truth is subtler and I think more common among Christians, because it can be mistaken for a virtue almost indefinitely. It is the person who will not say the hard thing, and who calls the not-saying kindness, and who has actually made a private calculation: saying it would cost me their good opinion, and I would rather they be uncorrected than that I be disliked. That is not gentleness. It is self-protection with good manners.

Truth without love is assault. Love without truth is cowardice wearing kindness as a costume. Paul puts both in the same phrase and does not offer a discount on either.

Warrant · Direct

Ephesians 4:15 — “speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ.” (ESV)

Directly stated. Note the purpose clause: the point of the speech is growth — the other person’s and the body’s. That is the test the phrase is usually used to dodge. “I’m just being honest” is almost never said by someone whose honesty was aimed at anybody’s growth.

The thing the technique cannot fix

Now the actual reason you did not say it in the room.

Every communication skill ever taught rests on one unstated assumption: that you can tolerate the other person being displeased with you. The whole apparatus — the scripts, the phrasing, the timing — assumes you are able to survive their disapproval long enough to finish your sentence.

And for a great many of us that assumption is simply false. Not because we lack technique. Because their approval is doing structural work in our lives — holding up something load-bearing — and we cannot risk it any more than you can casually remove a wall.

Proverbs names this with a precision I find slightly alarming. The fear of man lays a snare. A snare, not a weakness — a trap that closes, that you walk into, that you do not notice until you are in it and cannot move.

Warrant · Direct

Proverbs 29:25 — “The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the Lord is safe.” (ESV); Galatians 1:10 — “If I were still trying to please man, I would not be a servant of Christ.”

Both directly stated, and note that Proverbs supplies a contrast rather than a rebuke: the escape from the snare is not more courage but a different place of safety.

Every communication technique assumes you can survive being disliked long enough to finish your sentence. For some of us that is the entire problem, and no amount of better phrasing touches it.

Where the freedom comes from

So the release is not a better script. It is a settled verdict — which is chapter two arriving in the middle of a difficult conversation.

If your standing is already decided, and decided by someone whose opinion outranks everyone in the room, then another person’s displeasure is genuinely painful and genuinely survivable. It costs something. It does not cost you everything, because it cannot reach the thing that was holding you up.

That is what makes honest speech possible: not nerve, and not a technique, but the fact that the worst outcome of the conversation is no longer fatal.

You can afford to be disliked only if somebody has already settled what you are worth. Courage in a conversation is almost always downstream of a verdict.

How he did it

It is worth noticing how Jesus actually talked, because it does not resemble either of our two failure modes.

He was startlingly direct — direct in a way that would make most of us wince to copy — and there is not one occasion where the directness is cruel. He asked a great many questions, and often answered a question with one, which is a way of taking somebody seriously rather than dispatching them. He declined to answer some questions at all.

And he said no. He said no to people who wanted genuinely good things from him. In the first chapter of Mark he heals through an evening in Capernaum, gets up before dawn to pray alone, and is tracked down by Simon with what sounds like a reproach: everyone is looking for you. There are still sick people in that town. And he says: let us go somewhere else, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out.

He left. With need still outstanding, and without an apology, because he knew what he was for and the town’s expectations were not it.

Warrant · Entailed

Mark 1:32–38 — Jesus withdraws to pray, is pursued, and moves on to other towns with need remaining in Capernaum.

The narrative is directly stated. Drawing from it a general permission to decline good requests is entailed rather than commanded — and it should be held with some care, since he was declining on the basis of a mission he alone had. The transferable part is narrower: that leaving a good thing undone is not automatically a failure of love.

Yes and no

Jesus also said something about speech that sounds like manners and is not. Let what you say be simply yes or no.

The context is oath-taking — the practice of reinforcing your word so people know which of your statements to trust. His objection is that a person who needs an oath has already admitted their ordinary speech is unreliable. The alternative is a life in which your plain word is enough because it has never needed to be anything else.

Which cuts, for most of us, at exactly one place: the yes that meant maybe. The agreement given in the room because refusing was unbearable, later quietly not honoured, or honoured with resentment, or honoured at a cost the other person never knew they were imposing. Almost nobody reading this has a problem with lying. Most of us have a problem with a yes that was not a yes.

Let your yes be yes and your no be no. Most of the damage in our relationships is not done by a lie. It is done by a yes that meant maybe.

Is this just assertiveness with verses?

A fair objection, and worth answering, because Scripture praises meekness and Christians rightly get nervous when a therapy tells them to advocate for themselves.

But look at what meekness actually is in the text. Moses is called the meekest man on earth — and this is the man who walked into Pharaoh’s court and made demands, who argued with God to his face, who broke the tablets in fury. Meekness in Scripture is not the absence of force. It is force under control, held by someone who does not need to use it to establish that they exist.

Which is the opposite of what most of us are doing when we go quiet. Our silence is usually not meekness. It is fear wearing meekness as a disguise, and the tell is what happens afterwards: real meekness leaves you at peace, and fear leaves you rehearsing the conversation in the shower for three days.

Case · the perfect script

“L.” is twenty-nine and has done the interpersonal module twice. She can construct a request beautifully — her therapist has said so.

She has not used one with her mother in eleven months. She writes them out. She has a note on her phone with four of them. When she is asked what stops her, she says she is waiting for a good moment, and there has not been one, and when pressed further she says something that is closer to it: that her mother would be hurt, and then cool with her for a fortnight, and she cannot do a fortnight.

L. does not need a fifth script. She needs somewhere to stand during the fortnight.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The pattern this chapter targets. Clients who acquire interpersonal skills competently and do not deploy them. The usual reading is avoidance or insufficient practice; often the more accurate reading is that the client’s self-worth is substantially contingent on the specific person’s approval, so the skill is intact and the prerequisite is missing.

What is offered instead of more rehearsal. A non-contingent basis for standing — the same structure as chapter two — on the argument that disapproval becomes survivable when it can no longer reach what is holding the person up. Whatever you make of the source, the mechanism is the relevant one: this is contingent-self-worth work entering through a door the client already trusts.

Useful reframe for religious clients who resist assertiveness as incompatible with humility: the chapter distinguishes biblical meekness (force under control) from silence driven by fear, and gives a behavioural tell — whether the client is still rehearsing the conversation days later. Clients tend to find that test hard to argue with.

Watch for the opposite misuse: speaking the truth in love deployed as licence for unsolicited criticism. The chapter applies the purpose clause as the test — was this aimed at the other person’s growth — which is usable in session.

Practice

Find the wall. Take one conversation you have been avoiding and answer in writing: what exactly am I afraid this person will do? Then: and then what? Then and then what again, three or four times, until you reach the bottom. The bottom is usually not a consequence. It is a sentence about what their reaction would mean about you.

Then answer it. Whatever that sentence turned out to be — put the verdict next to it. Not a reassurance about the conversation. A statement about who has already decided what you are worth.

The yes audit. List every yes you gave in the last month. Mark the ones that were actually maybe. Look at what the maybes have in common; it is usually one or two people, or one particular kind of ask.

One honest no, this week. Small. Low stakes. Say it once, without a reason, without an apology, and without softening it into a maybe. Write down afterwards what actually happened, which is almost always less than what you predicted.

To bring to your counselor. “I have the skills and I don’t use them with one particular person. Can we work on what their approval is holding up?”
