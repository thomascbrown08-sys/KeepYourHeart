Part IV · Loving · Chapter Fourteen

Anger

On a reading that arrives at the wrong size in both directions.

Two people are going to read this chapter and they have opposite problems.

One of them says things. Not often — every few months — and then spends a fortnight repairing something that took eleven seconds. Between the eleven seconds and the fortnight there is a version of themselves they do not recognize and cannot account for.

The other has never once said the thing. Their anger does not arrive. Or it arrives so late, and so far past the event, that having it feels like further evidence that something is wrong with them, so they put it down and go and be pleasant.

Those look like opposite failures. They are the same one: an accurate signal that has never been allowed to do its job, arriving instead at the wrong size in whichever direction the person's history pushed it.

Anger too large and anger that never arrives are not opposite problems. They are the same signal, failing the same way, in the direction your history pushed it.

In My Toolbox

The clinical companion to this chapter:

  • Emotional flashbacks — why an emotion can arrive at ten times the size of the event in front of you, or from an old address.
  • Somatic work — the body half of it, which matters more with anger than with almost anything else.

Anger is a claim

Chapter thirteen established that an emotion is a reading rather than noise, and that the question worth asking is what it is a reading of. It said in passing that anger fires when something you believe you are owed has been withheld. That is the whole starting point here, so let me open it up.

Anger is not simply displeasure. Displeasure is what you feel about weather. Anger arrives with a proposition attached: this should not have happened, and someone is responsible. It is a claim, presented as a feeling — which means it can be examined the way you would examine any other claim, and it can be true or false.

So the first move is not to decide whether you are allowed to be angry. It is to read the bill. What do I believe I am owed here? By whom? And was it ever actually promised?

Sometimes the answer comes back clean: I was owed honesty, and I was lied to. Something real was taken. In that case the anger is accurate, and the rest of this chapter is about what to do with an accurate reading rather than how to talk yourself out of it.

And sometimes the answer is that you were expecting something nobody ever agreed to — that the debt was assumed rather than incurred. That is worth knowing too, and it is not a moral failing to discover it. Most of us are carrying a few.

Scripture is not embarrassed about this

If you were raised being told that anger is sin, the texts will surprise you.

God is angry. Not occasionally, and not as a lapse. It is one of the things Scripture says about him most often, and when he describes his own character to Moses the phrase is slow to anger — which is a statement about pace, not about capacity. A God incapable of anger would be a God indifferent to what happens to people.

Jesus is angry, and Mark records it plainly. Facing men who would rather a disabled man stayed disabled than see the Sabbath rules bent, he looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart.

Two things in that verse worth having. The anger and the grief are in the same clause — they are not alternatives. And the action it produces is a healing. The anger does not discharge onto the men. It goes into helping the person they were prepared to leave broken.

Warrant · Direct

Mark 3:5 — “And he looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart…” (ESV); with Exodus 34:6 — “slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.”

Both directly stated. Slow to anger describes the pace at which it arrives, not an absence of the capacity — and the same self-description continues into judgment two verses later.

The temple, handled honestly

The tables get cited as a licence, so it is worth reading what actually happened.

Mark tells it in two parts. Jesus enters the temple, looks around at everything, and then — because it is late — goes back out to Bethany with the twelve. He comes back the next day and clears the place. John's account has him making a whip out of cords first, which is not something that happens in a flash of temper; it takes time and both hands.

So whatever that was, it was not a loss of control. He saw it, he slept on it, he made a tool, and he came back. The most-cited example of righteous anger in the Bible is premeditated, and the people who quote it are almost always defending something that took four seconds.

He went in, looked at everything, and went back out because it was late. He came back the next day and did it. Whatever that was, it was not losing his temper.

The command, and the clock

Paul gives an instruction that is grammatically strange and pastorally exact.

Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil.

Be angry is an imperative. Whatever else the verse is doing, it is not treating anger as inherently disobedient — you cannot command someone to commit a sin.

And do not sin makes the distinction: there is a way to be angry that is not sinning, and a way that is.

Do not let the sun go down on it is a clock, and I do not think it means every conflict must be resolved by bedtime — some cannot be, and pretending otherwise produces the false closure that leaves things worse. It means: do not let it move in. Anger is a visitor with business. The problem starts when it stops being a response to an event and becomes a resident condition.

Which is what Hebrews calls a root of bitterness — the agricultural image is precise. A root is what something grows when it has been in the ground long enough to have a system.

Warrant · Direct

Ephesians 4:26–27 — “Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and the imperative form is doing real work. Paul is quoting Psalm 4:4, where the second line is ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent — anger taken to bed and examined rather than acted on immediately.

The sentence that changes the argument

And then James, who says the thing that makes all of this usable.

Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God. James 1:19–20

Read what that does not say. It does not say your anger is unjustified. It does not adjudicate your case at all. It says that human anger does not produce what it is aiming at.

That is a claim about efficacy rather than permission, and it is the only argument in this area that survives you being completely right. You can win the question of whether you were wronged — you may well win it — and the machinery still does not deliver. Anger is superb at establishing that something is wrong and almost useless at putting it right.

It never says your anger is unjustified. It says it does not produce what it is aiming at. That is a claim about efficacy, and it survives you being entirely right.

Warrant · Direct

James 1:19–20 — “the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God.” (ESV)

Directly stated. Note the qualifier of man — the verse distinguishes human anger from God's rather than condemning anger as such, and its claim is about what the former yields.

Why you cannot check this from inside

Now the hard part, and it is the reason this chapter has to offer external tests.

Righteous anger and self-interested anger feel identical. There is no phenomenological difference. Both arrive with total moral clarity, both produce a sense of standing on solid ground, and both recruit your best qualities — your seriousness about fairness, your care for the person who was hurt, your attention to what people are owed.

Worse, the certainty is manufactured by the anger itself. The angrier you get, the more obviously right you feel, and the feeling of rightness is produced by the very thing you would be using it to evaluate. That is a closed loop, and no amount of sincerity opens it.

There is no internal difference between anger at injustice and anger at being thwarted. Both feel exactly like justice — and the certainty is manufactured by the anger it is supposed to be assessing.

Five tests you can run from outside

1. Would you take the fix without the acknowledgment? If the wrong were fully put right, but you never got them to admit it — would that satisfy you? If not, the anger was at least partly about the admission, which is a different claim than the one you have been making.

2. Restoration or diminishment? Not whether you want consequences — wanting someone held to account is legitimate and God does it constantly. The question is what the fantasy is. When you play it out in the shower, does it end with the wrong righted, or with them smaller? God's anger in Scripture characteristically aims at getting someone back. Grievance aims at reduction.

3. Action or rehearsal? Same test chapter twenty-four runs on guilt. Does this produce something you can do — a conversation, a report, a boundary, a change — or does it produce another lap? Anger that has generated nothing in six months is no longer doing the job it arrived for.

4. Is the size about the event? Chapter thirteen's distinction, applied here. Magnitude is frequently about your body and your week rather than about the thing in front of you. Sleep, food, illness and the last month all belong in this question before any conclusion is drawn about the person who annoyed you.

5. Has it become resident? Is this a response to something, or is it the temperature you live at now? The second one has a root system and needs different work.

If yours never arrives

For a great many readers of this book the problem is the other direction, and it needs saying plainly: anger that is never allowed does not go anywhere.

It changes address. It becomes flatness, or contempt — which is anger that has given up on the person and cooled — or a bill presented years later at ten times the size, which then confirms the original fear that having it was dangerous. Chapter seven's principle applies exactly: you cannot be rid of something by trying not to have it, and the monitoring keeps it in the room.

If you were taught that a good person does not get angry, notice that you were taught something Scripture does not say, by people who may have benefited from your not having it. Chapter twenty-one covers what to do when that was the arrangement.

Anger that is never allowed does not leave. It changes address — into flatness, or contempt, or a bill presented years later at ten times the size.

Three guards

This is not permission to vent. Discharging anger at a person is not the same as being honest with them, and it reliably produces something you will spend chapter sixteen repairing. The next chapter is about how to actually say it.

Do not turn every correction into an occasion. Chapter twenty-one makes this point about care and control and it applies here with equal force: if being corrected reliably produces anger, you have made yourself unteachable, and the tell is the same — did it aim at your capability or your compliance?

And where the anger is accurate, the answer is not to stop. If you were genuinely wronged, chapter ten gives you somewhere to put it that is neither suppression nor revenge — the psalms hand rage over at full volume rather than performing calm. Chapter twenty-five adds the rest: vengeance is not denied, it is reassigned. Those two together are what to do with an anger that is right and cannot be acted on.

Case · the fortnight

“T.” is even-tempered by reputation and would describe himself that way. Roughly three times a year he says something to his wife that he cannot account for afterwards, and then spends two weeks being unusually kind.

Asked to describe what happens beforehand, he cannot — there is nothing beforehand, which is what makes it frightening. Asked instead to list what has irritated him in the preceding fortnight, he produced eleven items in four minutes and was visibly startled by the list, because he had registered none of them as anger at the time. He had registered them as nothing.

T. does not have an anger problem in the sense he thinks. He has eleven unread signals and one outlet, and the outlet is not the malfunction. It is the only place anything was ever allowed out.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

Why religious clients often present badly on this. Many have been taught anger is inherently sinful, which forecloses the ordinary work of identifying and titrating it. The result is usually not an absence of anger but poor interoceptive access to it, followed by episodic discharge that confirms the original teaching. The client then arrives asking for help with the discharge and is unable to work on anything upstream of it.

What this chapter supplies. Scriptural warrant for anger as legitimate (imperative form in Ephesians 4:26; divine and Christ's own anger), which removes the prohibition without licensing discharge — and then relocates the argument from permission to efficacy via James 1:20. That reframe is usable because it does not require adjudicating whether the client's grievance is valid, which is generally an unwinnable and unhelpful conversation.

Directly usable: the five tests, particularly the first (would you accept the fix without the acknowledgment?), which tends to separate injury from grievance quickly and without confrontation.

Watch for a client with an over-controlled presentation reading this chapter as permission and swinging. The material explicitly distinguishes allowing an emotion from discharging it at a person, and hands the expression question to the following chapter.

Practice

Keep an irritation list for one week. Not incidents that made you angry — everything that registered at all, however small, however unreasonable it seemed at the time. Do not evaluate the entries. Most people who believe they do not get angry produce a startling list, and the list is the point.

Read one bill. Take a single entry and answer three questions in writing: what do I believe I was owed here? by whom? was it ever actually promised? Some come back clean. Some do not. Both answers are useful.

Run test one on your largest current grievance. If the wrong were fully put right but you never got the acknowledgment, would that be enough? Answer honestly rather than correctly.

Journaling.

  1. What were you taught about anger, and by whom? Then the harder question: did any of them benefit from your not having it?
  2. When you play the confrontation out in your head, how does it end? Write the actual ending. That is test two, and the honest answer is usually not the one you would defend.
  3. What has your anger produced in the last six months? Name something concrete, or note that the answer is nothing — which is information about the machinery rather than about your case.

To bring to your counselor. “I don’t think I register anger until it’s already large. Can we work on catching it earlier rather than on managing the end of it?”
