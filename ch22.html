Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty-Two

Church Hurt

On harm done in God’s name, and why it is not like other harm.

When something goes wrong at work, you take it to church.

When a friend fails you, when a marriage strains, when somebody dies — church. That is what it is there for, and for most of a life it works exactly as advertised.

Now consider what happens when the harm comes from inside that building.

You cannot take it there, because that is where it came from. You cannot easily take it anywhere else, because the vocabulary you would need is the church’s vocabulary and the people fluent in it are all standing in the room. And when you try to take it to God, you find that the faces of the people who did it have got onto him — because they were speaking in his name at the time, and as far as you could tell they had the authority to.

Three exits, and the same building is standing in all of them. That is what makes this different from every other injury in this book.

Every other wound in your life, you could take to church. This one closed the door, took the vocabulary, and put its own face on God.

In My Toolbox

The clinical vocabulary for these patterns:

  • Institutional betrayal — harm by a body you depended on for protection.
  • Coercive control — the mechanism where it is systematic rather than incidental.
  • DARVO — what frequently happens when you raise it.
  • Testimonial injustice — the specific harm of being disbelieved because of who you are taken to be.

Whose side he is on

Start here, because it is the deepest part and everything else is downstream of it.

Your picture of God has been contaminated, and that is not a failure of reasoning on your part. It is a reasonable inference from the evidence you were given. Somebody told you God was disappointed in you, and they said it standing in his house, holding his book, wearing his authority. You had no way to audit the claim. Of course it stuck.

So the disentangling is not stop being irrational. It is what you would do with any report you had reason to doubt: check it against the source.

And the source is not neutral about this. Go and read what he actually says about people who use his name to harm the people in their care.

Ezekiel 34 — which chapter twenty-one worked through — is God prosecuting shepherds, in the first person, at length, for eating the fat and clothing themselves with the wool while the sick went unhealed. The weak not strengthened. The strayed not brought back. And then: with force and harshness you have ruled them.

Then look at where Jesus put his hardest recorded speech. Not at Rome. Not at the obviously sinful, whom he ate with. At the religious leadership — whitewashed tombs, heavy burdens laid on other people’s shoulders that the layers would not lift with a finger, and the charge of shutting the kingdom of heaven in people’s faces.

And then the sentence nobody quotes in this connection, which is about exactly this: whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in him to stumble — it would be better for that person to have a millstone hung round his neck and be drowned in the depth of the sea.

That is not mild. It is not balanced. It is the most violent image Jesus ever used and he aimed it at people who damage the faith of believers.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 23:4, 13, 27 — heavy burdens laid on others; shutting the kingdom in people’s faces; whitewashed tombs. Matthew 18:6 — the millstone, for causing a believer to stumble. With Ezekiel 34:2–6.

All directly stated. Matthew 18:6 is spoken of “little ones who believe in me” — children in the immediate scene and, on the standard reading, believers generally. The application here is to those who damage a believer’s faith, which is what the verse describes.

The harshest thing Jesus ever said was not aimed at Rome or at obvious sinners. It was aimed at religious leaders who laid weight on people. God is not neutral about what happened to you. He is angrier about it than you are.

How high the bar is

Now the brake, and it has to come early or the rest of the chapter becomes a weapon.

Churches are made of sinners, which means a church will disappoint you the way a family does — regularly, unevenly, and usually without malice. Somebody will forget your name for the fourth time. A group will close ranks in a way that stings. A sermon will land wrong. **None of that is what this chapter is about**, and reading it as though it were will cost you a church you should have kept.

What distinguishes harm from friction is not the temperature. It is duration and direction.

A congregation that is bad at welcoming people is not harming you; it is bad at something. A congregation that has been consistently cold to you specifically for three years while warm to everyone else is not bad at welcoming. It is communicating, and you have read it correctly.

Note that this means coldness counts. It would be convenient to rule it out — to say real church hurt requires an act — but the charge sheet in Ezekiel 34 is almost entirely omissions. Nothing done. The weak not strengthened, the injured not bound up, the lost not sought. Sustained neglect aimed at a particular person is the main category in that text, not a lesser one.

The difference between a cold room and a locked door: you can warm a cold room by staying in it. A locked door does not care how long you stand there.

The ditch on the other side

And the honest acknowledgment, because it exists and pretending otherwise would cost this chapter its credibility with the reader who most needs to finish it.

There are people who lose an argument with leadership and reclassify it as abuse. Sometimes sincerely, because being corrected feels like being crushed when you are already fragile. Sometimes tactically, because the word is powerful and it wins. The pattern is real, it is one of the reasons genuine victims are disbelieved, and every person reading this has probably met an instance.

So the chapter needs a discriminator that does not depend on how it felt — and the book already has one. Chapter twenty-one: correction aims at your capability, control aims at your compliance.

A leader who told you something true that you did not want to hear is not an abuser, however badly it landed. The test is not whether it hurt. It is whether you came out of it more able or more managed.

A referee and a rigged match are not distinguished by whether you lost.

Two tests, because there are two questions

Here is where most people get stuck, and it is because they are trying to answer one question with an instrument built for a different one.

The Reformation built a test for whether an institution is a church at all. Three marks: the Word rightly preached, the sacraments rightly administered, and discipline exercised. And the crucial thing about that test is how low it is set. It was designed to be minimal, so that a church could be substantially bad and still be a church, because the Reformers had seen what happens when people start unchurching each other for cause.

Warrant · Historical · Confessional

Belgic Confession, Article 29 — the marks by which the true church is known: the pure preaching of the gospel, the pure administration of the sacraments, and the exercise of church discipline.

Confessional warrant. Not Scripture, and deliberately minimal by design. It answers whether a body is a church. It was never built to answer whether you belong to one.

Which is the limitation nobody mentions. A congregation can have all three marks in excellent order and still be a place where not one person knows your name. The marks measure an institution’s fidelity. They say nothing whatever about your belonging.

The second test is one verse earlier than the one everybody quotes at people who stop attending. Hebrews 10:25 gets used as a rebuke — not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some. Read the sentence it is attached to:

Let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together… but encouraging one another. Hebrews 10:24–25

The meeting is for the one-anothering. That is the stated purpose, in the clause immediately before the command. Cut the verse at the comma and it becomes an attendance rule. Read it whole and it is about a body of people doing something to each other.

Warrant · Direct

Hebrews 10:24–25 — “let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together… but encouraging one another.” (ESV)

Directly stated, purpose clause included. The meeting is instrumental to the stirring up and the encouraging, not an end in itself.

The marks tell you whether an institution is a church. The one-anothers tell you whether you are in one. Two tests, because they answer two questions, and most people are trying to use the first to settle the second.

Two ditches, and neither of them is nothing

Which produces two failures that look nothing alike and are equally short of the thing.

The first ditch: real fellowship with ordinary believers — two or three people who genuinely know you — and no marks. No preaching ministry, no sacraments, nobody with any standing to correct anyone. The one-anothers without the structure.

The second ditch: a sound congregation where nothing is available to you. Good preaching, proper sacraments, functioning discipline, and eleven years of Sundays in which nobody would notice your absence. The marks without the one-anothers.

Neither is the thing. And neither is nothing — each holds precisely what the other lacks.

The first ditch is a good meal cooked on a camping stove. The second is a full kitchen with the gas turned off. One of them is feeding somebody.

And the second ditch is the more dangerous, for a reason worth naming plainly: its deficiency is invisible. Attendance looks like belonging from outside, and after a few years it looks like belonging from inside too. A man can sit in a doctrinally excellent church for a decade with no one who would ring if he vanished, and never once name the problem, because everything on the surface is in good order and the fault is an absence.

If you are in the first ditch and calling it your cope, notice that you can at least see what is missing. That is not nothing. The people in the second one frequently cannot.

Staying and leaving

This chapter will not tell you which. It cannot see your situation, and any book that issues that ruling from a distance is doing something it has no business doing.

What it can give you is the shape of the decision.

Start from the ideal, honestly. The New Testament assumes committed life together in a local body, and nothing here softens that. Leaving is a loss, and treating it as a preference is how people end up alone with a grievance and a podcast.

And then note that Scripture makes room for exceptions to bonds far stronger than this one. Asked about divorce, Jesus does not say never. He says Moses permitted it because of your hardness of heart — a concession to sin, given as protection for the injured party, in a covenant considerably weightier than congregational membership.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 19:8 — “Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so.” (ESV)

Directly stated. Note the structure: an ideal, a permitted exception, and a named cause. The hardness of heart in view is the offender’s, and the permission functions as protection for the person sinned against.

Two things follow, and the second one people get backwards.

First: the exception exists because other people sin, and it exists to protect the person they sinned against. If you have been reading that clause as though your own sinfulness were what prompts your wish to leave, you have it inverted, and the inversion pins exactly the person who most needs the permission.

Second: this analogy is stronger than your case requires. Marriage is a covenant between two named people before God. Congregational membership is not that, and Scripture nowhere treats leaving a congregation as its equivalent. So if the greater bond may be dissolved for hardness of heart, the lesser one certainly may. Do not let the comparison raise your bar.

And the asymmetry, which belongs in the arithmetic. Leaving wrongly and staying wrongly are not symmetrical mistakes. Leaving wrongly costs you a community and is reversible — you can return, or find another. Staying wrongly costs years, and the damage accrues while you are deciding. That is not a thumb on the scale toward leaving. It is a fact about the clock, and the clock is running either way.

A tenant deciding about a bad landlord is not asking whether he is a good man. He is asking whether the roof leaks, whether it gets fixed when he says so, and how many winters he has already spent under it.

Your own part in it

Some readers of this chapter contributed something. Some contributed nothing. This book will not sort you, because it cannot, and because being told which one you are by a page is worth nothing either way.

What it will say is the thing that keeps the question survivable: having contributed does not make you responsible for what was done to you. Those are two separate accounts, exactly as chapter twenty-five had it, and settling one has never once altered the figure in the other. You may have behaved badly. You may have been difficult, or defensive, or wrong on the substance. None of that transfers a single item from their column to yours.

Two ledgers on the same desk. Balancing one has never yet changed the figure in the other.

Discernment, and why yours may be underbuilt

This chapter has now declined to give you a rule about nine times. That is deliberate, and it incurs an obligation. If I will not hand you rules, I owe you help with the thing that replaces them — otherwise use discernment is just abandonment with better manners.

So: what is it, and where does it come from?

The key text has a phrase in it that almost everyone reads past. Solid food, Hebrews says, is for the mature — those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil.

Trained. By practice. It is an acquired capacity, not an issued one, and it is acquired the way every other capacity is acquired: by attempting it badly, repeatedly, and being corrected.

Warrant · Direct

Hebrews 5:14 — “those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil.” (ESV); with Philippians 1:9–10 — “that your love may abound more and more, with knowledge and all discernment, so that you may approve what is excellent.”

Both directly stated. Note the source in Philippians: discernment grows out of love and knowledge, not out of suspicion. A reader who thinks discernment means becoming harder to fool has the wrong model of it.

Why yours may not have developed

Now put that beside what chapter nineteen established: a skill is built by attempting it badly somewhere the failure is survivable. No lit room, no skill.

Discernment is a skill. So a person whose church was the unsafe room has been systematically prevented from developing the exact faculty they now need in order to evaluate that church.

That is not a character flaw, and it explains something that bewilders people about themselves: the swing between total trust and total suspicion, with nothing available in between.

Think about how anyone learns to judge weight. Nobody learns the difference between five kilos and eight by being told. You lift both, guess, get corrected, and lift again, a few hundred times, until your hands know. A person who was never handed anything has exactly two settings available — light enough and too heavy — because the middle requires calibration and calibration requires having been handed things.

That is the oscillation. It is a training deficit, not a defect, and the training can be done at any age.

You do not learn five kilos from eight by being told. You lift both, guess, and get corrected, a few hundred times. Someone who was never handed anything has two settings: light enough, and too heavy.

How it actually grows

On small stakes first. You do not calibrate on the decision that matters most. Practice reading situations where being wrong costs you very little — which is precisely the opposite of what a person in this position tends to do, having saved up all their judgment for the enormous question they cannot afford to get wrong.

With feedback. A guess you never check teaches nothing. Say out loud what you expect to happen, then watch. Write it down first, because memory quietly edits itself in favour of whatever occurred.

In company, and mutually exposed. Chapter nineteen’s clause applies here too: keep watch on yourself. Calibration happens alongside people who are also taking a risk, not under observation by people who are not.

Slowly, and after the grief rather than during it. Judgment made inside acute distress is not discernment; it is triage. Triage is a legitimate thing and it is for emergencies, and most of these decisions are not emergencies — which is what the high bar earlier in this chapter is protecting.

And if you cannot presently tell safe from unsafe: you are not broken. You were kept from the training. Building it is not a prerequisite to the repair. It is part of the repair.

Where this book stops

If a crime was committed

Some of what gets filed under church hurt is criminal, and at that point this chapter has obligations rather than opinions.

Scripture is not on the side of silence. Leviticus says a witness who does not speak bears iniquity. Proverbs says to open your mouth for those who cannot. Ephesians says to take no part in the works of darkness but to expose them.

And Romans 13 settles the question people are actually stuck on. The civil magistrate bears the sword as God’s servant, an avenger who carries out wrath on the wrongdoer. Reporting a crime to civil authority is therefore not a failure of Christian forbearance. It is the use of an instrument God himself appointed for exactly that purpose.

The text used against this is being misread. Somebody will quote 1 Corinthians 6 — dare any of you go to law against another? That passage is about civil disputes between believers: property, money, grievance. It is not about crimes, and Paul is not instructing a church to handle felony internally. A congregation citing it to discourage a report is misapplying it, and you may say so.

Finding counsel — what to look for: somebody with no stake in the institution’s reputation; somebody who says I don’t know about the legal side and points you elsewhere; somebody who asks what you want rather than telling you. And get legal advice separately from pastoral advice. A pastor advising on legal exposure is outside his competence and may have a conflict of interest he has not noticed.

Reporting obligations vary by jurisdiction and by role. This book does not state them and neither should anyone who is not qualified to. That question goes to someone who is.

What God may make of it — and who is allowed to say so

Now the last thing, and it has been kept until last on purpose.

There is a true and enormous claim available here: that God intends to use this. That the wound may one day be the thing that makes you useful to somebody nobody else could reach. Luther’s years of crushing scrupulosity were the preparation for the clearest teaching on grace the church has had in five hundred years. John Newton had commanded slave ships, and it was the man who had done it who wrote the confession that helped end it.

All of that is true. And it is the single most commonly mistimed true thing said to hurt people, which is why it is at the bottom of this chapter and not the top.

The rule about who is speaking

Joseph says to the brothers who sold him: you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good.

It is his to say. Had one of the brothers said it, it would be monstrous. Identical sentence, opposite function, and the only variable is who is speaking.

So this chapter will not say it to you. It will put the material where you can reach it, and leave the saying to you, if and when you can — and if you cannot, you have not failed at anything.

Warrant · Direct

Job 42:7 — “My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and worth sitting with: the friends’ theology was largely orthodox. Their error was positional — they applied true things to a man who had not asked, at a time he had not chosen. Being right about God is not the same as speaking rightly of him.

Two claims that must not be run together

God intends to use it and God intended it to happen are different sentences. Paul writes that God works all things together for good — works with, not authors. The older confessions put a guard on it that belongs here: God ordains whatsoever comes to pass, yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin.

Without that guard the comfort curdles into something unbearable, which says that God did this to you on purpose, the way he did it to his Son. He did not. He was against it, in the strongest language in the book, and chapter twenty-one is his charge sheet.

Backwards, never forwards

Luther could say it at the end. Nobody could have said it to him in the monastery, and anyone who tried would have been Job’s friend. Newton could say it after the pamphlet. Nobody could have said it to him mid-voyage.

Charles Simeon was appointed to Holy Trinity in Cambridge over the congregation’s objections and they made him pay for it. The pew-holders locked their pews and stayed away. He put chairs in the aisles; the churchwardens threw them out. That went on for something over a decade, and he stayed fifty-four years, and it made him the pastor he became.

Which is a magnificent story and is also the one somebody will hand you to explain why you should stay. So note what Simeon had: a freehold nobody could revoke, a university fellowship, no dependants, and an income. Fifty-four years of patience is cheaper when you cannot be evicted. His path is a path. It is not the path, and the people who quote him rarely mention the freehold.

It is a thing you may one day be able to say. It is not a thing anyone may say to you now.

Going back

Is returning to a church the hoped-for end? Honestly, yes — because the one-anothers require other people and there is no version of the Christian life that is finally solitary.

But hold that the way Hebrews holds it, which is about meeting, not membership rolls. Two or three believers who actually know you is not a failure state. It is missing real things, and you can name what they are, and it is more than a great many people in full pews have. Sufficient for now, and not the end state — which is a different sentence from you are doing it wrong.

And one word on the question people will ask you, which is what do you think God is teaching you through this?

It is a fair question in one mode and a cruel one in another. As a prayer, asked by you, in your own time, it is exactly right and Scripture is full of it. As a demand — answerable now, to a person who is waiting — it has no biblical warrant at all. Job never gets an answer and is vindicated without one. Jesus, handed the same question about a blind man, refuses to run the calculation.

So ask it if you want it. And if nothing comes back, that is not a failed exam. Some of it is answered later. Some of it is not answered here.

Case · the two settings

“N.” left a church four years ago after a long, bad year and has visited eleven since. Each visit runs the same way: she likes it enormously for three weeks, then notices something — a phrase from the front, a way an elder answered a question — and cannot get past it, and does not go back.

She describes this as having become discerning. Her sister describes it as being unable to settle. Both readings fit the evidence, which is the problem, and N. has no way to tell which is true because the faculty that would tell her is the one under question.

What nobody has said to her is that she is not choosing between trust and suspicion. She has only those two settings available, and she has them because for eleven years she was in a room where testing anything was expensive. The middle setting was never trainable, and she is now being asked to use it on the biggest question she has.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

Why this presentation is distinctive. Harm from a religious community differs structurally from other institutional betrayal in that the framework the client would ordinarily use to interpret and metabolize suffering is the same framework the harm was delivered in. Coping resources and injury source are not separable, which is why these clients often present flatter and more disoriented than the reported events would predict.

The God-concept is the load-bearing item. Where harm was administered under divine authority, the client’s working representation of God frequently acquires the features of specific people. This is not a doctrinal problem and does not respond to doctrinal correction; it is closer to a learned association. Chapter twenty’s material on surveillance is the companion piece.

The discernment deficit is worth taking seriously as a deficit. Clients from high-control settings frequently show poor calibration rather than poor judgment: trust and suspicion present as the only available settings, because graduated testing was never safe enough to practise. Framing this as an untrained capacity rather than a personality trait tends to open work that reassurance does not, and small-stakes calibration exercises are the intervention.

Two things this chapter deliberately does not do, which you may want to know: it does not advise staying or leaving, and it does not offer redemptive meaning to the client. It supplies criteria for the first and holds the second as something only the client may say, on the Joseph principle. If a client reports being told by others what God is teaching them, that is worth examining.

Where a crime is disclosed, the chapter explicitly supports reporting to civil authority, identifies the scriptural argument commonly misused to discourage it, and hands jurisdiction-specific questions to qualified advisers.

Practice

Run the two tests separately. On your current situation, answer them one at a time and do not let the answers bleed. Marks: is the Word preached, are the sacraments administered, does discipline function? One-anothers: is there anybody here who would notice if I stopped coming, and anybody I could tell something true? Most people find one answer clear and the other uncomfortable.

Name your ditch, without the verdict. Which one are you in? Write what it holds and what it lacks. Then resist ranking yourself.

Start calibrating on something small. This week, pick a low-stakes social read — how a conversation will go, whether someone meant what you thought. Write your prediction down before, then check it. Do it five times. You are not trying to be right; you are trying to find out how accurate you currently are, which is information you have probably never gathered.

Read the millstone verse and Ezekiel 34 in one sitting. Ten minutes. Then answer: whose side does the evidence say he is on?

Journaling.

  1. Whose face is on God when you pray? Name the person if there is one. Then ask what evidence you actually have about him, independent of them.
  2. What are you afraid you will become if you conclude they were wrong? Most people have an answer to this and have never said it.
  3. Where is your judgment currently set — trust or suspicion? When was the last time you were in the middle, and what made that possible?

To bring to your counselor. “I can’t tell whether I’ve become discerning or just unable to settle. Can we work on how I’d know the difference?”
