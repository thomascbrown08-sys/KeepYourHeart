Part III · Enduring · Chapter Nine

The Cup and the Garden

On the night the bravest being in the universe asked to be let off.

There are days when none of it works.

You do the practice. You name the thing. You say your sentences and they come back off the ceiling like they were made of tin. The distress does not move, and somewhere in hour four you stop believing that any of this was ever going to help, and the not-believing is worse than the distress was.

This chapter is for that day, and I am not going to open it with a technique.

What acceptance is for

The skill that addresses this is the most philosophically loaded thing in the whole treatment, and also one of the most genuinely useful.

Term
Radical acceptance
Fully and completely accepting a reality you cannot change, in order to stop the secondary suffering that fighting it produces. The core observation is that pain plus non-acceptance equals something far worse than pain: the refusal itself becomes an additional and often larger source of anguish.

That observation is correct and it is not trivial. A great deal of what we call suffering is the exhausting overlay of protest on top of a fact — the internal argument with what has already happened, run at volume, for years. Watching someone put that argument down is watching them get a portion of their life back.

So: real skill, real benefit, no sneering. The question is what happens when you push on the word accept and ask what it is pointed at.

Because acceptance is a transitive act. It goes toward something. And if what it goes toward is simply reality — the way things are, impersonally, with nobody behind it — then what you have is not really acceptance. It is capitulation dressed in better clothes. You stop fighting because fighting is pointless, which is true, and which leaves you exactly as alone as you were.

Worse, the instruction is often heard as stop wanting it to be different. And a person in real anguish cannot do that, and cannot be made to, and mostly ends up pretending to.

The garden

So let me take you to the one night in the Bible that everything in this book has been walking toward.

He is thirty-three. He has spent three years telling frightened people not to be afraid. He once slept through a storm so violent that professional fishermen — men who had spent their whole lives on that lake — were screaming that they were about to die, and he had to be woken up for it.

And now he is face down in the dirt in a grove of olive trees, and the Gospel writers, who had every reason to tidy this up, record it without the slightest attempt at management.

Mark uses two words for what happened to him that are among the strongest in his whole Gospel. The first carries the sense of being appalled — shocked, horrified, the way you are when something arrives that you cannot take in. The second means anguished distress. And then Jesus says it himself, in the plainest words available: My soul is very sorrowful, even to death.

Not I am troubled. Sorrowful to the point of dying of it.

The letter to the Hebrews, looking back, remembers that he prayed in the days of his flesh with loud cries and tears. Loud. Not composed, not dignified, not the serene figure of the paintings. A man making noise in a garden at night.

Warrant · Direct

Mark 14:32–36; Hebrews 5:7 — the distress in Gethsemane, and the loud cries and tears.

Directly stated in both places. Mark’s vocabulary is unusually strong and is not softened anywhere in the tradition; Hebrews independently confirms the manner of the praying.

Warrant · Contested · textual

Luke 22:43–44 — the strengthening angel and the sweat like great drops of blood.

Flagged honestly: these two verses are absent from several of the earliest and most reliable manuscripts and present in many others. Most modern translations print them with a marginal note. Serious scholars disagree about whether Luke wrote them. This book does not lean on them — Mark and Matthew record the anguish without them, and Hebrews records the crying out — and it would be dishonest to build on a detail while hiding that it is disputed.

The bravest being in the universe, on the last night of his life, went into a garden and asked to be let off. That is not a marginal detail the Gospels tolerate. Three of them put it in on purpose.

What he was afraid of

Not the nails. Plenty of ordinary men have walked to worse deaths with their composure intact, and some of them were his own followers within a generation.

He says it himself, and the word he uses is doing all the work: Father … remove this cup from me.

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures “the cup” is settled imagery for the wrath of God against sin — the cup of staggering, of judgment, handed to the guilty and drained to the dregs. Isaiah uses it. Jeremiah uses it. The Psalms use it. Any Jewish reader in the first century would have heard it instantly.

So what is bearing down on him in that garden is not primarily execution. It is the cup — the entire weight of God’s just verdict against every false worship and every self-made ledger and every attempt anyone ever made to be their own justification. He is about to drink the God-forsakenness that all of that earned.

Warrant · Entailed

Isaiah 51:17, 22; Jeremiah 25:15–16; Psalm 75:8 — the cup as the wrath and judgment of God.

The Old Testament imagery is directly stated. That this is the cup Jesus means in Gethsemane is entailed rather than stated: he does not define the term. The identification rests on the settled usage above and on the cry from the cross, and it is the reading of the Christian tradition essentially throughout.

The two things he does

And here, undone and trembling and face down, he does two things, and I want to take them separately because everything practical in this chapter comes out of them.

He calls God by a family name

Mark alone preserves the actual Aramaic word he used: Abba. It is the ordinary intimate word for father inside a household — not baby-talk, as it is sometimes sentimentally rendered, since grown children used it too, but family address rather than formal address. In the worst hour of his existence, with the cup an arm’s length away, he does not reach for a title. He reaches for the family word.

He asks — and then he says yet

Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.

Read the order, because the order is the whole thing.

He asks first. He states plainly that he wants out. Matthew records him going back and asking a second time and a third. This is not a man performing serenity. He wants the cup removed, he says so, he says it repeatedly, and the Holy Spirit saw fit to preserve every instance of it in Scripture rather than quietly editing them down to the resolved version.

And then, having asked, he says yet.

He asked. Three times. In the dirt. The asking is preserved in the text on purpose, and it comes before the yielding, not instead of it.

Resignation and surrender are not the same act

Here is the distinction this chapter exists to make, and once you have it you cannot unsee it.

Resignation says: this cannot be changed, so stop fighting. Its object is a fact. Its posture is the closing down of wanting. It works, after a fashion, and it costs you something — because a person who has practised not wanting things has practised something that generalizes.

Surrender says: this could be changed, and it is not being changed, and I am handing it to the one who is not changing it. Its object is a person. And it requires you to still have a will, because you cannot hand over something you have already destroyed.

Now look at what Jesus actually says, because it is astonishing and almost nobody notices it. All things are possible for you.

He explicitly does not accept the cup as inevitable. He says out loud that it is not. There is no fate in that garden, no impersonal necessity, no “reality as it is.” He states plainly that the Father could remove it and then submits to the Father not removing it.

He did not accept the cup because it was unavoidable. He said out loud that it was avoidable. He accepted it because of who was holding it out.

Which means the Christian version of acceptance is not stop wanting it to be different. It is: want it, say so, and hand the wanting to somebody. And not what I will is not the deletion of your will. It is the transfer of one, which is a completely different operation and requires you to still be in possession of it.

The bottom

Hours later, nailed to a piece of wood in the dark, he says the most desolate sentence in the Bible: My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?

Two things about it, and then I am going to stop, because there are places where explanation is the wrong instrument.

The first is that he is quoting. That line opens Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in abandonment and ends in vindication and a coming kingdom, and he would have known where the quotation goes. That does not make the cry less real. It means the man drowning knew the whole psalm and started it anyway from the first line.

The second is that the forsakenness is not theatre. He was not performing an emotion for our benefit. Something real happened there, and the church has never claimed to be able to explain its mechanism — which is the right instinct, and the same instinct that produced Chalcedon in chapter four. We say what happened. We decline to say how. Whatever it cost within the life of God is not ours to diagram, and I am not going to pretend otherwise in order to give you a tidier paragraph.

Warrant · Direct · with the mystery left standing

Mark 15:34 — “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”; quoting Psalm 22:1.

Directly stated. What is deliberately not stated here is any account of how this works internally — the tradition affirms that Christ bore real God-forsakenness in his human nature without asserting any division in God’s own being, and stops there. Any explanation you are offered that resolves this neatly is going further than the church has ever gone.

What this actually gives you on the bad day

Three things, and they are not techniques.

You are allowed to ask. Out loud, repeatedly, without composure. The asking is not a failure of submission; in the one place Scripture shows us perfect submission, the asking is the larger part of the text. If you have been quietly ashamed of praying for the thing to be taken away, stop. He prayed it three times.

Fear is not a verdict on your faith. Whatever your dread means, it cannot mean what you have been afraid it means, because the same physiological catastrophe happened to the Son of God in a garden and it did not indicate any deficiency in him. It indicated that what was coming was genuinely terrible.

The courage is borrowed, not manufactured. This is the difference between Christian endurance and the white-knuckle bravery you can buy on any bookshelf. You are not being asked to generate strength. You are being asked to look at someone who held his own cup in the dark and did not let go — and the holding on your side is downstream of that, not a fresh production of it.

Where this book stops

This is the chapter that comes nearest to crisis, so let me be exact about the limits.

Nothing here is a crisis protocol, and nothing here should be used as one. If the distress is acute — if you are unsafe, or close to it — the material you need is the material your clinician gave you, and it should be reached for first and without guilt. Reaching for it is not a failure of faith. A ladder is not less useful because you also believe in God.

And a warning about a specific misuse of this chapter, because it is a real one: Gethsemane is not an instruction to endure something you could get help for. Christ drinking the cup does not mean you must drink yours. That is the precise inversion of the point — he drank it so that what is handed to you is a different cup. If you find yourself using this chapter to justify not asking for help, you have it exactly backwards, and please tell someone this week.

Case · the wrong kind of yes

“S.” is thirty-eight and has been described by three separate people as remarkably serene about a genuinely difficult diagnosis. She does not complain. She says the right things and appears to mean them.

She has also stopped praying about it, and if pressed will say that it seemed presumptuous to keep asking for something God had evidently decided against. Somewhere in the last eighteen months she concluded that acceptance meant no longer wanting it to be otherwise, and she has become very good at not wanting things. Her husband says she has gone quiet in a way that has nothing to do with the illness.

S. has not surrendered. She has resigned, and she has done it so competently that everyone around her has praised her for it. Nobody has told her that the man she follows asked three times.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The distinction that may be clinically useful. This chapter separates acceptance from the suppression of desire. Clients — religious ones especially — frequently hear acceptance-based interventions as an instruction to stop wanting their circumstances to differ, and comply by flattening affect and reducing expressed need. That presents as excellent progress and is not.

The reframe. Here, acceptance is modelled on a scene in which the subject states his desire explicitly and repeatedly, expresses acute distress without regulation, and then yields — with the wanting preserved throughout. This gives a religious client permission to keep the want and the request, which is usually where the affect is.

The specific misuse to watch for, and it is serious: this material can be used to justify enduring what should be treated, or remaining in a harmful situation on the grounds that suffering is Christlike. The chapter names and forbids that reading explicitly, on the grounds that the whole point of the cup is that it was drunk instead of being handed on. If you hear a client reasoning toward endurance of something modifiable, that box is worth reading with them.

Practice

Read Mark 14:32–42 slowly, twice. The second time, count. How many verses describe distress and asking, and how many describe yielding? The proportion is the permission.

The Gethsemane prayer, in three parts. Say it in this order and do not skip to the third:

  1. Address. Use the family word, whatever that is for you.
  2. Ask. Say plainly what you want removed. Do not soften it, do not add qualifiers, and do not preface it with an apology for asking. He didn’t.
  3. Yet. Hand it over — without cancelling the asking. The word is yet, not never mind.

Journaling.

  1. What have you stopped asking for because you decided the answer was already no? Write it down. Then ask.
  2. Which are you actually doing — resigning or surrendering? The test: is there still something you want, or have you arranged not to want anything?
  3. Where have you been ashamed of being afraid? Put Mark’s two words next to it and see whether the shame survives.

To bring to your counselor. “I think I’ve been calling it acceptance when what I’ve actually done is stop wanting things. Can we look at that?”
