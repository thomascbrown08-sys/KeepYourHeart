Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty-Six

A Name They Did Not Give You

On the difference between a description and a destiny.

A label does not only describe you. That is what makes it dangerous.

Once a classification is attached — a diagnosis, a status, a category, a word said about you at school that stuck — it starts doing two things that description alone would never do.

It works backwards. Everything you ever did gets re-read through it. Behaviour that was ordinary at the time is retrieved and re-interpreted as an early sign. Your own memory cooperates, because you cannot un-know the label while remembering.

And it works forwards. It becomes the frame every future action is assessed inside. Do well and it is managing your condition. Do badly and it is confirmation. There is no behaviour available to you that the label cannot absorb, which means no amount of evidence changes anything.

A label does not only describe. It reorganizes everything before it and forecloses everything after — which means no amount of subsequent evidence can dislodge it, because the evidence gets read through it too.

In My Toolbox

The clinical and sociological treatment: Labelling theory — how a single classification becomes a master status that organizes all other interpretation. And Testimonial injustice — the specific harm of having your account discounted because of who you are taken to be.

The version that happens inside a church

There is a form of this that is sharper than the secular kind, and it is worth stating plainly because it is rarely said out loud.

The Christian faith holds that the Spirit of God remakes people from the inside. Not improves them. Remakes them. That claim is not a marginal doctrine; it is close to the centre, and any church will say it on a Sunday without hesitation.

And the same room can hold, at the same time, a settled conviction that you are what you were categorized as, permanently, and that the doctrine has an exception with your name on it.

That is not a complaint about being treated unkindly. It is an observation that the position is internally incoherent. A doctrine of the Spirit's power that excludes a particular person is not a stricter version of the doctrine. It is a denial of it, wearing caution as a costume.

The verb tense that carries the argument

Paul writes to a church in Corinth and lists the kinds of people who will not inherit the kingdom. It is a serious list. It includes things that carry permanent social consequence in any century, and I am not going to reproduce it item by item, because the argument does not live in the items.

It lives in the sentence immediately after.

And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God. 1 Corinthians 6:11

Such were.

Past tense, and the whole thing turns on it. And note the setting: Paul is not writing to a seminar. He is writing a letter to a functioning congregation, in which he says out loud that some of the people currently sitting there used to be on that list. They were not excluded from the room on that basis. They were the room.

Warrant · Direct

1 Corinthians 6:9–11 — the list, followed by “And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified.” (ESV)

Directly stated. The three verbs after the turn are all past tense and all passive — washed, sanctified, justified. Things done to them, already completed, not achievements they produced.

And such were some of you. The entire argument is a verb tense — in a letter addressed to a working church made partly of those people.

And Paul keeps his own label

Now the complication, because if the chapter stopped there it would be too easy and you would not believe it.

The same Paul writes to Timothy that Christ came into the world to save sinners — of whom I am the foremost.

Present tense. Not was. He has just spent a career arguing that the old identity is past, and he applies the worst available description to himself in the present, decades after his conversion.

Both are true and they are not in tension, because they answer different questions. His standing is settled and past tense. His self-assessment is honest and present tense. He is not confused; he is holding chapter four's discipline on the most personal material available to him.

And then he does something with it that I think is the most hopeful sentence in this wing. He says he received mercy for this reason: that in him, as the foremost, Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who would believe.

His record is not deleted. It is repurposed. The very thing that should have disqualified him becomes the exhibit — the demonstration case, the thing you point at to prove the mechanism works on hard material.

Warrant · Direct

1 Timothy 1:15–16 — “of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example.” (ESV)

Directly stated, present tense, and the purpose clause is explicit: the history is retained and assigned a function.

Paul keeps the worst description of himself in the present tense, and says he was shown mercy as an example. The record was not deleted. It was made the exhibit.

The name on the stone

There is one more thing, in a strange corner of Revelation, and for someone whose identities have all been assigned by other people it is worth sitting with.

To the one who conquers, he says, I will give a white stone, with a new name written on the stone that no one knows except the one who receives it.

Consider what that is not. It is not on file. It was not issued by a court, a clinic, a school, a department, or a congregation. It cannot be looked up, quoted back at you, entered into a record, or used to organize anyone else's interpretation of you — because nobody else can read it.

Every other name you carry was given by an institution and is stored somewhere. This one is given by God and held privately between the two of you.

Warrant · Direct

Revelation 2:17 — “a white stone, with a new name written on the stone that no one knows except the one who receives it.” (ESV); with 2 Corinthians 5:17.

Directly stated. The privacy clause is in the text and is unusual: most biblical naming is public and covenantal. This one is explicitly not.

A new name that no one knows except the one who receives it. Every other name you carry was issued by an institution and is on file somewhere. That one is not.

The guard, and it is not optional

Now the thing this chapter must say plainly, because everything above could be misused and the misuse would hurt people.

A new identity does not remove accountability structures.

Whatever conditions, supervision, restrictions, disclosure obligations or limits apply to you exist for other people's protection, and nothing in this chapter touches a single one of them. Not one. A theology that produced fewer safeguards for the people around you would have refuted itself on contact, because it would have made the doctrine an instrument of harm.

Being a new creation and being under legitimate restriction are not in tension, and Scripture shows exactly that combination without embarrassment.

When Paul arrived in Jerusalem after his conversion, the disciples were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. These are the apostles. They had heard the reports. And they were frightened of him and kept their distance.

The text does not rebuke them for it. Their caution is recorded as reasonable — because it was reasonable; the man had been arresting people like them. What happens instead is that Barnabas takes him, brings him to them, and vouches.

Both things happened. The caution was legitimate and someone vouched anyway. Neither cancelled the other, and it took a person willing to attach their own credibility to him to bridge it.

Warrant · Direct

Acts 9:26–27 — “And when he had come to Jerusalem, he attempted to join the disciples. And they were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and the narrative offers no criticism of the disciples' fear. It records reasonable caution and a person willing to vouch, as two separate facts.

They were all afraid of Paul, and Scripture does not rebuke them for it. Caution was reasonable. Barnabas vouched anyway. Both happened and neither one cancelled the other.

What a label can and cannot do

To be exact, because vagueness here helps nobody.

It can be accurate. Often it is. Arguing with the accurate part is a waste of your life and it destroys your credibility on everything else you say. If the description fits the act, concede it immediately and completely.

It can properly govern how institutions treat you. That is what institutions are for, and a category that carries consequences is not an injustice merely because the consequences are heavy.

It cannot determine what you become. That is a prediction dressed as a description, and it is the extension you are entitled to refuse.

And it does not settle what God says you are. He may well name the act, and name it more exactly than any institution did — Scripture is not shy about calling things what they are. What no institution can do is make that naming final for someone who has been washed, sanctified and justified. The label is not overruled by being ignored. It is overruled by verse eleven.

So the practical move is not to fight the label. It is to concede the accurate part fast, refuse the extension, and decline to argue about the last two — which are not up for negotiation with anybody who is not God.

The label may be accurate. Accurate and total are different words, and the whole fight is over the gap between them.

Case · the exception

“J.” has been at his church nine years. He has never missed a work day, gives regularly, and has been told more than once that he is an example of what grace does.

He has also asked twice to help with something — anything — and been told both times, kindly, that it is complicated. Nobody has ever explained what the standard is or whether a version of him could meet it, and he has stopped asking, because asking a third time would confirm something.

What J. cannot say out loud is that the sermons keep asserting a doctrine the building does not appear to believe about him. He is not angry about the caution. He would accept a stated condition and meet it. What he cannot get is a sentence he could act on.

Composite. Not a real person. Note that some restrictions are appropriate and non-negotiable; the difficulty here is the absence of any stated standard at all.

For a counselor

The pattern. A master status — diagnostic, legal, or social — that has become the organizing frame for the client's self-concept and for others' interpretation of them, with the characteristic feature that no behavioural evidence can revise it, because evidence is read through the label.

What this chapter attempts. Not to dispute the label's accuracy, which would be both dishonest and clinically counterproductive, but to separate description from prediction and to locate identity in a source the labelling institutions do not control. For a religious client that source is already available and frequently has never been applied to themselves.

The guard is explicit and load-bearing. The chapter states without qualification that a changed identity removes no safeguards, restrictions, or obligations, and models this from Acts 9 — where reasonable fear of a genuinely dangerous history is recorded without criticism alongside someone willing to vouch. If a client is using new-creation language to argue against conditions that exist to protect others, the chapter does not support them and says so.

Worth watching: the distinction between grief at a foreclosed future and resentment at appropriate consequence. They present similarly and require different work.

Practice

Split the label. Write the classification you carry. Then two columns: what it accurately describes, and what it has been extended to predict. Be scrupulous in the first column — overstate it if anything. The second column is the only thing you are entitled to refuse, and refusing it is only credible if the first is fully conceded.

Find the backwards work. Name one thing from before the label that has since been re-read as an early sign. Ask honestly whether the re-reading is true. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is a story imposed on an ordinary memory, and noticing which is which is worth doing.

Read 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 and stop at the verb. Read verse 11 five times. Such were some of you. Write what it would mean if the tense applied to you.

Journaling.

  1. Who gave you the name you actually live under? Not the legal one — the operative one. When was it assigned, and by whom?
  2. Where have you argued with the accurate part? That fight has almost certainly cost you credibility you needed elsewhere.
  3. Paul's history became the exhibit rather than the disqualification. Is there any version of that available to you — not as a platform, but as one true thing you could say to one person who needed it?

To bring to your counselor. “I can’t tell any more which parts of how I see myself are accurate and which parts are the label doing my thinking. Can we sort that?”
