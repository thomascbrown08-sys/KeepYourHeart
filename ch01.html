Part I · Foundations · Chapter One

All Truth Is God’s Truth

On receiving a good gift from a hand you do not recognize.

Picture the room. Fluorescent light, a circle of chairs, a laminated handout. The instructor is teaching you to notice your own thoughts without immediately grabbing hold of them, and the strange thing is that it works — you try it, and a wave that would ordinarily have taken you under goes by instead.

And then, somewhere in the next ten minutes, a phrase goes past that snags. Maybe it is the instruction to observe without judging. Maybe it is a line about trusting what your deepest self already knows. Maybe it is nothing you could quote back afterward — just a change in the air, a sense that this is not merely a technique but a whole account of what a person is, and that the account is not yours.

You do not say anything. What would you even say? The skill worked. You are not about to stand up in a therapy group and pick a fight with a laminated handout. So you put the snag in your pocket and take it home, and it sits there for months.

This chapter is about what to do with the snag.

Two ways to get this wrong

The first is to shoot the messenger. Trace the mindfulness training back to its sources, find Zen at the bottom of it, and conclude that the whole enterprise is a Trojan horse. This has the pleasant feeling of vigilance. It is also cheap, and it costs the person who does it something real, because the skills in question are keeping people alive.

The second way is more common among believers, and it looks like humility. You take the skills and simply decline to think about it. Faith goes in one drawer, treatment in the other, and you never open both at once. This holds up fine right until the drawers make competing claims about who you are — which they will, because both of them are in the business of telling you where your worth comes from and what you are for.

What we want is a third thing: to receive the good gift without accepting the account of reality that was handed over with it. That is not a dodge. It is a specific and defensible position, and it has a name.

One instinct throws out the medicine because it distrusts the pharmacist. The other swallows the whole bottle without reading the label. Neither one is faith.

Common grace

Term
Common grace
God’s goodness given broadly to everyone, believer and unbeliever alike — rain, harvest, intelligence, skill, beauty, and the capacity to discover true things. It does not save anyone. It is not a lesser version of salvation. It is God being generous to a world that has not asked him to be.

Jesus is the one who states it most bluntly. God, he says, makes the sun rise on the evil and the good and sends rain on the just and the unjust (Matthew 5:45). Read that slowly, because it is stranger than it sounds. He is not saying God tolerates the unjust. He is saying God feeds them. The harvest comes up in the field of a man who has never once given thanks for it, and it comes up because God sent the weather.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 5:45 — “For he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust.” (ESV)

The text says the thing directly: God distributes real goods without regard to the recipient’s standing. Nothing has to be inferred.

Now extend it, because Scripture does. When the tabernacle needed building, God did not merely command the work; he filled a craftsman named Bezalel with his Spirit for the specific purpose of giving him skill — design, metalwork, stonecutting, carpentry (Exodus 31:1–5). Competence is treated there as a divine gift, not a neutral human possession. And if craftsmanship comes from God when it shows up in a believer, it did not stop coming from God when it showed up in somebody else.

Warrant · Entailed

Exodus 31:1–5 — God fills Bezalel with his Spirit for ability, intelligence, knowledge, and craftsmanship.

The passage directly states that skill is a gift of God’s Spirit. That it applies to all human skill, including skill in people who do not know him, is entailed rather than stated — it follows from Matthew 5:45 and from James 1:17, but Exodus itself is talking about one man and one building.

John Calvin, who is nobody’s idea of a soft touch on human depravity, put this about as strongly as it can be put. Reading the pagan Greek and Roman writers, he said flatly that the admirable light of truth shining in them ought to teach us that the human mind, however fallen, is still clothed with God’s gifts — and that to reject truth wherever it appears is to insult the Spirit who gave it. Calvin thought contempt for pagan learning was not piety. He thought it was ingratitude.

Warrant · Historical

Calvin, Institutes II.ii.15–16 — on truth found in secular writers as a gift of the Spirit, whose rejection is an insult to its giver.

Historical warrant means this is the settled judgment of a major voice in the tradition, not a claim the Bible makes in these words. It carries weight. It does not carry the weight of Scripture.

The compressed version of all this is a saying usually traced back to Augustine: wherever truth is found, it belongs to the Lord. It was never the property of whoever happened to notice it first.

A true thing does not become less true because the wrong person found it. It only means somebody else got to your Father’s property before you did.

The four layers

Here is the tool this book will use for the rest of the way. Any account of human beings — a therapy, a philosophy, a self-help book, a Sunday sermon — is really four things stacked on top of each other, and they come apart under pressure.

  1. The observation. What is actually the case about people. That intense emotion narrows attention. That skipping sleep makes everything worse. That a person raised in a home where their feelings were routinely dismissed will grow up with an unreliable read on their own inner state.
  2. The explanation. Why that is the case — what kind of universe produces creatures like this.
  3. The engine. What is supposed to make a person change, and where the power for it comes from.
  4. The destination. What the changed person is for. What counts as arrival.

Almost all the noise in the Christianity-and-therapy argument comes from confusing these layers. Somebody rejects a true observation because they dislike the metaphysic attached to it. Somebody else swallows a whole destination because they were impressed by an accurate observation.

The position of this book, stated once so I do not have to keep re-arguing it: DBT’s observations are largely excellent, its explanation is incomplete, its engine is underpowered, and its destination is too small. Four different verdicts, and they need to stay four.

Most arguments about faith and therapy are two people agreeing about layer one while shouting at each other about layer four.

The woman who built it

It is easy to argue with a system. It is harder, and more honest, to argue with a system once you know who made it.

On the ninth of March, 1961, a seventeen-year-old from Tulsa named Marsha Linehan was admitted to the Institute of Living in Hartford, Connecticut. She spent something like two years there, much of it as the sole occupant of a seclusion room on the ward reserved for the most severely ill patients. She was diagnosed with schizophrenia, which was wrong, and treated accordingly, which did not help. She hurt herself badly and repeatedly. She was, by the hospital’s own reckoning, among the worst cases in the building.

She was also a Catholic girl who prayed. She wrote letters asking her psychiatrist whether some treatment existed for what she had, and the answer was that it did not. And at some point in that room she made a vow that reads, from this distance, like something out of a much older kind of book: she would get herself out of hell, and then she would go back for everyone else who was still in it.

She got a doctorate. She built the treatment that did not exist. It is now among the most heavily researched psychological interventions in the world, and the number of people alive because of it is not calculable but is not small. She kept the vow.

On the eighteenth of June, 2011, after fifty years of silence, she went back to the Institute of Living — the same building — and told a room of colleagues and patients that the girl in the seclusion room had been her.

Whatever else you conclude in this book, do not conclude it about a straw man. That is who is on the other side of the argument.

The part that should make us uncomfortable

There is one more detail, and it is the one that ought to sting.

Linehan needed a way to teach desperate people how to hold still inside an unbearable moment. She was a Christian looking for it. She did not find it in the Christian material available to her in a form she could teach — so she went and trained in Zen, for years, seriously, and eventually as a teacher of it. That is where the mindfulness in DBT comes from. Not from an infiltration. From a shortage.

We are the ones with the shortage. The Christian tradition has an enormous literature on exactly this — meditation on Scripture, the examination of the conscience, the practice of the presence of God, the psalms as a school for the disordered heart, three hundred years of Puritan pastors writing careful books about melancholy and how to bear it. Most of it sits unread on the top shelf while the people in our pews learn to breathe from somebody else.

So before this book raises a single objection to DBT’s contemplative inheritance — and it will, in Part II, because there are real ones — let the objection carry its own bill.

You are not permitted to take a practice out of someone’s hands unless you have something better to put in them. Criticism that leaves a person emptier than it found them is not discernment. It is vandalism.

Case · the drawer problem

“R.” is thirty-four, six months into a skills group, and doing well by every measure his therapist can see. Fewer crises. Better sleep. He has stopped disappearing for days after an argument with his wife.

He has also stopped praying. Not from a crisis of faith — he would tell you he believes exactly what he always did. But the skills are what actually works now, and prayer has quietly become the thing he does when the skills are not enough, which is less and less often. When his pastor asks how he is doing spiritually, R. says “fine,” and means it, and something in him knows he is lying.

R. has not rejected anything. He has just put the two drawers far enough apart that they never have to be opened at the same time. It will hold until the next thing the skills cannot fix.

Composite. Not a real person; assembled from patterns common enough to be unremarkable.

For a counselor

This chapter is doing something you may find useful rather than obstructive: it is removing the client’s permission to reject the treatment on religious grounds. The argument is that skills developed outside the faith are legitimately theirs to use, and that refusing them is not devotion but waste.

What it also does is name the compartmentalization pattern — competence in one domain, silence in the other, with no traffic between. If you have a religious client who reports that treatment is going well and seems oddly flat about it, that pattern is worth a question.

The rule for the rest of this book

Take the observation. Test the explanation. Replace the engine. Reset the destination.

We are not decontaminating anything. We are doing what the Israelites did on their way out of Egypt, walking off with the gold and putting it to a use its previous owners had not imagined.

Warrant · Interpretation

Exodus 12:35–36 — Israel leaves Egypt carrying Egyptian silver, gold, and clothing.

Flagged honestly: the text reports an event, not a principle. Reading it as a pattern for using pagan learning is an application made by the early church — Origen and Augustine both use it — and it is an illustration in this chapter, not the argument. The argument rests on Matthew 5:45 and Exodus 31. If the Egyptian gold does nothing for you, drop it; nothing above falls down.

Practice

Journaling — the snag. Write for ten minutes on each, longhand if you can:

  1. Name one specific moment in treatment when something snagged. Not the general unease — the actual sentence, the actual phrase, the actual minute.
  2. Run it through the four layers. Was the thing that bothered you an observation (is it true?), an explanation (why is it true?), an engine (what makes me change?), or a destination (what am I becoming?). Most snags turn out to be layers three and four wearing the clothes of layer one.
  3. Where have you kept two drawers? Write down one thing you have learned in treatment that you have never once mentioned in a prayer.

To bring to your counselor. One sentence: “There is a part of this I think is true and a part I think is a claim about what I am. Can I show you which is which?”
