Part III · Enduring · Chapter Twelve

The Key of Promise

On the discovery, made late on a Saturday night, that the door had been openable since Wednesday.

Two travellers come off the path.

Not dramatically. The road has got stony and their feet hurt, and there is an easier meadow running alongside it with a stile into it, and one of them suggests they walk on the softer ground for a while. It is the most reasonable decision in the book.

Then it rains. The water rises, they cannot find their way back in the dark, and they lie down and sleep — and the ground they have slept on belongs to a giant named Despair, who owns Doubting Castle, and who finds them at dawn and drives them into his dungeon.

Bunyan describes the next four days in flat, unhurried prose. They are shut in a dark place with nothing to eat or drink, without light, and nobody knows where they are. On Wednesday the giant beats them. On Thursday he comes down and gives them advice: since their life is nothing but bitterness, they may as well make an end of themselves. On Friday he takes them out to the castle yard to show them the bones of others who took the advice.

And on Saturday, near midnight, they begin to pray. They pray until almost break of day.

And then Christian, still in the dungeon, says something that has stopped readers cold for three and a half centuries: What a fool am I to lie in a stinking dungeon when I may as well walk at liberty. I have a key in my bosom called Promise, that will, I am persuaded, open any lock in Doubting Castle.

He had it when they arrived. He had it while the giant was beating him. He had it on Friday in the yard among the bones.

The key was in his pocket the whole time. That is not a story about a stupid man. It is the most accurate description in English literature of what despair does to memory.

Bunyan knew

It matters enormously who wrote this.

John Bunyan was a tinker — a mender of pots, near the bottom of the social order — who had almost no formal education and who wrote the second best-selling book in the English language from a cell in Bedford county gaol, where he spent twelve years for refusing to stop preaching without a licence.

And before the prison, he had spent years in something worse. His spiritual autobiography, Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners, is a document that a modern clinician would recognize on the first page. He describes intrusive blasphemous thoughts arriving unbidden and repeatedly, thoughts he was horrified by and could not stop. He describes tormenting himself over whether he had committed an unforgivable sin, going over the same ground for months. He describes a period when a sentence would come into his mind and he would seize on it as evidence of salvation, and then another would come and he would seize on that as evidence of damnation, and this went on and on for years.

He was not writing an allegory about a condition he had heard about. He was drawing a map of a place he had lived.

Bunyan wrote Doubting Castle from a prison cell, about a dungeon he had actually been in. The reason the description is so exact is that it is not description.

Warrant · Historical · literary

John Bunyan, The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678), the Doubting Castle episode; John Bunyan, Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666).

Literary and historical warrant, which is the weakest kind in this book. Bunyan is not Scripture and an allegory proves nothing. He is here because his description of despair is unusually accurate and because he had standing to write it — not because the story settles any question. Everything doctrinal in this chapter rests on the passages cited below.

Three things Bunyan noticed that are worth having

Despair talks

The giant does not simply hold them. He comes down into the dungeon and counsels them. He gives reasons. He makes an argument, and the argument is coherent, and it takes the form of practical advice offered by someone who appears to understand the situation better than they do.

Anyone who has been in that dungeon knows this is exactly right. Despair does not arrive as a mood. It arrives as a case — a well-organized presentation of evidence, delivered in your own voice, which is what makes it so persuasive.

Giving it a name and a shape, as Bunyan does, is not childishness. It is the beginning of being able to answer it, because you cannot argue with something you have not yet distinguished from yourself. That is chapter six’s move — talking to yourself rather than listening to yourself — and Bunyan is doing it with a stage set.

Despair has weather

Bunyan puts in a small, strange detail that no allegory needed. Giant Despair, he says, has fits in sunny weather, and in those fits he loses for a time the use of his hand.

He is telling you that this thing fluctuates for reasons that have nothing to do with your spiritual performance. It is worse in the dark and better in the light and it comes in waves and its strength on Thursday is not a report on you. A seventeenth-century tinker noticed that and wrote it into the story.

Nobody escapes alone

Christian is not by himself in that dungeon. Hopeful is with him, and when Christian is at his lowest — when he is genuinely considering the giant’s advice — it is Hopeful who talks him out of it, at length, reminding him of things he already knows and cannot presently reach.

And when they pray, they pray together, and the key surfaces afterwards.

Christian did not get out alone. Hopeful was locked in with him, and kept talking, and reminded him of what he already knew and could not currently reach.

What the key actually is

Bunyan is precise about the name. Not the key of Faith, not the key of Effort, not the key of Feeling Better. The key of Promise.

Which is a specific and unglamorous thing. A promise is a commitment somebody has made, in words, that binds them regardless of the state of the person holding it. Its power is not in your grip on it. It is in the character of whoever made it.

Scripture is full of them and they are concrete. That he will never leave you nor forsake you. That nothing in all creation can separate you from his love. That the one who began a good work in you will finish it. That he will not break a bruised reed or snuff out a smouldering wick.

Warrant · Direct

Hebrews 13:5; Romans 8:38–39; Philippians 1:6; Isaiah 42:3; 2 Peter 1:4 (“precious and very great promises”); Hebrews 6:17–19 (the hope set before us as an anchor of the soul).

Each directly stated. Note the Hebrews image especially: an anchor does not hold because the sailor is strong. It holds because of what it is fixed to, and the ship rides out the weather at the end of a chain it is not gripping.

And note what that means about the key itself. It does not open the locks because Christian is holding it well, or because he has held it a long time, or because he believed hard enough on the way down the stairs. It opens them because of who cut it. His possession of it makes it available; it does not make it work.

And this is where chapter seven cashes out. Christian could use the key because he had it with him — not in the library at the House Beautiful, not somewhere he could go and look it up. In his pocket, in the dark, where no fetching was possible.

That is what memorizing a promise is for. Not devotional credit. Portability.

A promise holds you the way an anchor holds a ship — not because you are gripping it, but because of what it is fastened to.

Why he could not remember

The obvious question. If he had the key on Wednesday, why did Saturday take so long?

Bunyan does not explain, and I think the refusal is deliberate, because there is no satisfying explanation and inventing one would falsify the thing. What he does instead is report it accurately, which is more useful.

This is what the condition does. It does not take your resources away. It takes away your access to the knowledge that you have them. You can hold every relevant truth, correctly, and be entirely unable to bring one of them to hand — and then, an hour later, have it back and be unable to understand where it went.

If that has happened to you, and you concluded it meant your faith was fraudulent — because surely if you really believed it you would have been able to reach it — Bunyan is telling you no. Christian really had the key. The key really worked. He really could not think of it for four days. All of those are true at once, which is chapter four’s discipline applied to the worst week of your life.

Where this book stops

Bunyan is explicit that the giant counselled them to end their lives, and honest that Christian considered it. That honesty is why the passage has helped people for three hundred years, and it is also why I want to be plain here.

If despair has been making that case to you, the answer is not a better argument against it by yourself at two in the morning. It is Hopeful — somebody else, in there with you, doing the remembering you currently cannot. Tell your clinician. Tell one person. That is not a detour from the chapter; it is the chapter.

Case · the unreachable shelf

“C.” is forty-four and has been a believer for twenty years. She can teach the doctrine of assurance to a room. She has led studies on Romans 8.

During her episodes — which last between two and six weeks — none of it is available to her. She describes it as knowing the books are on the shelf and being unable to reach the shelf. Afterwards she can reach it again, effortlessly, and each time she concludes that the episode proved her faith was never real, because a real faith would not have gone missing.

Nobody has ever told her that this is a described phenomenon with a three-hundred-year literature, or that the man who wrote the most famous Christian allegory in English lost the key in his own pocket for four days.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The phenomenon. This chapter addresses state-dependent access to belief — the client holds a conviction stably between episodes and cannot retrieve or feel it during them. Clients frequently interpret this as evidence that the conviction was never genuine, which adds a layer of identity-level distress on top of the episode and often outlasts it.

The intervention. Normalizing it from inside the tradition, using a canonical text in which the protagonist possesses the resource throughout and cannot access it for days. This gives the client a way to hold I believe this and I currently cannot reach it simultaneously, without either being false.

Practically useful: the chapter argues for pre-loaded, memorized, portable content on precisely the grounds you would — that generative and retrieval capacity is lowest exactly when the need is highest, so material must be laid down in advance rather than sought during.

Note the companion emphasis. The escape in the story is preceded by another person doing the remembering the protagonist cannot do. That is a useful frame for a client resistant to leaning on others: in the source text, being talked through it is not weakness, it is the mechanism.

Practice

Make your keyring. Write out three promises — not encouragements, not affirmations. Actual commitments God has made, in his words. Start from the passages listed above if you like. Put them somewhere they travel with you: a card in a wallet, a note on a phone, taped inside a cupboard door.

Name your giant. Next time the case is being made to you, write down the argument in full, as an argument, in the third person. The case being put to me is… Seeing it laid out as a prosecution rather than felt as a fact is often enough to break its hold, and it is exactly what Bunyan did by giving it a costume.

Recruit Hopeful. This is the assignment, and it is the hardest one in the book. Ask one person — today — to be the one who remembers on your behalf. Tell them plainly: when I am in it I will not be able to reach this. I need you to say it to me even if I argue. Give them your three promises.

Journaling.

  1. What do you know to be true that you could not reach the last time it was bad? Write it down now, while you can, and date it.
  2. What does your giant actually say? Get the wording exact. The specific sentences matter more than the general mood.
  3. When has it had weather — when has it been lighter for reasons that had nothing to do with your faithfulness? Bunyan noticed the sunny days. Notice yours.

To bring to your counselor. “When I’m in an episode I can’t access things I believe the rest of the time, and I read that as proof I never believed them. Can we work on that specifically?”
