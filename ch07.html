Part II · Attending · Chapter Seven

Filled, Not Emptied

On the discovery that you have been meditating for years already, and that nobody told you what you were doing.

Three in the morning. You did not order this and you cannot return it.

The thought arrives fully formed — the thing you said in 2014, the worst available interpretation of a look someone gave you, the specific way it will go wrong. You try to put it down. It comes back. You try harder. It comes back with company.

And somewhere in the middle of that you have probably been told to empty your mind, which at three in the morning is roughly as useful as being told to be taller.

Why emptying does not hold

The contemplative practices DBT inherited work by subtraction. Let go. Release. Do not attach. And this genuinely helps, for a while, in the way that opening a window helps a smoky room — which is to say it moves something out and does not decide what comes in next.

The trouble is that a mind is not a container that stays empty. It is more like a field: leave it alone and it does not stay bare, it grows whatever the local seed happens to be. And your local seed is not neutral. It is whatever you have spent the last twenty years watering.

Jesus said this, in a strange little parable most people skip. An unclean spirit goes out of a man, wanders around, finds nowhere to settle, and decides to go home. And when it arrives it finds the house empty, swept, and put in order — so it fetches seven others worse than itself, and the man ends up further gone than he started. The house was not dirty. That was the problem. It was clean, tidy, and unoccupied.

Warrant · Interpretation

Matthew 12:43–45 — the spirit that returns to find the house empty, swept, and put in order.

Flagged honestly. In context Jesus is speaking about a generation that had received his ministry and remained unchanged, not delivering a lecture on mental hygiene. It is used here as an illustration of a principle established elsewhere — that the goal is occupancy, not vacancy — and that principle stands on Philippians 4:8 and Colossians 3:1–2 whether or not you accept the illustration. If it does not land for you, drop it; nothing else moves.

The house in the parable is not dirty. It is clean, swept, and empty — and that is precisely what makes it available. A mind does not have a neutral setting.

The word we have been mistranslating in our heads

Now the part that changed how I think about this, and I suspect it will change how you think about it too.

When Psalm 1 says the blessed man meditates on God’s law day and night, and when God tells Joshua to meditate on the book day and night, the Hebrew verb underneath is hāgâ. And hāgâ does not mean what we picture when we hear meditate. It does not mean sitting cross-legged, emptying, going quiet.

It means to mutter. To murmur, to growl, to say something over and over under your breath. The same verb is used of a lion growling over its prey and of a person moaning in distress. It is a low, repetitive, half-audible working-over of something.

Terms
Hāgâ
The main Old Testament word for meditation. Its root sense is muttering or growling — a sound, not a silence. To meditate biblically is to chew something over, repeatedly, half out loud, until it has gone in.
S̄îaḥ
The other main word, common in Psalm 119. It carries the sense of musing, rehearsing, going over something in speech. Same picture: turning a thing over and over rather than clearing the decks.

Sit with that for a second, because here is what it means.

Biblical meditation is not the opposite of rumination. It is rumination with different material. The mechanism is the same one your illness already runs flawlessly — the compulsive turning-over of a thing, again and again, until it has worn a groove. You are not being asked to acquire a strange new capacity. You are being asked to redirect a capacity you have been operating at industrial scale for years.

You already know how to meditate. Depression taught you, and taught you well. It just chose the material.

I find that enormously freeing, and I think anyone who has lain awake circling the same sentence for six hours will too. The instruction is not stop doing the thing your mind does. It is put something else in the groove.

Warrant · Direct

Psalm 1:2 — “his delight is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he meditates day and night.” Joshua 1:8 — the same verb, with the added instruction that the book should not depart from his mouth.

Directly stated, and note Joshua’s detail: not depart from your mouth. The meditation is spoken. That is not a metaphor for interior devotion; it is a description of a man muttering.

The direction, not the evacuation

Paul gives the same instruction in Greek and makes the direction explicit. Whatever is true, honourable, just, pure, lovely, commendable — think about these things. The verb is one used for reckoning or calculating: dwell on, take account of, let your mind work on this.

And to the Colossians: set your minds on things above. Set — a deliberate act of aiming, the way you set a course or set a table. Not empty your mind. Point it.

Warrant · Direct

Philippians 4:8; Colossians 3:1–2 — the two clearest New Testament instructions about what to do with your attention.

Both directly stated, and both are instructions to aim rather than to clear. This is the honest biblical ground for actively directing your thinking — and, as chapter six noted, it is the ground that 2 Corinthians 10:5 is so often misused to supply.

The manuals we stopped reading

Chapter one accused us of having the material and losing the method. This is the place where that accusation is least fair, because in this one area the Protestants absolutely did write the manuals. We simply stopped opening them.

In 1606 an English clergyman named Joseph Hall published a short book called The Art of Divine Meditation — a practical, step-by-step method, written in plain English for ordinary people, on how to take a passage and work it. Hall was an odd choice for the job: a combative man who spent much of his career in ecclesiastical fights and ended it having his cathedral stripped and his income sequestered. He wrote the gentlest instructional book of his century.

Thomas Watson followed with a whole treatise on meditation. Richard Baxter devoted a substantial part of The Saints’ Everlasting Rest to method — when to do it, for how long, what to do when your mind wanders, what to do when it will not start at all. These are not devotional reflections about meditation. They are instructions.

Warrant · Historical

Joseph Hall, The Art of Divine Meditation (1606); Thomas Watson, A Christian on the Mount (1657); Richard Baxter, The Saints’ Everlasting Rest (1650), Part IV.

Historical warrant. Cited to establish that a practical Protestant method for meditation exists and is old, not to make any of these men an authority. The method below is a compression of what they hold in common.

The practice

Here is the method, stripped to something you could do on a bus.

How to actually meditate on a text
  1. Take one verse. One. Not a chapter, not a reading plan. A single sentence you can hold in your mouth.
  2. Say it out loud, slowly, four or five times. This is the hāgâ part and it is not optional; saying it aloud does something that reading it does not. Mutter it if you are in public. Muttering is the original technique.
  3. Move the stress. Say it again, leaning on a different word each time. He knows our frame. He knows our frame. He knows our frame. Watch how much falls out of a sentence you thought you had already understood.
  4. Ask three questions. What does this say about God? What does it say about me? What difference would it make in the next two hours if it were true?
  5. Turn it into address. Say one sentence back to God about it. That is beat three from chapter five, and it closes the loop.

Five to ten minutes. If your mind wanders, come back — Baxter’s counsel was that wandering is not failure, it is the weather, and the practice is the returning rather than the not-leaving.

What to do with a thought you did not invite

Now the practical thing this chapter owes you, and it comes with a warning attached that you must read.

First, three facts.

One: an intrusive thought is not a sin. You did not choose it. It arrived. Scripture does not treat involuntary mental events as things to repent of, and treating them that way converts a symptom into an accusation, which is how a bad night becomes a bad decade.

Two: fighting it directly makes it stronger. This is well documented — the classic experiment asks people not to think about a white bear, and they think about almost nothing else. Suppression requires monitoring for the thing you are suppressing, and the monitoring keeps the thing in the room. This is why just stop thinking about it has never once worked for anybody.

Term
Ironic process
The finding that deliberately trying not to think of something reliably increases how often you think of it, because part of your mind must keep checking whether you are succeeding.

Three: therefore the move is not suppression but substitution. You do not evict the thought. You occupy the room. Which is exactly the shape of everything in this chapter, and exactly what Chalmers said back in chapter two about affections: nothing is ever simply removed, it is only ever displaced.

And this is where Scripture memory stops being homework and becomes something else entirely. You cannot go looking for the right verse at three in the morning; at three in the morning you have no capacity for looking. Whatever is in the room is whatever you put there in advance, on an ordinary Tuesday, when you were fine.

Memorizing Scripture is not piety with a gold star on it. It is loading the room before the night comes, because at three in the morning you will not be capable of fetching anything.

The warning — do not skip this one

Everything above assumes ordinary unwanted thoughts. If your intrusive thoughts are repetitive, distressing, and feel alien to who you are — especially blasphemous, violent, or sexual thoughts that horrify you — this chapter’s practice can be turned against you, and you need to know how.

In obsessive-compulsive patterns, seeking reassurance is not relief. It is a compulsion, and every time you perform it the loop gets stronger. A person with religious scrupulosity can use Scripture, prayer, and confession as reassurance rituals — and the more devout and sincere they are, the more efficiently the illness runs on it. Reciting a verse fifty times to make the fear go away is not meditation. It is the compulsion wearing meditation’s clothes, and it will make things worse while looking like faithfulness from every angle including the inside.

The tell is function, not content: meditation is done because something is true; a compulsion is done to make a feeling stop. Same words. Opposite machinery.

If any of that describes you, take this page to your clinician before using the practice, and say the word scrupulosity to them. It is treatable, the treatment is specific, and it is not more devotion. This is a place where a book must stop.

Case · the empty room

“P.” is forty-seven and has got very good at letting go. When a thought arrives she can release it within seconds — her group leader has used her as an example.

What she has not told anyone is that the thoughts come back at a rate of perhaps one every ninety seconds, all evening, every evening, and that she now spends four hours a night releasing things. She is not distressed by any individual thought. She is exhausted by the traffic.

P. has become extremely skilled at emptying a room that nobody is occupying. What changed things for her was not a better release technique. It was having something in there — four sentences she knew by heart, which she could not have fetched at eleven at night because she had put them in at ten in the morning.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What this chapter is doing. Substituting attentional redirection for suppression, with pre-loaded content. The mechanism will be familiar; the innovation, if it is one, is that the substituted content is rehearsed in advance and externally sourced, so it does not have to be generated during acute distress when generative capacity is lowest.

The reframe on rumination that clients tend to find useful. Biblical meditation and depressive rumination share a mechanism — repetitive working-over. Framing the task as redirecting an existing capacity rather than acquiring a foreign one lowers the perceived difficulty considerably, and it removes the shame attached to being “a ruminator.”

What to watch, and it is important. This practice is highly co-optable by OCD, particularly religious scrupulosity. Reassurance-seeking through Scripture, prayer, or confession functions as a compulsion and strengthens the loop, while presenting as devotion to everyone including the client. The chapter flags this and refers out, but the presentation is easy to miss because it looks like a client doing their homework unusually well. The discriminator is functional: is the practice performed because something is believed, or to terminate an unbearable feeling?

Practice

Load the room. Choose four sentences and learn them properly, this week, while you are well. Suggested starting set, one from each chapter so far: James 1:17, Romans 8:1, Psalm 103:14, Psalm 42:11. Say each one aloud ten times a day for a week. It will feel absurd. Do it anyway; that is what hāgâ is.

Run the method once. Take Psalm 103:14 and do the five steps above, including moving the stress. Write down what fell out of the sentence that you had not noticed.

Journaling.

  1. What is currently in the groove? Write down the sentence your mind actually returns to when it is unoccupied. Everyone has one. Most of us have never written it down and are startled by it in ink.
  2. How long have you been meditating on it? Count honestly — months, years, decades. That number is what you are up against, and it is also proof that you are extremely good at this practice.
  3. What would you rather have in there? Not what you think you should have. What you would actually want to find running when you wake at four.

To bring to your counselor. “I think I’ve been trying to empty rather than fill. Can we look at what I do in the gap?” And, if the warning box applied to you: “I want to ask you about scrupulosity.”
