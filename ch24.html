Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty-Four

The Record That Survives

On everything you built and lost, and the one account of your life that has not been misplaced.

Somewhere there are files on you.

A school kept one. Possibly a county, a court, a clinic, an employer, a landlord, a prescriber, a congregation. Every one of them was built from an account given about you, by people whose job was to reach a conclusion, and several of them were built by people with an interest in how you were understood.

Now count the records you have kept of your own.

For a lot of people the honest answer is close to none. Not because they never made any — letters, journals, photographs, plans, notebooks, the things a person accumulates to hold their own life in place. Because they were lost. And they were not lost dramatically. They were lost in a move, in an eviction, in a hospital admission, in a box left at somebody's house that was not there when you went back for it. Institutions have storage. People in trouble have carrier bags.

Every institution that ever touched you kept its file. You kept none. That is not a defect of temperament. It is what happens to people who move under duress.

Why that costs more than it sounds

Because a record is not sentiment. It is the ability to say this happened and have something outside your own head agree.

Without it you are the sole witness to your own life. And you will already have learned what happens to sole witnesses: they are asked why there is nothing to corroborate it, and the absence becomes part of the assessment. The record was lost because your life was chaotic; the chaos is then read back as the sort of thing that happens to people who are unreliable. The gap does the work of an accusation without anybody having to make one.

And underneath that is a quieter injury. The version of you that does survive — the one on file, the one that gets read — was written by people who were not curious about you. Not malicious, mostly. Just busy, and working from a form.

In My Toolbox

Related clinical material: Testimonial injustice — the specific harm of having your account discounted; and Disenfranchised grief, which covers losses nobody around you recognized as losses. A lost box of writing is one of those.

The verse about the bottle

David wrote Psalm 56 when the Philistines had seized him at Gath — in enemy hands, in a foreign city, with nobody on his side and certainly nobody writing anything down for him.

And in the middle of it he says this:

You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book? Psalm 56:8

Three images of record-keeping in one sentence, and every one of them is about the part nobody saw.

My tossings. The word is for wandering, restlessness — the turning over in the night. Not the events of his life. The sleepless hours between them, which are precisely the part no observer is ever present for. God counted those.

My tears in your bottle. A small vessel, kept deliberately. You do not collect something by accident. The image is of someone who has gone to the trouble of preserving a thing that has no value to anybody else.

Your book. Written down. Not held loosely in memory — recorded.

Warrant · Direct

Psalm 56:8 — “You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book?” (ESV)

Directly stated. The superscription places the psalm at Gath, in Philistine custody. The three record-keeping images are all in the one verse, and all three refer to unwitnessed material.

He counted the tossings. Not the events — the sleepless turnings, which are exactly the part no one was ever going to witness.

The book of remembrance

There is a second one, and I think it is stranger and better.

Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament and it is written into a discouraged period. The people are saying openly that it is useless to serve God, that the arrogant are the ones who get blessed, and that evildoers prosper and get away with it. It is not a high point.

And then this:

Then those who feared the Lord spoke with one another. The Lord paid attention and heard them, and a book of remembrance was written before him. Malachi 3:16

Stop on what got recorded.

Not their achievements. Not a list of works, or a summary of their doctrine, or anything an institution would have thought worth filing. A conversation. Some discouraged people, in the worst chapter of their national life, talking to each other — and that is the thing God had written down.

Which is the least documentable event imaginable. Nobody minutes a conversation between two people who are struggling. There is no form for it. It leaves no trace anywhere, and it was written in a book before him.

Warrant · Direct

Malachi 3:16 — “The Lord paid attention and heard them, and a book of remembrance was written before him.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and note the sequence: he paid attention, he heard, and then it was written. The recording follows the attending. What is preserved is a conversation, not an accomplishment.

What went into the book of remembrance was a conversation. Some discouraged people talking to each other in the worst chapter of the Old Testament — and that is the thing God had written down.

Engraved rather than filed

And one more, because it answers the specific fear that a record can be lost.

Isaiah is speaking to people who have concluded God has forgotten them, and the reply uses a mother and a nursing child — could she forget? Even if she did, he says, I will not. And then:

Behold, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands.

Not written on a page that can be mislaid. Not stored somewhere that can be emptied. Cut into the hands of the one doing the remembering — which is a record that cannot be separated from its keeper, and which a Christian reader has never been able to read without thinking of what was eventually done to those hands.

Warrant · Direct

Isaiah 49:15–16 — “Behold, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands.” (ESV)

Directly stated. The connection to the crucifixion is a Christian reading rather than something Isaiah states, and is offered as resonance, not as the argument. The argument is that the record is inseparable from the one keeping it.

What makes that file different

Three things, and they map exactly onto what is wrong with the others.

It is complete. The institutional files hold what was observable to someone whose job brought them into contact with you for a limited period. This one holds the tossings. It has the parts no one attended, including the ones you have never been able to prove and the ones you have never told anybody.

It is accurate. Which cuts both ways, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. It contains what was done to you, correctly named, without the smoothing that other accounts applied. It also contains what you did, correctly named, without the mitigation you would have added. It is not a defence file. It is the true one.

And the second category — the part with your name on it as the one who did the harm — is not held there pending a decision. It was settled, in full, at a cost you did not pay and could not have paid, which is the argument of the other twenty-three chapters and is the only reason a complete and accurate file is good news rather than the worst news imaginable. A total record kept by an unfavourable reader would be a nightmare. The whole difference is who is holding it.

It survives. It does not get lost in an eviction, confiscated at an intake, or routed through somebody who had a reason not to deliver it.

The only complete account of your life, the only accurate one, and the only one that cannot be lost in a move — is the one you did not write and cannot read.

Which changes what being unwitnessed means

Here is the thing this chapter exists to say, and it is the last argument in the book.

The particular loneliness of a history like this is the conviction that you are the only person who knows. Not the only person who knows about it — people know facts, versions, summaries. The only person who holds the whole thing: what happened, in order, with the parts that never made it into any account, and the weight each part actually carried.

That conviction is very nearly right. It is wrong by exactly one.

Somebody was present at all of it. Not reading a file compiled afterwards by an interested party — present, at the time, for the parts with witnesses and the parts without, holding the true version rather than the useful one.

Which is what the word known has meant in every chapter of this book. Not attended to. Not assessed. Known.

You are not the only person who knows. You are wrong about that by exactly one, and the one has been there the whole time.

Two honest limits

This does not restore what was lost. No theology gives you back a box of journals, and I am not going to suggest that the eternal record makes the temporal one unimportant. It was important. Losing it was a real loss and it is allowed to be grieved as one — chapter ten gives you the vocabulary and the permission.

And it is not a reason to stop keeping records. Documentation still matters — for treatment, for legal purposes, for the ordinary business of not having to reconstruct your own history from memory every time somebody asks. That God has an accurate account is not an argument for you having none. If anything it is the reverse: a thing worth recording in heaven is worth writing down here.

So start one. Not as evidence, and not for a hearing. Just the fact of it — because a person who has been documented only by others has never once seen their life in their own handwriting, and there is something in the doing of it that the reading of it will not supply.

Case · the sole witness

“A.” has told her story four times to four professionals and stopped telling it. Each time it came out compressed, because the appointment was fifty minutes, and each time the compressed version sounded thinner than the thing it was describing.

She has no documents. The people who could corroborate any of it are dead, estranged, or were children at the time. When she says so she hears how it sounds, and has developed a habit of conceding the doubt before anyone raises it — which, she has noticed, makes her sound less credible rather than more.

What A. has never been offered is the possibility that the thinness is a property of fifty minutes rather than of her life. She has been treating a bandwidth problem as evidence about herself.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The pattern. Clients with fragmented histories — care systems, custody, displacement, incarceration, poverty, repeated moves — are frequently extensively documented by institutions and hold nothing themselves. The asymmetry is structural rather than characterological, but it is regularly experienced as a personal failing and is sometimes read as one by services.

Clinical consequence worth naming. Where corroboration is absent, clients often pre-emptively concede uncertainty about their own history — which reads as unreliable narration and can be recorded as such, compounding the original problem. Distinguishing uncorroborated from uncertain explicitly, and doing so on the record, can be materially useful to a client who will meet this again.

What this chapter offers. A frame in which the unwitnessed material is nonetheless held and accurate, which for a religious client can reduce the pressure to prove everything in order for it to count. It is careful to say this does not replace documentation and encourages record-keeping rather than discouraging it.

Practically: supporting a client to build their own account — in writing, at their own pace, in their own order — is frequently valuable independent of any evidential use, and this chapter recommends it directly.

Practice

Write the inventory of what was lost. Not the events — the records. The journals, the letters, the photographs, the plans, the box. What was in each one, as far as you remember. This is a grief exercise, not an archive; the point is to notice that these were losses and to treat them as such rather than as untidiness.

Start one thing. A notebook, a file, anything. Not comprehensive and not chronological — start wherever it is easiest, which for most people is not the beginning. Write one page. The requirement is that it is yours and that nobody assigned it.

Read Psalm 56 whole, with the superscription. Notice where he was when he wrote it, and then read verse 8 again.

Journaling.

  1. What is the one thing you most wish somebody had seen? Write it now, in your own words, unedited and uncompressed, with no fifty-minute limit on it.
  2. What have you conceded uncertainty about that you are not actually uncertain about? There is usually something, and conceding it pre-emptively has usually cost you more than it bought.
  3. If a book of remembrance recorded a conversation between discouraged people, what conversation of yours might be in it? Most people can think of one and have never counted it as significant.

To bring to your counselor. “A lot of my history has no corroboration, and I’ve started conceding doubt about things I’m not actually in doubt about. Can we separate uncorroborated from uncertain?”

Memory · Chapter Twenty-Four

“You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book?”

Psalm 56:8 (ESV)

That is the end of the wing, and the end of the book.

Twenty-four chapters, and the argument has been one thing said in a lot of directions: that the question you have been carrying — whether you are acceptable, whether your life counts, whether the verdict is still open — was settled a long time ago by somebody else, and that almost everything else changes once it stops being live.

The main book closed at a table you did not set, with an instruction to sit down and eat. This one closes at a book you did not write, in which you appear, accurately, including the parts nobody witnessed.

You are expected at the table. You are written in the book. Neither one is waiting on your performance, and neither can be lost in a move.

So: put the ledger down, and go.
