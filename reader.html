Front Matter

Note to the Reader

Read this part first. It is short, and it will save you from expecting the wrong thing.

I am writing this from inside the problem, not above it.

That matters, because there is a whole genre of Christian writing about emotional struggle that reads as though it were composed by someone who has never had a bad week. This is not that. I have sat in the class. I have held the worksheet. I have felt the skill work and felt something underneath it not sit right, and I have not always been able to say what.

This book is my attempt to say what.

What this is

It is two things bound together. It is a short textbook — an argument about where emotions come from, why they misfire, and what actually changes a person. And it is a workbook — prompts, passages to memorize, and questions to bring to someone else.

Every chapter carries the same furniture: a piece of teaching, a boxed note showing exactly which Scripture is doing the work and how directly, a composite case to make it concrete, a sidebar written for a counselor who may not share the framework, and a practice section at the end.

What this is not

It is not treatment, and I want to be blunt about that rather than bury it in a disclaimer at the bottom of a page.

DBT was built by and for people in genuine danger. Its crisis material exists to keep somebody alive between ten at night and two in the morning, and it has a track record for doing exactly that. I am not going to write a devotional substitute for it, and if you find yourself using this book to talk yourself out of treatment, please put it down. That would be a misuse of everything in here, and the chapters themselves will keep saying so.

The line this book will not cross

Where the material approaches crisis — what to do when the distress is acute, what to do with an urge to hurt yourself — this book will stop and hand you back to your clinician. That is a design decision, not an oversight. A book cannot assess you, and it cannot sit with you at two in the morning. A person can.

If you are in immediate danger, contact your clinician now, or in the US call or text 988.

What I am not going to do to DBT

Two temptations, both easy, both wrong.

The first is to line the thing up and shoot it — to go module by module flagging pagan influence until the reader concludes the whole enterprise is poison. That would be dishonest, because a great deal of what DBT sees about human beings is just accurate, and because the people who built it were trying to save lives and largely succeeded.

The second temptation is quieter and more common among believers: to swallow the whole thing and keep faith in a separate drawer. Skills on Tuesday, Jesus on Sunday, and no traffic between them. This is comfortable and it does not hold, because sooner or later the two drawers make competing claims about who you are.

So this book does a third thing. It receives the observations gratefully, tests the assumptions underneath them, and rebuilds the whole structure on a different foundation. Same problems. Different footing.

About the citations

Two conventions, both deliberate.

On DBT: I name it, describe it, and argue with it in my own words. I do not reproduce its skills lists, its acronyms, its worksheets, or its training sequence. Those are the work of Marsha Linehan and her publishers and they are not mine to hand out. If you want the actual skills, get them from a trained clinician, which is where they belong anyway.

On Scripture: every chapter marks how hard a passage is working. Some verses say the thing directly. Some imply it. Some require an interpretive step that other faithful Christians would take differently, and where that is true I say so in the box rather than pretending the text is more settled than it is. You should be able to disagree with me and see exactly where.

Two words worth defining now
Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)
A structured treatment developed in the late 1980s, originally for people with chronic suicidal behavior and what is now called borderline personality disorder. It teaches concrete skills in four areas: paying attention, surviving distress without making it worse, managing emotional intensity, and handling relationships. It is one of the most thoroughly researched psychological treatments in existence.
Sanctification
The Christian word for the process by which a person actually becomes different — not merely forgiven, but progressively remade. This book is largely about the relationship between that word and the word skill.

Who this is for

Written first for myself, second for a friend, third for the counselor either of us might hand it to. If you are none of those and reading anyway, you are welcome here. Assume nothing is being kept from you.

One last thing. If you are reading this because you are tired — not philosophically curious, just tired — skip ahead to chapter two. The rest will keep.
