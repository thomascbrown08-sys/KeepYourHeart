Note to the Counselor

If a client handed you this, here is what you are holding and what they are probably asking for.

You do not need to share this book’s convictions to use it well. You need to know what it is doing, so you can tell the difference between a client integrating their treatment and a client quietly withdrawing from it.

What your client is doing

They are in a treatment that works and that they largely trust, and they have hit friction — not with the skills, but with the assumptions underneath them. Rather than drop out, argue in session, or split the difference by keeping faith in one compartment and therapy in another, they are trying to build an explicit bridge.

Clinically, this is a good sign. It usually means the client is engaged enough to notice the tension and committed enough to work on it instead of leaving.

What this book does and does not claim

  • It does not tell anyone to stop treatment. The opposite: every chapter that approaches crisis material stops and refers back to you, by design.
  • It does not treat DBT as an adversary. Its governing premise is that accurate observations about human beings are worth having regardless of who made them.
  • It does dispute some of DBT’s philosophical commitments — principally the contemplative sources of its mindfulness training and its framing of the client’s task as constructing a life worth living.
  • It supplies no crisis protocol. That stays with you.

Three handles that may be clinically useful

Whatever you make of the theology, these three moves have observable consequences in the room, and you may find them easier to work with once named.

1 · Worth is settled before performance

The central Christian claim this book leans on is that the client’s standing is already decided and is not contingent on progress, symptom reduction, or moral improvement. Theologically this is called justification. Functionally, it is a strong intervention against contingent self-worth — and contingent self-worth is frequently the engine underneath perfectionism, shame spirals, and the all-or-nothing collapse after a lapse.

Practically: when this client fails at a skill, the book gives them somewhere to stand that is not “I am the failure.” That is worth reinforcing even if you locate it differently than they do.

2 · Suffering with a destination

Radical acceptance asks a person to stop fighting reality. This book asks for something related but not identical: acceptance directed toward a personal God who is understood to be both sovereign and good, and who is believed to be doing something with the pain.

Clinically this tends to increase distress tolerance rather than reduce it, because endurance with meaning is cheaper than endurance without. It can also misfire — see the caution below.

3 · A vocabulary for values

Where a treatment plan asks the client to identify values and act consistently with them, this client’s values are not self-generated preferences; they are received obligations and loves. In practice, that usually makes values-based work more stable, not less, because the values do not evaporate when mood drops.

Two things to watch for

These are failure modes this book actively argues against, but they are worth your eye anyway, because the tradition it draws on has produced both.

Spiritualizing a medical problem. The idea that sufficient faith should have prevented the illness, or that symptoms are simply sin, does real damage and is theologically wrong — a point English pastors were making in print by the 1690s. Chapter 3 attacks it directly. If you hear it from this client, it is not coming from here, and you can say so.

Using theology to avoid affect. Doctrine can be deployed to skip over feeling rather than to hold it. The book’s treatment of lament exists precisely to block that exit, since the biblical material on grief is unusually permissive about complaint. If the client is reciting truths at their distress rather than feeling it, the lament chapter is the one to point them back to.

What would help

Nothing here requires you to affirm anything you do not believe. If you are willing to let the client bring this framework into the room and translate between it and the treatment model, that is the whole request. Where the two genuinely conflict, the client would rather hear you say so plainly than have it smoothed over.

If it is easier to ask

Three questions that will get you most of what you need:

  1. Which part of the skills training feels like it is asking you to believe something you do not?
  2. When you fail at a skill, what does that failure mean about you?
  3. What are you enduring this for?

This book is not treatment. It is written to be read alongside care from a qualified professional, not instead of it. If you are in immediate danger, contact your clinician or, in the US, call or text 988.
