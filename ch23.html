Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty-Three

Formed and Answerable

On holding two things that sound like they cancel, and do not.

This is the hardest chapter in the book, and it is the one most likely to be misread in both directions at once.

Here is the situation it is for. You did something wrong — genuinely wrong, with real consequences for real people, and you do not dispute it. You also know things about how you were formed that a stranger reading the file would not know, and those things are true, and they are not nothing.

And every time you have tried to hold both, one of two things has happened. Either someone heard the second part as an attempt to get out of the first — and everything you said afterward was discounted. Or you swallowed the second part entirely, said nothing, and paid for it in a way that helped nobody.

So most people in this position eventually pick one. Both choices are wrong, and this chapter is about why you are allowed to keep both.

Two questions, and they are not the same question

Culpability asks: who is answerable for this act?

Formation asks: how did a person come to be capable of it, and what would actually change him?

Notice that the second question has no bearing whatever on the first. Formation does not divide culpability, reduce it, or distribute it among the people who shaped you. It answers a different question entirely — and it happens to be the only one of the two with anything to say about whether things improve.

Culpability asks who is answerable. Formation asks how a person came to be capable of it. Two questions, and only the second one has anything to do with whether anything gets better.

They get collapsed because in ordinary speech why did you do it and whose fault is it sound like the same enquiry. They are not, and everything downstream depends on keeping them apart.

Scripture calibrates, and says so plainly

Here is the thing that surprised me, because I expected to have to argue for it and did not.

Jesus tells a parable about servants and their master's return, and then does something we almost never quote. He grades the outcomes by what each servant knew.

The servant who knew his master's will and did not do it receives a severe beating. The servant who did not know, and did wrong, receives a light one.

Sit with that for a second, because it is startling. Both are punished — the ignorance does not produce acquittal. But the sentences are different, and the difference is knowledge. And then Jesus states the principle outright: to whom much was given, much will be required.

Warrant · Direct

Luke 12:47–48 — the servant who knew and the servant who did not, beaten with many blows and with few; “Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required.” (ESV)

Directly stated. Both servants are held answerable and neither is excused. What varies is the weight, and what varies it is what they had been given. This is calibration by formation, spoken by Christ, in a judgment parable.

One honest note on the extension. In its setting, much was given refers most directly to revelation and stewardship entrusted — what a servant had been told and put in charge of — rather than to developmental advantage in the modern sense. Applying it to formation is a step beyond the immediate referent. It is a defensible step, and it does not stand alone.

Paul does something similar in Romans, where he says God shows no partiality and judges each according to what they did — and then says that those who sinned without the law will be judged apart from the law, and those under it by it. Same structure. Answerability is universal; the standard is fitted to what a person actually had.

Jesus grades by knowledge. The servant who did not know receives fewer blows — not none, fewer. Calibration is not a modern excuse somebody invented. It is in a judgment parable, in the Lord's own mouth.

And Scripture keeps culpability personal

Now the other side, at equal strength, because a chapter that only did the first half would be worthless to you and dangerous.

Ezekiel is dealing with a nation that has developed a proverb: the fathers ate sour grapes and the children's teeth are set on edge. It means we are being punished for what they did. God's response is to forbid the proverb, and then to state the rule with unusual bluntness: the soul who sins shall die. The son shall not bear the guilt of the father, nor the father the guilt of the son.

Read it in both directions, because it cuts both ways and you need both.

It means the wrong done to you was theirs. Their account, not yours. You did not inherit their guilt and you are not carrying it, however much it has felt that way.

And it means what you did is yours. Not transferable. Not partly assignable to the people who formed you, however badly they formed you.

Warrant · Direct

Ezekiel 18:20 — “The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and stated as a correction to a proverb that made guilt hereditary. The chapter as a whole is God refusing both the idea that you inherit their account and the idea that you can transfer yours.

Both, at full strength

So run chapter four's test on the pair, because this is exactly what it was built for.

A genuine paradox raises the stakes on both sides. A compromise lowers them on both.

I am fully answerable for what I did, and I was genuinely formed in a way that made it more likely. Both heavier. The responsibility is not reduced by one degree, and the formation is not waved off as an excuse. Nothing is discounted.

Now the fudge: well, given everything, it's understandable, and I've paid enough. Notice what happened. The wrong got smaller and the history got smaller, and everyone went home relieved and nothing was faced. That is not a both/and. It is an average.

Both at full strength, or you have not held them. If naming your formation made the wrong feel lighter, you were not holding a paradox. You were negotiating.

The strongest objection, granted without qualification

There is a reason institutions that deal with serious offences refuse to hear formation history, and it is not cruelty. It is this: people who have done real harm routinely deploy exactly this material to minimize, and the people they harmed are injured a second time when a proceeding turns into a biography of the person who hurt them.

That rule was written in blood. It should be granted without argument, and I grant it here completely. If you are looking for material that gets you a better outcome, or that shifts weight off you and onto the people who formed you, close this chapter. It will not help you and it is not trying to.

But notice what kind of rule it is. It is a rule about a proceeding — about what belongs in a hearing, and what a victim should not be made to sit through.

The complaint worth making is narrower and much harder to dismiss: that rule has a way of escaping the room it was written for. Carried into treatment, a provider who declines to examine formation is not protecting anybody — there is no victim in that room to protect — they are declining to do the work that would change anything. Carried into a church, the refusal contradicts the church's own doctrine, which claims the Spirit remakes people from the inside and therefore has an obvious interest in what the inside is made of.

State it in those terms every time. In that form it concedes everything that should be conceded and still stands.

The rule keeping formation out of a hearing was written in blood and should stay there. The complaint is not that it exists. It is that it escaped the courtroom and turned up in the consulting room and the pew, where there is no one left for it to protect.

The sentence you have to split

Now the guard, and it is the most important paragraph in this chapter.

There is a sentence that people in this position say, and it goes something like: I shouldn't be expected to be perfect when my formation was tampered with.

That sentence is doing two jobs at once, and only one of them is safe.

The safe job: expectations for skills you were never taught should be calibrated to someone acquiring them late, not to someone who had them and discarded them. That is legitimate, it is what Luke 12 supports, and you should say it without embarrassment.

The unsafe job: the same words can quietly mean therefore less should be required of me going forward — and that is deficit used as licence. It is a short slide from there to rationalization, and the people most vulnerable to that slide are precisely the ones with the most legitimate grievance, because they have real material to build it out of.

So say it as two sentences, always, everywhere, out loud:

I am fully answerable for what I did.
And the runway I need to build what I was never given is longer than average.

Both are true. Said together in one breath, a listener hears the second as a qualification of the first and stops listening. Said as two, they hear a person who has done the work of separating them — and that is far more persuasive than any argument you could make.

Say it as two sentences. In one, the second sounds like a qualification of the first, and the listener stops. In two, it sounds like a person who has already done the sorting — which is the only version anyone believes.

What formation actually licenses

To be exact about the boundaries, because vagueness here is what makes people distrust the whole category.

It licenses calibrated expectations. If you were never taught how to handle conflict, or money, or your own anger, then acquiring those now is a beginner's task and should be resourced like one. Being thirty years late to a skill is not the same as refusing it.

It licenses a longer runway. Change will be slower and will involve more relapse than it would for someone who is recovering a competence rather than building one from nothing.

It licenses asking for instruction rather than only prohibition. A system that only tells you what not to do cannot produce competence, and then punishes the absence of the competence it declined to build. Asking to be taught is a legitimate request and not a deflection.

It does not license reduced answerability. Not by a degree. Not now, not later.

And it does not license less safety for anyone else. Whatever conditions, supervision, or limits apply to you exist for other people's protection, and nothing in this chapter touches them. A theology of formation that resulted in fewer safeguards would have disproved itself.

The man who had everything

One more thing, because a chapter like this can leave the impression that formation explains more than it does.

David had the best formation available in his world. Anointed young. Personally taught. Given a kingdom, a prophet on staff, and — the text is emphatic about this — everything, with more offered if he had asked. There is no deficit in that history.

And he took another man's wife and had the man killed to cover it.

Formation is real and it is not determinative. It shapes what is likely; it does not settle what is possible in either direction. Which cuts against despair as hard as it cuts against excuse: if a bad formation does not doom you, a good one does not protect you, and the traffic runs both ways.

And look at how Nathan handles him, because the method is the whole point of this book. He does not open with the verdict. He tells a story about a man and a lamb, gets David to render judgment on it himself, and only then says you are the man. The confrontation is total. It arrives second.

Nathan does not lead with the verdict. He gets David to see it first, and only then names him. Being known and being judged are not opposites — and the order matters more than either.

Case · the sentence that did two jobs

“R.” has an accurate account of a bad childhood and an accurate account of what he did as an adult. He can give either one clearly.

What he cannot do is give them in the same conversation. Every time he tries, the history arrives as a clause attached to the admission — I did it, but you have to understand what I came from — and he watches the other person's face change at the word but. He has concluded from this, several times, that nobody wants to hear it, and he is half right.

What has actually happened is grammatical. The conjunction is doing the damage. The same two facts, said as two sentences with a full stop between them, land completely differently, and no one has ever told him that.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What this chapter is trying to make possible. A client who can hold developmental history and personal responsibility simultaneously, without the first functioning as mitigation-seeking and without the second requiring the first to be suppressed. Clients with offence histories often present at one pole or the other, and both poles obstruct treatment: externalizing blocks accountability work, and total self-attribution blocks formation work, which is where the change mechanisms actually are.

The theological framing is doing real structural work here. It supplies calibration-by-formation (Luke 12) and non-transferable personal responsibility (Ezekiel 18) as two claims from the same source, so the client is not choosing between competing authorities and cannot use one to defeat the other.

Watch for, and the chapter names it explicitly: the slide from skills I was never taught to less should be required of me. The recommended intervention is grammatical rather than confrontational — requiring the two claims to be stated as separate sentences. In practice this is a usable and low-conflict way to surface the slide, because a client who cannot separate them will find the exercise difficult in a way that is informative to both of you.

Also worth knowing: this chapter concedes without qualification that formation history is properly excluded from legal proceedings, and locates the complaint solely in its migration into treatment and pastoral settings. It is not arguing for mitigation.

Practice

Write the two sentences. Your own, in your own words. One states what you are answerable for, without a single qualifier attached. One states what you were not given, without reference to what you did. Full stop between them. No but, no because, no although.

Then read them aloud in that order and notice how different it feels from the version you have been carrying.

Sort a list. Take three things you struggle with. For each, ask: is this a competence I was never taught, or one I had and abandoned? The answers usually differ, and the two require completely different responses — instruction for the first, repentance for the second. Chapter three's triage applies here directly.

Ask for instruction, once. Find one thing you have only ever been prohibited from and never taught, and ask somebody to teach you. That request is legitimate. Most people in this position have never made it because it felt like an excuse in advance.

Journaling.

  1. Where have you used the history as a clause? Find an actual conversation and write out what you said. Look for the conjunction.
  2. Where have you suppressed it entirely, and what did that cost? Suppression usually shows up as exhaustion rather than as silence.
  3. What would you need to be taught, if anyone were willing? Write the list. It is worth having even if nobody ever takes it.

To bring to your counselor. “I want to work on formation without it becoming an excuse. I’m answerable for what I did and I also need to build things I was never given. Can we work on the second without touching the first?”
