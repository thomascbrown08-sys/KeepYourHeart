Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty-Five

What Was Done To You

On the word that gets used for three different things, and the damage that does.

Somebody has told you to forgive.

They may have been right. But listen to what the word carried when they said it, because it was almost certainly not one thing. Wrapped inside it were three, handed over as a single package, and you were expected to accept all three or be told you had accepted none.

Stop wanting them to pay.

Restore the relationship.

And behave as though they are now safe.

Those are three separate things. Scripture treats them as three separate things. And a great deal of the harm done to people in the name of forgiveness comes from being handed all three at once, by someone who genuinely meant well and had never taken them apart.

Forgiveness, reconciliation and trust are three different things. Most of the damage done in the name of forgiveness comes from being handed all three as one.

In My Toolbox

The clinical vocabulary: Institutional betrayal for harm by a party you depended on; Moral injury for the specific damage of betrayal by legitimate authority, which behaves differently from fear-based trauma; and Disenfranchised grief for losses nobody around you recognized as losses.

Taking the three apart

Forgiveness is releasing your claim. It is something you do, before God, and it does not require the other person to participate, agree, apologize, or still be alive. It is available to you unilaterally, which is precisely why it is commanded — nobody can be ordered to do something that depends on somebody else's cooperation.

Reconciliation is the restoration of a relationship. It takes two. It requires the person who did the harm to have turned, and it cannot be performed by the injured party alone no matter how willing they are.

Trust is a judgment about future behaviour, formed from evidence. It is earned, it can be lost, and rebuilding it takes time and demonstrated change. Nothing in Scripture requires you to hand it back on request.

Only the first is commanded of you regardless of what the other person does. The second is conditional. The third is a matter of wisdom, and Scripture treats it as such.

Forgiveness is not saying it did not matter

This is the part most people get backwards, and Paul is unusually blunt about it.

He tells the Romans not to avenge themselves — and then gives the reason, which is not that there is nothing to avenge. Leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, vengeance is mine, I will repay.

Read that carefully. Vengeance is not denied. It is reassigned. The claim is real, the debt is real, and it is being transferred to a court with better jurisdiction and better information than yours. What you are releasing is your right to collect it personally — not the fact that something is owed.

Warrant · Direct

Romans 12:19 — “Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, ‘Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord.’” (ESV)

Directly stated. Note the logic of the connective: the reason given for not avenging yourself is not that no wrong occurred, but that the case belongs to someone else. This is the same structure as the imprecatory psalms in chapter ten — the fury is fully articulated and then deposited.

Vengeance is not denied. It is reassigned. Leaving room for wrath is not pretending there was nothing to be angry about.

And Jesus is less sentimental about it than we are

The verse everyone quotes about repeated forgiveness has a first half that almost nobody quotes.

If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him.

Rebuke comes first. The sin is named, out loud, to the person who did it. And repentance appears in the sentence — which is not a loophole for withholding forgiveness, but it is certainly not the picture of forgiveness as silent absorption that most of us were handed.

Warrant · Direct

Luke 17:3–4 — “If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him, and if he sins against you seven times in the day, and turns to you seven times, saying, ‘I repent,’ you must forgive him.” (ESV)

Directly stated, including the sequence. The famous seven-times clause is about reconciliation with someone who keeps turning back, and the turning is in the text both times.

Scripture does not require you to trust anybody

This is where people get hurt, so I want to make it unambiguous.

Proverbs tells you outright that when a certain kind of person speaks graciously, you should not believe him. That is not cynicism smuggled into the canon. It is wisdom literature doing its job, and it assumes the reader will meet people whose pleasant manner is not evidence of anything.

And then there is a single verse in John that settles the question by example. After the Passover, many believed in his name when they saw the signs — and John says: Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people.

The same verb. He did not entrust himself. Not because he was bitter, not because he had failed to forgive anyone, but because he knew what people were like and made a judgment accordingly.

Warrant · Direct

John 2:24–25 — “But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people.” (ESV); with Proverbs 26:24–25.

Directly stated. Whatever forgiveness requires of you, it cannot require a level of indiscriminate trust that Christ himself did not extend.

Jesus did not entrust himself to people, because he knew what people were like. Whatever forgiveness asks of you, it does not ask you to be less discerning than Christ.

Joseph, who did it properly

The clearest working example in Scripture is a man whose brothers sold him into slavery and told their father he was dead.

Two things about how he handles them, and both matter.

He did not skip to the embrace. When they turn up in Egypt needing food, he does not immediately reveal himself. He tests them — repeatedly, over multiple visits, including engineering a situation to see whether they would abandon a younger brother the way they abandoned him. He wanted evidence of change before he restored the relationship, and Scripture records the process without a hint of disapproval.

And when he does forgive, he names the thing accurately. The famous sentence is usually quoted as a triumph over bitterness, and it is, but look at what he actually says:

As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good. Genesis 50:20

You meant evil against me. He does not say it was a misunderstanding. He does not say they had their reasons. He does not say it turned out fine so it was not really wrong. The word evil is in the same sentence as the forgiveness, and he never takes it back.

Warrant · Direct

Genesis 50:20; with the testing sequence in Genesis 42–44.

Directly stated. Joseph forgives and names the act as evil in one sentence, and the narrative gives him three chapters of due diligence before reconciliation. Both are in the text and neither is criticized.

“You meant evil against me.” He names it evil in the same sentence in which he forgives it, and he never takes the word back. That is what accurate forgiveness sounds like.

It was not an enemy

One more thing, because it is worth knowing that Scripture makes this distinction and does not consider you dramatic for feeling it.

Psalm 55 is a man describing betrayal, and he goes out of his way to say what kind it was:

For it is not an enemy who taunts me — then I could bear it… But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend. We used to take sweet counsel together. Psalm 55:12–14

Then I could bear it. He is saying explicitly that harm from a stranger is a different weight from harm by someone who owed you care. The clinical literature agrees with him, several thousand years later, and calls it betrayal trauma. The psalm got there first and put it in the songbook.

So if you have been told that you are making too much of something because worse things happen to other people — the objection does not survive contact with Psalm 55. Who did it is part of what was done.

What forgiveness actually costs

It costs you the right to collect. That is a genuine loss and you are permitted to experience it as one.

Something is owed — an acknowledgment, an apology, a correction of the record, an admission in front of the people who believed the other account. In most cases it will never be paid. Releasing the claim means giving up the possibility of ever being paid by them, and pretending that costs nothing is how people end up performing a forgiveness they have not done.

It also is not a single event. You will frequently find you have to do it again about the same thing, which is not evidence that the first time failed. It is what releasing a claim looks like when the claim keeps reappearing.

Two guards

Naming it is not the same as living in it. Hebrews warns about a root of bitterness that springs up and defiles many, and the image is agricultural and exact: a root is something that has been in the ground long enough to have a system. Naming a wrong accurately is necessary. Building a house on the site is a different decision, made later, and it is one you can make without noticing.

And this chapter is not a place to relocate your own ledger. If you have also done harm, that account is dealt with in chapter twenty-three and does not move here. Being sinned against and sinning are two separate books, both of which can be open at once, and neither one settles the other. A person who reads this chapter and comes away lighter about what they themselves did has read it wrong.

Case · the package

“S.” was told at nineteen to forgive a family member, and did — sincerely, as far as she understood the instruction. She was then expected at the same table twice a year, and to be pleasant, and to accept that raising it again would prove she had not really forgiven.

Twenty years on she describes herself as someone who cannot forgive, because the feeling never went and the dread before each visit never went. She has concluded that something is spiritually wrong with her.

What actually happened is that she did the first thing, was required to perform the second, and was told the third was obligatory. She forgave. She was then handed two more items with the same label and has spent two decades failing at tasks that were never hers.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The obstacle this addresses. Religious clients are frequently unable to engage with harm done to them because the available framework appears to require immediate release, continued contact, and restored trust as a single obligation. Attempting the whole package produces either suppression or a conviction of spiritual failure — and the client usually reports the second, not the first.

What separating the three permits. The client can meet the genuine religious obligation (releasing the claim to personal retribution) while retaining full discretion over contact and trust. In practice this frequently unlocks boundary work that was previously unavailable, because the boundary no longer registers as disobedience.

Two things worth reinforcing. First, that Scripture explicitly models withheld trust — the John 2:24 material tends to do more work with these clients than any amount of psychoeducation about boundaries. Second, that naming an act as wrong is a component of forgiveness rather than an obstacle to it; clients often believe accurate naming is what they must give up.

Watch for the chapter being used to defer forgiveness indefinitely on the grounds that trust has not been earned. The three are separable, but the first is not conditional on the other two, and the chapter says so.

Practice

Separate the package. Take one specific wrong. Write three headings — release the claim, restore the relationship, trust them again — and answer each independently. Note which ones you have been treating as a single question.

Name it accurately, once, in writing. Joseph's sentence is the model: name the act as what it was, with the right noun, and do not soften it. You are not building a case. You are declining to lie about it, which is a prerequisite for forgiving the real thing rather than a reduced version of it.

Hand it over. Say to God, out loud, that the debt is real, that you are not pretending otherwise, and that you are releasing your right to collect it. Expect to do this again. Repetition is not failure.

Journaling.

  1. What are you actually owed? Write it specifically — the apology, the admission, the correction of the record. Most people have never written it down and are surprised how precise it is.
  2. Who told you forgiveness meant restored contact? Was it ever actually said, or absorbed?
  3. Read Psalm 55:12–14. Does then I could bear it describe something you recognize? Write what it means that the difference is in Scripture.

To bring to your counselor. “I think I’ve been treating forgiveness, contact and trust as one obligation. Can we work out which of them I actually owe and which I don’t?”
