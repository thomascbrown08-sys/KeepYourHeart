Part VI · The Bruised Reed · Chapter Twenty

Searched, Not Surveilled

On the sentence that is supposed to comfort you and does the opposite.

God knows everything about you.

For a great many people that is the warmest sentence in the Christian faith. It means nobody has to be told, nothing has to be explained, and the loneliest part of being a person is answered.

And for some people it lands as a threat, and they have never said so out loud, because saying so sounds like unbelief.

If your private writing was read by someone who then used it. If openness was required of you and then produced consequences. If you were made to disclose to people who had already decided what you were — then you are completely known does not sound like intimacy. It sounds like a file. And no amount of being told it should feel different has ever made it feel different.

Chapter five raised this and deliberately left it standing. This is the answer.

What surveillance actually is

It is worth being precise, because the word gets used loosely and the precision is where the relief comes from.

Surveillance has three marks. It is covert — gathered without your knowledge or against your will. It is asymmetric — the watcher is not watched, and gives nothing back. And it is instrumental — the information exists to be used, in a decision about you, by someone with an interest in the outcome.

That third mark is the one that does the damage, and it is the one people miss when they try to comfort you. They think your problem is being seen. Your problem is what the seeing is for.

The injury is not being known. It is that everything known about you was gathered in order to be used, by someone who had already decided.

In My Toolbox

The clinical shape of this: The double bind — a demand and a prohibition issued together, with the contradiction unnameable, which is exactly what be completely open with me plus openness will be used against you produces. Also relevant: Coercive control and Testimonial injustice.

The psalm asks for it

Now go back to Psalm 139, which chapter five marked as one of this book's three cornerstones, and read what David actually does with it.

He spends the whole poem establishing that God's knowledge of him is total and inescapable. He knows when I sit and when I rise. He discerns my thoughts from far away. Before a word is on my tongue he knows it altogether. If I ascend to heaven you are there; if I make my bed in Sheol you are there. There is no exit anywhere in the psalm and David is not looking for one.

And then, at the very end, having spent twenty-two verses proving that God already knows everything, he says: search me.

That request is either redundant or it is the point of the whole psalm. It is the point. Because if the searching were extraction — information gathered for use — then inviting more of it would be insane. Nobody asks to be surveilled.

He can ask because the searching is not for anything except him.

David spends the entire psalm establishing that God already knows everything, and then asks to be searched. That request only makes sense if the searching is not extraction.

The woman who was told everything

There is one scene in the Gospels where somebody is comprehensively found out, and what happens next is not what anyone would predict.

A woman comes to a well at noon. That detail matters: water was fetched in the cool of the morning, in company. Coming at the hottest hour, alone, is what a person does who has arranged her life around not meeting anybody.

Jesus asks her for a drink, which begins a conversation, and partway through he says something that should have ended it: go, call your husband. She says she does not have one. And he tells her the truth about her history — five husbands, and the man she is with now is not one of them.

She is completely exposed, by a stranger, on the one subject she came at noon to avoid.

And here is what she does. She leaves her water jar — she came for water and forgets the water — and runs into the town she has been avoiding, and says to the people she has been avoiding: come see a man who told me all that I ever did.

That is her pitch. Not come see a prophet, not come hear his teaching. The selling point is that he knew everything. The total exposure is the good news she is running to report.

Warrant · Direct

John 4:7–19, 28–29 — the woman at the well; the disclosure of her history; “Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did.” (ESV)

Directly narrated. The noon timing and the abandoned water jar are in the text and are standard readings; the significance drawn from them is ordinary commentary, not invention.

She was told everything she had ever done and ran to tell the town about it. Being completely found out was the best thing that had happened to her, and she led with it.

Naked and exposed — and then

Hebrews has a sentence that sounds like the worst news in the Bible, and then a sentence immediately after it that changes what the first one means. Most people know the first and not the second, which is why they misremember the passage as a warning.

First: no creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account.

That is as total as it gets. Naked, exposed, and an account owed.

And the very next word is therefore.

Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens… let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace.

Read the logic, because it is the reverse of every instinct. Total exposure is stated, and the conclusion drawn from it is approach. Not hide, not brace, not explain yourself first. Draw near, and do it with confidence.

And the reason given is the one that matters here: the person you are exposed to is not a disinterested examiner. He is a high priest who has been through it — able to sympathize, tempted in every respect as we are.

Warrant · Direct

Hebrews 4:13–16 — “all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account… Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and the connective is doing the work. The passage moves from total exposure to confident approach in two sentences, and gives the reason for the move: the one who sees is the one who has been through it and does not stand outside it.

Naked and exposed — and the very next sentence says therefore draw near with confidence. The order is the entire argument.

The thing that actually changes

Here is where the relief is, and it is structural rather than emotional.

Every disclosure you have ever made to a person was a transfer of information. You had something they did not have, you handed it over, and from that moment they had something they could use. That is why disclosure is a risk. The risk is real and you were right to feel it.

Disclosure to God is not that. He is not receiving anything. The information has been fully in hand since before you had words for it, and — this is the part that matters — the verdict was already rendered on the basis of all of it, and it came out in your favour, and it came out that way for reasons that had nothing to do with what he found.

Which means what happens when you tell him is not a transfer. It is the end of a performance that was never fooling anybody.

You cannot be found out by someone who already knows. Telling him is not handing over information. It is putting down an act.

Two things this chapter is not saying

It is not saying human disclosure is safe. Chapter seventeen said the opposite and meant it: some rooms have proven they cannot hold honesty, and discernment about where to be open is wisdom rather than fear. The argument here is that God is unlike the people who taught you otherwise. That is the whole claim. It says nothing good about them.

And if this chapter does not land, that is information. If he sees everything still reads as a threat after all of the above, the problem is probably not that you have failed to understand the argument. It is more likely that the God you are picturing has acquired the face of someone else — and that is common, it is worth saying out loud to somebody, and it does not get solved by trying harder to feel differently about a verse.

Case · the file

“V.” has been a believer for eleven years and can recite Psalm 139. She quotes it to other people. She does not like it.

When she prays, she reports finding herself composing — selecting what to raise, phrasing things carefully, and moving past certain material quickly. Asked what she thinks would happen if she stopped, she said, after a long pause, that it would go in the file.

Nobody had ever asked V. what she pictured on the other end of prayer. She had assumed everyone pictured what she pictured, and that the difference between her and people who found the psalm comforting was that they had less to hide.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The presentation. A client with a history of coerced or punished disclosure may experience divine omniscience as surveillance rather than as comfort — and will usually not report this, because it reads to them as a failure of faith rather than as a symptom. It can present instead as flat or effortful prayer, or as reluctance to engage religious material that they otherwise affirm.

What this chapter does with it. It distinguishes being known from being surveilled by function — whether the information exists in order to be used against you — rather than by degree. That gives the client a way to keep the doctrine while discarding the affective template attached to it, which is the actual obstacle.

Clinically useful in its own right: the same distinction applies to therapeutic disclosure. A client who has learned that openness is instrumental will read note-taking, records, and mandated reporting through that template, and may be doing so silently. Naming the difference between information held for them and information held about them is worth doing early rather than waiting for it to surface as guardedness.

The chapter's own caution: if the reframe fails, it treats that as diagnostic rather than as resistance — usually indicating a punitive God-concept that has taken on the features of a specific person. That is worth pursuing directly.

Practice

Name the face. When you imagine being fully known by God, whose expression are you picturing? Do not answer theologically. Answer honestly, and write down the name if there is one. Most people can, and most have never been asked.

Read Psalm 139 in two halves. Verses 1–12 first: notice that David states there is no escape and does not treat this as bad news. Then 23–24: notice that after all that, he asks. Sit with the fact that the request is voluntary.

Try one uncomposed prayer. Sixty seconds, out loud, saying the thing you normally move past — on the express understanding that you are not informing him of anything. Nothing is being transferred. You are only putting something down.

Journaling.

  1. What was the first time being open cost you? What rule did you take from it?
  2. Where do you still edit before you pray? Write the specific subjects. The list is usually short and specific, not general.
  3. The woman at the well led with he told me everything I ever did. What would have to be true for that to be your best news rather than your worst?

To bring to your counselor. “I think I experience being known as being watched, including here. Can we talk about what happens to what I say in this room?”
