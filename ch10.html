Part III · Enduring · Chapter Ten

Lament

On what you are permitted to say, and to whom.

Nobody ever taught you the rule. You absorbed it anyway.

The rule is that a Christian does not complain. You can mention a difficulty as long as you attach the correction in the same breath — it’s been hard, but God is good — and the but is compulsory. Say the first half without the second and something in the room changes. You have not broken a doctrine. You have broken an etiquette, and etiquette is enforced far more reliably than doctrine.

So you learned to file the report and skip the complaint. And then you opened your Bible and found that God had published a book of them.

The largest section of the hymnbook

Roughly a third of the Psalms are laments. Depending on how you count, it is the single biggest category in the Psalter — larger than praise, larger than thanksgiving, larger than the royal psalms and the wisdom psalms together.

Sit with the implication. God gave his people a songbook for public worship, and the biggest section in it is complaints.

God wrote a hymnbook and made complaint the largest section in it. We have somehow produced a church culture in which the biggest section is the one you are not allowed to sing.

And they are not gentle. We have sanded them down by reading them in fragments on greeting cards, so let me put a few of them next to each other with nothing softening them.

How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever? Psalm 13:1
Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord? Rouse yourself! Psalm 44:23
You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness. Psalm 88:18

A man accusing God of forgetting him. A man telling God to wake up. If a member of your congregation prayed the second one out loud on a Sunday morning, somebody would take them aside afterwards. It is in the Bible. It is a song. There are performance notes at the top of it.

Warrant · Direct

Psalms 13, 44, 88 — representative laments, including direct accusation of divine forgetfulness, absence, and sleep.

Directly stated, in the canon, formatted for corporate worship. That these are prayers God supplied rather than merely tolerated is the entailment, and it is the one the whole chapter rests on: he did not have to include them.

Calvin, introducing his commentary on the Psalms, called the book an anatomy of all the parts of the soul — his point being that there is no interior condition a human being can arrive in that is not already represented, mapped, and given words. Whatever you have got, somebody has already prayed it and it was published.

The psalm that does not resolve

Most laments turn. Somewhere near the end the psalmist pivots — but I have trusted in your steadfast love — and the piece closes upward. That turn is real and it matters, and chapter six was largely about how to make it.

One does not turn.

Psalm 88 begins in trouble, stays in trouble, and ends in trouble. There is no pivot, no resolution, no lesson extracted, no upward close. The last word in the Hebrew is darkness. The psalmist says his companions have become darkness, and stops, and that is the end of the psalm.

The Holy Spirit put that in the book.

He did not attach a corrective, or a happier psalm immediately afterwards to balance it, or an editorial note explaining that the writer eventually cheered up. A prayer that ends in the dark was judged worth preserving forever in the worship material of the people of God.

Psalm 88 ends in the dark. No turn, no resolution, no lesson learned. The Spirit put it in the book anyway — which means a prayer that does not come out right is still a prayer.

If you have ever finished praying worse than you started and concluded you had done it wrong, that psalm is there for you specifically.

Lament and grumbling are not the same thing

Now the objection, and it is a fair one, because Scripture is genuinely severe about complaining. Israel grumbled in the wilderness repeatedly and it is treated as serious sin — not a minor lapse, a rebellion. Paul tells the Philippians to do everything without grumbling.

So what separates the grumbling that God judges from the lament that God publishes? They sound identical from outside. The content can be word for word the same.

The difference is direction.

Grumbling talks about God, to other people, or to nobody. It is a withdrawal: the complainer has concluded, at least functionally, that the person in charge is not listening, or not able, or not good — and has taken the grievance elsewhere. Israel in the wilderness did not petition God. They muttered to each other about Egypt.

Lament talks to God. Which means it is an act of trust rather than a withdrawal of it — and a fairly costly one, because you only bring a complaint to somebody you still believe is there, still believe can act, and still believe is worth the effort of addressing. The complaint is the evidence.

Grumbling talks about God. Lament talks to him. Same words, opposite direction — and the difference is that you do not bother complaining to someone you have given up on.

Warrant · Entailed

Numbers 14:2–4, 27; Philippians 2:14 — grumbling condemned; set against the canonical laments.

The condemnations are directly stated and so are the laments. That the distinguishing feature is the addressee is entailed — drawn from the shape of the texts rather than defined in them. It is, however, a distinction the texts consistently support: the wilderness complaints are made to one another about God, and the psalms are made to God about everything.

The shape of a lament

The psalms of lament have a form. Not a rigid one, but consistent enough that once you see it you can write one, and writing one is the practice this chapter is handing you.

The five movements
  1. Turn toward. Address him. One line. O Lord, God of my salvation. This is the smallest and most important movement, because it is what makes the whole thing a prayer rather than a spiral.
  2. Complain. Say the actual thing, unedited, at length, without the compulsory but. This is usually the longest section in the psalms and it is usually the shortest in ours.
  3. Ask. Say what you want. Concretely. Consider and answer me. Light up my eyes. Not a wish that things were generally better — a request.
  4. Turn — if it comes. Something you know about God that is true regardless of the last three movements. If it does not come today, go to Psalm 88 and stop at movement three. That is a legitimate ending.
  5. Vow or praise — again, if it comes. I will sing to the Lord, because he has dealt bountifully with me. Often future tense in the psalms, which is worth noticing: I will, not I do.

Movements four and five are frequent but not required. Do not manufacture them. A manufactured turn is the but back again in liturgical dress, and your body knows the difference even when your theology does not.

The psalms that ask God to break teeth

One more category, briefly, because if you read the Psalter honestly you will hit it and nobody will have prepared you.

Some psalms ask God to do violent things to enemies. Break their teeth. Let their children be fatherless. The one about dashing infants against rocks. They are called imprecatory psalms and they are genuinely shocking, and I am not going to pretend they are not.

Here is the one thing about them that is useful for our purposes, and I will flag immediately that how Christians should pray these is a live disagreement.

Notice what the psalmist is doing with his rage. He is not acting on it. He is handing it over. The psalm is what he does instead of the revenge — the fury is fully articulated, at full volume, and then deposited with the only judge who can be trusted with it. Paul says exactly this to the Romans: do not avenge yourselves, leave it to the wrath of God. The imprecatory psalm is that instruction performed out loud.

For a person carrying real rage about a real wrong, that is a genuinely useful thing to know: there is somewhere to put it that is neither pretending it is not there nor doing something with it you cannot undo.

Warrant · Contested

Psalms 58, 69, 109, 137; with Romans 12:19.

Genuinely contested. Faithful Christians differ about whether believers may pray these psalms as their own, pray them only against evil in the abstract, or read them exclusively as belonging to Christ. This book takes no position on that question. What is claimed here is narrower and much safer: that the psalms model the transfer of vengeance to God rather than its enactment, which is what Romans 12:19 directly commands.

Case · the compulsory but

“E.” lost her brother two years ago. She can tell you what happened in clear, well-organized sentences, and every one of them ends the same way: but he knew the Lord, but God has been faithful, but I know I’ll see him again.

All of those are true. She believes every one. She has also not cried since the funeral and cannot pray for longer than about ninety seconds before the prayer turns into a recital of things she already knows.

When her counselor asked her to say one sentence about her brother without adding anything to the end of it, she could not do it. She got about four words in and stopped, and then said — and this is the part that matters — “I don’t think I’m allowed.”

Nobody ever told E. she was not allowed. She had simply never once seen it done.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What this chapter is for. Religious clients frequently arrive with a strong prohibition on expressed negative affect toward or about their circumstances, usually absorbed from community norms rather than taught doctrine. It presents as premature cognitive reframing: the client reaches an adaptive interpretation within seconds of naming an emotion, which reads as resilience and functions as avoidance.

The lever. Telling such a client that it is acceptable to express distress often does not work, because the permission is coming from outside the authority structure they are actually answering to. This chapter supplies the permission from inside it — a third of their own Scripture is complaint, one canonical prayer ends in unresolved despair, and the tradition distinguishes lament from grumbling by addressee rather than by content or intensity.

Practically usable. The five-movement structure is a written emotional-processing exercise with a built-in permission structure and a legitimate option to end without resolution — which is unusual and, for clients who compulsively resolve, the important feature. Movements four and five are explicitly optional and the chapter warns against manufacturing them.

Watch for: a client who writes the complaint and then edits it. The editing is the material.

Practice

Write one. This is the assignment. Use the five movements. Handwrite it if you can, and do not read it back until tomorrow.

Two rules. First: no but until movement four. If you catch yourself attaching a correction to a complaint, cross it out and continue the complaint. Second: you may stop at movement three. If the turn does not come, finish there and leave it. Psalm 88 is the precedent and it outranks the etiquette.

Read Psalm 88 out loud — all of it, to the last word, without flipping to Psalm 89 to feel better. Sit for a minute afterwards. That minute is the point of the exercise.

Journaling.

  1. Where did you learn the rule? Not who taught it — nobody taught it. Where did you first notice that the room changed?
  2. Write the complaint you have never said out loud to God. Not the presentable version. The one with the accusation in it. Psalm 13 has an accusation in it.
  3. Read what you wrote and ask honestly: is it addressed to him, or about him? That is the only question that determines what kind of speech it is.

To bring to your counselor. “I add a correction to the end of every sentence about my own life. Can we work on stopping before the correction?”
