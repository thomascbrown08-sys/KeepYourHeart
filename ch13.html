Part IV · Loving · Chapter Thirteen

Ordered Affections

On the possibility that your emotions are not malfunctioning but reporting.

Everything up to here has been about surviving. This part is about what you are surviving for, and it starts with the thing you have probably spent the most energy trying to switch off.

The treatment’s account of emotion is, first of all, kind. It says an emotion is not a moral event. It is a physiological and behavioural process with a function, a timeline, and triggers — something that happens to you, arrives at a size you did not choose, and passes.

If you grew up being told that anger was sin, that anxiety was a failure of trust, and that sadness was ingratitude, hearing an adult say this is a process with a function can feel like a window opening. It takes the accusation out. That is a real gift and I want to name it before I complicate it.

Where the account runs short

Now the complication, and it comes from a strange direction: Scripture takes your emotions considerably more seriously than the clinic does.

Not more suspiciously. More seriously — as carrying real information about the shape of a life rather than being weather passing over one.

Jesus says it in nine words that function like a diagnostic instrument: where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

Read that backwards, which is how it becomes useful. If your heart is somewhere — if that is where the fear goes, where the ache goes, where you return when the room is quiet — then you have just located your treasure. You did not have to introspect for it or decide it. The emotion went there on its own, and it went there because that is where the treasure was buried.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 6:21 — “For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.” (ESV)

Directly stated. The verse asserts a reliable correspondence between what a person values and where their inner life goes — which means the direction of an emotion is evidence, and readable evidence, about what is being valued.

An emotion is not noise to be silenced. It is a reading. The only question worth asking is what it is a reading of.

Fear is the clearest instrument on the panel. Whatever you are most afraid of losing is whatever you have quietly decided you cannot live without — and that is not a psychological observation, it is a theological one, because the thing you cannot live without is functioning as your god whatever you would say if asked. Anger works the same way from the other side: it fires when something you believe you are owed has been withheld, so it will tell you, faster than any amount of self-examination, what you think the world owes you.

Part V takes this considerably further. For now, the working principle is enough: emotions point.

The guard, before we go one sentence further

I can feel exactly how this could be misused, so let me put the fence up now rather than at the end.

Everything above could be turned into: your feelings are morally significant, therefore examine every one of them for sin. That is the chapter eight disease with a new hunting ground, and it would be a disaster. So, three limits, and they are not softenings — they are the actual doctrine.

A feeling that arrives is not yet a sin. Same principle as the intrusive thought in chapter seven. Anger arriving is not the sin; what you do with it is the moral territory, and Scripture assumes as much when it says be angry and do not sin — which would be an incoherent instruction if the anger itself were the transgression.

The size is usually about your body; the direction is usually about your loves. This is the single most practically useful sentence in the chapter. A dysregulated nervous system produces emotions at the wrong magnitude — grief the size of a house over a dropped plate. That magnitude is chapter three’s infirmity, not a moral report. But where the feeling goes — what it reaches for, what it protects, what it grieves — is about what you love. Two different questions. They require two different responses.

The size of the feeling is usually about your body. The direction of the feeling is usually about your loves. Most bad counsel comes from answering one when you were asked the other.

And you cannot fix a love by inspecting it. Inspection has a real office — it identifies, and you cannot address what you have not named. What it cannot do is dislodge. Naming the thing is the beginning of the work and is routinely mistaken for the whole of it, which is why so many people can describe their disordered loves with great precision for twenty years without any of them moving. The next section is about what actually moves them.

Loves in the wrong order

Augustine had a phrase for all this: ordo amoris, the order of loves. His claim was that virtue is not a matter of loving the right things and refusing the wrong ones, because almost everything we love is genuinely good. Virtue is loving good things in the right order and to the right degree.

Term
Ordo amoris
Literally “the order of loves.” The idea that a well-formed person is not one who loves fewer things, but one whose loves are correctly ranked — so that lesser goods are loved as lesser goods, and the highest is loved highest.

Sit with how much that changes. Almost nothing that is wrecking you is a love of something evil. Your family, your work, your reputation, your competence, the good opinion of a particular person, being useful, being safe — every one of those is a genuine good, and every one of them will destroy you if you put it in the top position, because it cannot survive the weight and neither can you.

Sin is rarely loving bad things. It is mostly loving good things too much, or in the wrong order — which is why you cannot fix it by wanting less.

Warrant · Historical

Augustine, City of God XV.22 — virtue as rightly ordered love.

Historical warrant. The formulation is Augustine’s; what it is drawn from is the scriptural insistence that the first commandment is first — and that everything else is genuinely commanded, and genuinely second.

Edwards, who watched both kinds

The most careful book ever written about religious emotion was written by a man who had just watched a great deal of it and had reason to distrust it.

Jonathan Edwards presided over an extraordinary religious awakening in his New England town, and then watched a good deal of it evaporate. He saw genuine transformation and he saw theatrical counterfeits of it standing side by side, sometimes in the same person. So in 1746 he sat down to sort them.

His answer, in Religious Affections, refused both easy positions. Against the people who thought emotional intensity was the thing, he said intensity proves nothing whatever — you cannot tell a genuine work from a counterfeit by how loudly anyone wept. And against the people who wanted religion to be a matter of correct doctrine calmly held, he said something that must have startled them: that true religion consists in large part in the affections, and that a religion which never reaches them has not reached anything at all.

The distinction he drew is the one worth keeping. Affections are not the same as passions. A passion is a feeling that sweeps over you. An affection is the settled inclination of the will — what you are actually oriented toward, what you would move toward if nothing stopped you. Affections often produce feelings. They are not identical to them, which is why a person can be genuinely oriented toward God on a morning when they feel absolutely nothing.

Edwards was dismissed by his congregation after twenty-three years, went to a frontier mission town, wrote his greatest books there, was made president of Princeton, and died a few weeks after arriving. He is not a man who found emotional life simple.

Warrant · Historical

Jonathan Edwards, A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections (1746) — on the affections as central to true religion, and on the unreliability of emotional intensity as evidence.

Historical warrant. Edwards is arguing from Scripture throughout but the affection/passion distinction is his analytic framework, not a biblical category as such. It is included because it is the most useful available tool for a person who cannot presently feel what they believe.

Intensity proves nothing. A religion that never reaches the affections has not reached anything. Both true, from the same man, in the same book — which is chapter four’s discipline applied to your feelings.

How a love actually changes

So how do you reorder your loves? Not by ranking them on paper. You already know the order they should be in; the knowing has never once shifted them.

Two things do the work, and they run together.

The first is displacement, which we met in chapter two. Chalmers’ argument was that a love is never removed by an argument against it — only by a better love arriving. So the practical question is never how do I care less about my reputation. It is what would have to become vivid to me for my reputation to matter its proper amount. And “become vivid” is not something you can do to yourself by trying. It is something you put yourself in the way of — which is what all those unglamorous practices in Part II were for.

The second is that action runs backwards into affection. Scripture consistently commands behaviour that is supposed to flow from a heart you may not currently have. Put off the old self; put on the new. Put on compassion, kindness, humility, patience. Every one of those is an imperative addressed to people whose feelings were presumably as uncooperative as yours.

The clinical version of this is acting opposite to what the emotion demands, and it works. The theological version is more than behavioural, because the New Testament never says imitate a new self. It says put on the one you have been given — the language is of clothing yourself in something that is genuinely already yours, not of impersonating someone better. You are not faking a person you are not. You are practising a person you have been made.

Warrant · Direct

Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:12 — put off the old self, be renewed in the spirit of your minds, put on the new self; put on compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, patience.

Directly stated, and stated as command. Note that these are addressed to people already described as chosen and beloved — the putting on follows the standing, and never establishes it.

And the fruit language cuts the same way. The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience — fruit, which is grown rather than manufactured, on a tree, over a season, by processes largely invisible from outside. You do not strain a plum into existence. You do tend the tree.

Case · the reading nobody took

“B.” is thirty-six and has an anger problem, by which he means he sometimes says things to his wife that take a week to undo. He has worked on it seriously: he can name the emotion as it rises, he has learned to leave the room, and the frequency is genuinely down.

What nobody has asked him is what the anger is about. It fires almost exclusively when he feels he has not been given credit — for the hours, for the effort, for how hard he is trying. He has become skilled at managing a signal without ever reading it.

The signal says he believes he is owed something, and that the debt is large, and that it is overdue. That is not a regulation problem. It is a much older problem in a nearby room, and the elder brother in chapter two is standing in it.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What is being added. Standard emotion-regulation work treats affect primarily as something to be understood and modulated. This chapter adds a values-reading layer: emotions are read as evidence about what the client holds highest, with anger and fear treated as the most legible instruments.

Why it may be clinically useful. It converts affect from a problem into data without dismissing it, and it connects emotion-regulation work to values-based work in a way religious clients find natural — their values are already explicit and already ranked, so the question what does this reaction say about what I am treating as most important is one they can answer.

The distinction to hold them to, and it is the important one: magnitude is treated as physiological and non-moral, direction as values-laden. Clients who moralize their affect will try to collapse these. The chapter argues against it explicitly and you can hold the line by asking which question is on the table.

Watch for the client using values-reading as another self-examination loop — see chapter eight. The four rules there apply here unchanged.

In My Toolbox

The clinical companion to this chapter:

  • Emotional flashbacks — why a feeling arrives at the wrong size, which is the magnitude half of this chapter.
Practice

Read the instrument. Three questions, on one recent strong emotion. Do not do this on a live feeling; do it on one from last week.

  1. What was the size? Was it proportionate? If not, check sleep, food, illness, and the last month generally before drawing any conclusion. Size is chapter eleven’s territory.
  2. What was the direction? What did it reach for or protect? Name the actual object.
  3. Where does that object rank? Is it a genuine good sitting in a position too high for it? Almost always the answer is yes, and almost always the good is real.

The better yes. For whatever you found in question three: do not write down how to want it less. Write down what would have to become more vivid to you for it to find its proper size. Then write one thing you could do this week to put yourself in the way of that.

Journaling.

  1. What are you most afraid of losing? Answer fast, before the theologically correct answer arrives. The fast answer is the accurate one.
  2. What makes you angriest? Then: what does that suggest you believe you are owed?
  3. Name three good things you love. Now put them in the order you actually live them, not the order you would defend. What does the real order cost you?

To bring to your counselor. “I’ve got better at managing the reaction and I’ve never asked what it’s about. Can we look at what it keeps pointing at?”
