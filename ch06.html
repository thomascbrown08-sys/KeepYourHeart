Part II · Attending · Chapter Six

Talk to Yourself

On the discovery that you can answer back — and on what you are allowed to say.

The first time it works, it feels like a magic trick.

A thought arrives — you have ruined this, they can all tell, you should not have come — and instead of being carried off by it, you notice it as a thought and let it go past. Two seconds of daylight. It is the skill from the last chapter, and clinically it has a name.

Term
Cognitive defusion
Loosening the grip a thought has by seeing it as a thought rather than as a straight report on reality. The classic move is a change of grammar: not I am worthless but I am having the thought that I am worthless. The content is unchanged; the authority is reduced.

It works, it is worth having, and the Bible got there roughly three thousand years earlier. It also did not stop there, and the place it went next is the subject of this chapter.

A man arguing with himself, in the middle of the Bible

Psalm 42 is written by someone in serious trouble. Not metaphorical trouble. He says his tears have been his food day and night, that people keep asking him where his God has got to, and that the whole thing feels like breakers and waves going over him. He is remembering better days — how he used to go with the crowd to the house of God, with glad shouting — and the memory is not comforting him. It is making it worse. Anyone who has been depressed will recognize that specific cruelty: the good memories turning hostile.

And then, in the middle of it, he does something startling. He turns and speaks to himself in the second person.

Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.

Read the mechanics. He has split into two, exactly like the observing practice. But the half that is standing back is not taking notes. It is not describing the distress non-judgmentally and letting it pass.

It is preaching.

He puts a question to his own soul and then he tells it what to do — not what to feel, which he cannot control, but where to look, which he can.

Warrant · Direct

Psalm 42:5, 42:11, 43:5 — “Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.” (ESV)

Directly stated, in the second person, three times across the two psalms — which most scholars read as originally one composition. The self-address is not incidental to the psalm. It is its structure.

The psalmist splits in two, exactly like the skill teaches. But the half standing back is not observing. It is arguing. And the argument has content.

The Welsh doctor

The best thing ever said about this psalm was said by a physician.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones spent the 1920s in London as chief clinical assistant to one of the most eminent doctors in Britain, on a trajectory that would have made him a very successful man. He gave it up in 1927 and went to a mission hall in a struggling industrial town in south Wales, which his colleagues regarded as a spectacular waste. He never quite stopped being a diagnostician; he simply changed what he was diagnosing.

Preaching on Psalm 42 in the 1950s, he made an observation that has outlived nearly everything else he wrote. Most of your unhappiness in life, he said, comes from the fact that you are listening to yourself instead of talking to yourself. Your self talks to you every morning, before you are properly awake, dragging up yesterday and producing the whole bleak inventory. You did not ask for it and you certainly did not approve it. The psalmist’s move is to stand up in the middle of that and say: no. Now I will speak. And I am going to speak to you.

Warrant · Historical

D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones, Spiritual Depression: Its Causes and Cure (1965), ch. 1 — on Psalm 42 and the distinction between listening to yourself and talking to yourself.

Historical warrant. The distinction is his; what it is drawn from is the psalm, which is doing the thing before anyone named it.

Your self talks to you every morning without being asked, and it has not prepared kind remarks. Lloyd-Jones’ whole point is that you are permitted to interrupt.

The difference this makes

Defusion creates distance. Address creates relationship with content. That sounds like a fine distinction until it is two in the morning, and then it is the whole thing.

Distance is neutral. It gets you out from under the wave, and out from under the wave is a location, not a destination. You can stand at that distance indefinitely and nothing further happens; the thought floats off and another one arrives.

Address takes you somewhere. Hope in God is not a technique for reducing the authority of a thought. It is an instruction with a direction and an object. It says: that inventory you were just reciting is not the whole of the evidence, and here is where the rest of it is kept.

Which is why the content of what you say to yourself turns out to matter enormously — and why most self-talk advice fails precisely when you need it.

Why positive self-talk breaks on the bad days

The usual counsel is to replace the harsh sentence with a kind one. I am strong enough. I have got this. I am doing my best. And on an ordinary Wednesday that helps a little.

Here is why it fails on the day you actually need it. Every one of those sentences is a claim about you, and its truth depends on your current state — which is exactly the thing that has collapsed. On the morning you are not strong enough and demonstrably have not got this, saying it does not steady you. It adds a second problem, because now you are lying to yourself and you know it, and a person who has caught themselves lying trusts themselves less than before.

Notice what the psalmist does not say. He does not tell his soul to cheer up. He does not tell it that things are fine, or that he is coping well, or that this feeling is not warranted. He never once contradicts the distress.

He changes the subject from himself to God.

Hope in God. That sentence is true at four in the morning in exactly the way it is true at noon, because its truth does not run through you. It is not an assessment of your resources. It is a report on somebody else’s.

“I am strong enough” fails on a bad day because its truth depends on your condition. “He is faithful” does not. That is not a difference of tone. It is a difference of load-bearing.

Before the sermon, the complaint

And now the guard, because everything above can be turned into something poisonous inside about a week.

If you take “talk to yourself” and use it to shut yourself up — to stop the feeling before it has been felt, to hurry past the grief and get to the correct conclusion — you have not learned Psalm 42. You have learned to interrupt yourself with doctrine, which is a way of not being present at your own life.

Look at the order in the psalm. The tears come first. Nine verses of them. He describes his distress at length, in detail, with metaphors, to God, and nothing in the text suggests he was supposed to compress that or move it along. Then he preaches.

You do not get to skip to the sermon. If you find you always skip to the sermon, that is worth bringing to somebody, because it usually means the complaint feels forbidden — and chapter ten is going to spend its whole length demonstrating that it is not.

And when it does not work

Here is the detail I find most freeing in the whole psalm, and almost nobody points it out.

He says it three times.

Once in 42:5. Again in 42:11. A third time in 43:5. He preaches the identical sermon to his own soul three separate times — and the composition ends with him still saying it, which means at the close of the psalm he is still cast down.

There is no resolution. The Holy Spirit saw fit to preserve, in the middle of the Scriptures, a man doing the right thing three times without visible result, and did not attach a happy ending to it.

So when you do this and nothing happens, you have not failed at it. You have joined it.

He preaches to himself three times and is still downcast when the psalm ends. That is in the Bible on purpose. Doing the right thing without immediate relief is not evidence that you did it wrong.

What you are actually speaking to

One more thing, because the word soul in that psalm is doing something we usually mishear.

Term
The heart, in biblical usage
The Hebrew lêb and lêbâb — usually translated heart — does not mean the emotions as opposed to the intellect. That split is modern. In Scripture the heart is the whole inner governing centre: thinking, choosing, valuing, feeling, deciding, all together. It is where you are steered from.

Which changes how the verse this book is named for actually reads. Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life. That is not counsel about managing your feelings. It is about guarding the control room — because everything downstream comes out of it, including the feelings.

Warrant · Direct

Proverbs 4:23 — “Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life.” (ESV)

Directly stated. The verb translated keep carries the sense of guarding a post, the way a watchman guards a gate. It is an active assignment, not a mood.

A verse that is constantly misused — including at you

Someone has probably quoted this at you already, so let me deal with it honestly.

Second Corinthians 10:5 says something about taking every thought captive to obey Christ, and it gets applied to intrusive thoughts as though Paul were giving instructions for what to do at three in the morning when your brain will not stop producing horrors. Used that way it lands as an accusation: you should have captured that one, and you did not.

Read the surrounding verses and it is plainly not about that. Paul is describing his own apostolic ministry against opponents — the imagery is siege warfare, and what is being demolished is arguments and lofty opinions raised against the knowledge of God. He is talking about contending with false teaching in public, not about managing the involuntary contents of a distressed mind.

Warrant · Interpretation · commonly misapplied

2 Corinthians 10:3–5 — the passage about destroying strongholds and taking every thought captive.

Flagged because it is one of the most misapplied verses in pastoral practice. In context it is about polemical warfare against arguments opposing the knowledge of God, not about the involuntary contents of a distressed mind. There is good biblical ground for actively directing your thinking — Philippians 4:8 and Colossians 3:1–2 do that work honestly and are the subject of the next chapter. This verse is not that ground, and it should stop being used as a stick.

If someone has used it on you: an intrusive thought is not a disobedience. It is not something you chose, and Scripture does not treat involuntary mental events as sins to be repented of.

Case · the interrupter

“K.” is thirty, and her therapist has begun to notice a pattern. When asked how something felt, K. answers accurately for roughly four seconds and then corrects herself — but I know God is in control, but I shouldn’t complain, but other people have it worse. Every sentence about her life ends in a doctrinal amendment.

The therapist reads this as avoidance, which it is. K. reads it as faithfulness, which she was taught it was. Both readings are sincere and the two of them have been stuck on it for months.

What nobody has said to K. is that the psalm she is imitating spends nine verses crying before it says a single true thing about God — and that the crying is not the part you get through to reach the faith. It is nine verses of Scripture.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

What this adds to defusion. The client is being taught not only to observe a thought but to respond to it with specific propositional content. Practically this resembles cognitive restructuring more than pure defusion — but with an important difference in where the counter-evidence sits.

Why the content is chosen the way it is. Standard restructuring often generates self-referential counter-statements (I am capable, I have coped before). These are state-dependent and reliably fail during severe episodes, which can teach a client that the technique itself is unreliable. This chapter deliberately routes the counter-statement to an external, non-state-dependent referent, so its credibility does not fluctuate with mood. Whatever you make of the referent, note the structural advantage: the client is not required to believe something about themselves that is currently disconfirmed by their own experience.

What to watch. This same move is an excellent avoidance strategy, and religious clients often deploy it that way without knowing. The marker is timing: the theological statement arriving within seconds of the affect rather than after it. The chapter names this failure explicitly and insists the complaint must come first, so you can address it as an internal requirement rather than an external challenge to their faith.

Practice

Read Psalm 42 and 43 as one piece, out loud, slowly. Mark the two kinds of sentence in different colours: complaint and address. Look at how much of it is complaint. That proportion is the permission.

Build your three sentences. Not affirmations. Three sentences that are true about God, not about you, and true whether or not you slept. Write them on something you carry. Suggested test: could you say it honestly on your worst morning of the last year? If not, it is an affirmation and it will break. Replace it.

Journaling.

  1. Write down, verbatim and without softening it, what your self says to you in the first ten minutes of a bad morning. Seeing it written is often the first time it looks like a claim rather than a fact.
  2. Now write the reply — not a denial of it, and not that’s not true. The psalmist never contradicts his distress. Change the subject the way he does.
  3. Do you skip to the sermon? Look back over the last month for a moment when you told yourself the correct thing before you had felt the actual thing. What was it protecting you from?

To bring to your counselor. “I think I use true statements to cut feelings off early. Can we work out where the line is between comfort and avoidance?”
