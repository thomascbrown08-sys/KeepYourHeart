Part V · Closing · Chapter Sixteen

A Life You Did Not Build

On the goal of the whole enterprise, and the one word in it that has to change.

We should end where we started, in a seclusion room in Hartford.

The vow the seventeen-year-old made in there — that she would get herself out of hell and then go back for everyone still in it — became, forty years later, a treatment with a stated goal. Marsha Linehan named it plainly and has used the phrase ever since. The point of all of it, the skills and the modules and the worksheets, is to help a person build a life worth living.

I want to say before anything else that this is a good goal, and a serious one, and that it came from somewhere real. It is not corporate wellness language. It was formulated by somebody who had spent two years in a locked room believing her life was not worth living, and who decided that other people should not have to stay there. There is nothing cheap about it.

And it is very nearly right. Nearly enough that the last chapter of this book is about one word.

The two assumptions in the phrase

Read it again slowly and notice what it quietly assumes.

Build — the life is a construction project, and you are the builder.

Worth — the life has a value, and somebody has to assess it. In the sentence as written, that somebody is also you.

So you are the architect, the labourer, and the inspector. And I want to ask what happens to a person who holds all three jobs, because I have held them and I can tell you what it costs.

Three witnesses who saw it clearly

None of the three men in this section were writing from inside the church. That is precisely why they are worth hearing: they described the disease with great accuracy and had no particular interest in the cure this book is proposing.

Becker: everything is a monument

The anthropologist Ernest Becker won a Pulitzer for a difficult book arguing that underneath most of human behaviour runs a single unbearable terror — not primarily of dying, but of being erased; of having been small, and brief, and finally of no consequence.

His central idea was that we manage that terror by constructing what he called immortality projects — some enterprise large enough to pour a life into, so that the life demonstrably counted. A body of work. A reputation. A family. A cause. A career. We are, he thought, all engaged in purchasing a significance that will outlast us.

Now hold that against the phrase. If your project is yourself, improved — you, finally adequate; you, at last a person whose life was worth it — then every setback is not a bad week. It is the project failing. And if the project fails, you have not merely fallen short of a goal. You have failed at the thing you were doing instead of mattering.

That is why encouragement slides off. They think you are asking whether you can succeed at something. You are asking whether you are permitted to exist.

Wallace: everybody worships

In 2005 the novelist David Foster Wallace gave a commencement address that has since been read by millions, and in it he said something that should have been said from a pulpit.

In the daily grind of adult life, he told them, “there is no such thing as not worshipping; everybody worships.” His point was that worship is not a religious activity that some people opt into. It is what human beings do, constantly and mostly unconsciously, and the only genuine choice available is what.

And then the part that has stayed with me for years. Whatever you choose, he warned, if it is not God, it will eat you alive. Worship your intellect, your competence, being seen as capable and good — and you will end up feeling like a fraud, permanently braced for the moment you are found out.

Read that again as a description of the fear of not being good enough. It is exact. It is the condition of a person who has made adequacy their religion and who knows, somewhere below language, that this particular god can never be satisfied.

And the next thing, said carefully

Wallace took his own life three years after that speech, after a long illness.

I mention it with real grief and real respect, and not to score anything, because it shows us something this book has been circling since chapter one: you can see the disease perfectly and still have no cure for it. Naming your idol does not dethrone it. Knowing that your competence will consume you does not, by itself, set you free from it. The clearest sight in the world, without rescue arriving from outside, is not enough to save a person.

That is not a verdict on him. It is a verdict on insight — and it tells us what kind of help we actually need. Not a better diagnosis. Someone to come in and get us.

If any of this is close to home for you: that is not a failure of understanding on your part either, and it is not something to carry alone. Please tell your clinician this week. If you are in immediate danger, contact them now, or in the US call or text 988.

Kierkegaard: the dizziness

The man who saw furthest into this was a Christian, and a strange one. Søren Kierkegaard gave the feeling its truest name when he called anxiety the dizziness of freedom — that swimming vertigo you get standing at the edge of your own possibility, looking down into everything you might become and everything you might fail to become, with nothing to hold.

But he did not stop at the vertigo. He said where it ends. The self only comes to rest, he wrote, when it stops trying to be its own foundation and rests transparently in the Power that established it.

Which is a diagnosis of the spinning. As long as you are trying to be the ground of your own significance — author, builder, and inspector of your own life — you will spin, because you are standing on yourself and there is no floor down there.

The dizziness is the sound a self makes trying to stand on itself and finding nothing underneath.

What your fear has been telling you

So here, at last, is the full version of the principle from chapter thirteen.

Fear is a readout. What you are most afraid of losing is what you have decided you cannot live without — and whatever you cannot live without is functioning as your god, whatever you would say if somebody asked. That is not a psychological observation with a religious flavour. It is a theological fact with psychological symptoms.

And the fear is so large because the god is so small. You have laid the entire weight of your significance on something that was never built to hold it — your competence, your usefulness, your recovery, your improvement — and you can feel it buckling. The dread is not irrational. It is an accurate report on a structure that is failing.

The fear is the idol, straining under a load it was never built to bear.

The word that has to change

Which brings us back to the phrase, and to what this book has been arguing for sixteen chapters.

A life worth living is right. There is such a thing, you should want one, and it is worth working for.

Build is the word that has to go.

Because the Christian claim is not that you can construct a life valuable enough to justify your existence. It is that a life is given — at a price you had nothing to do with paying, its worth settled by someone else before you were consulted — and that it becomes yours by being received rather than achieved. That receiving has a name in the New Testament: being joined to Christ, so that what is his counts as yours. It is not a general fact about human beings. It is a gift with your name on it that has to be taken out of somebody’s hand.

Peter calls it an inheritance. That is a precisely chosen word. An inheritance is not earned, is not built, and is not proportional to the merit of the person receiving it. It is transferred because of a relationship, and usually because somebody died.

Warrant · Direct

1 Peter 1:3–5 — born again to a living hope, to an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you.

Directly stated. Note where the safekeeping is located: not in the recipient’s grip, but in the place it is being held. Your inheritance is not vulnerable to your bad weeks.

And Paul goes further, and stranger. He does not say you have been given a good life. He says your life is hidden with Christ in God — that the real thing, the actual article, is currently out of sight and secured somewhere else, and will appear when he does.

Warrant · Direct

Colossians 3:3–4 — “For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. When Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and it resolves the tension this whole book has lived in: the life is genuinely yours now and genuinely not yet visible. Already and not yet, applied to the thing you have been trying to build.

An inheritance is not something you build. It is something somebody died to leave you, held somewhere safer than your own hands.

Which changes what the work is for

Let me be careful here, because everything in this book falls over if this is heard wrong.

None of it means the work stops. You will still do the practice, still take the medication, still go to the appointment, still learn the skill, still get up on the mornings you do not want to. Chapter two settled that: effort is real, effort is not the power, and Paul joins them with the word for.

What changes is what the work is.

It stops being construction and becomes stewardship — the tending of something that was handed to you, by someone who intends to finish it. You are not building the house. You are living in it, and keeping it, and it is not going to be repossessed on the strength of a bad quarter.

And that is the difference between a setback and a collapse, which is where we came in.

Love bade me welcome

There is a poem that says all of this better than I can, and it is where this book ends.

George Herbert had every advantage. Public Orator at Cambridge, well connected, on a track that pointed straight at court and high office. He ended up instead as the parson of a tiny parish at Bemerton with a few dozen souls in it, and he died there of consumption at thirty-nine.

He never published his poems. On his deathbed he sent the manuscript to a friend with instructions: publish it if he thought it might do good to any dejected poor soul, and otherwise burn it.

He thought it was a coin toss. It is one of the great books in the language, and it exists because a dying man thought it might help one dejected poor soul.

The whole of Herbert’s Temple survives because a dying man told his friend to publish it only if it might help one dejected poor soul, and to burn it otherwise. He genuinely did not know.

The last poem before the final section is called Love (III), and it is about a soul standing in a doorway, too ashamed to come in.

Love bade me welcome. Yet my soul drew back,
  Guilty of dust and sin.
But quick-eyed Love, observing me grow slack
  From my first entrance in,
Drew nearer to me, sweetly questioning,
  If I lacked any thing.

A guest, I answered, worthy to be here:
  Love said, You shall be he.
I the unkind, ungrateful? Ah my dear,
  I cannot look on thee.
Love took my hand, and smiling did reply,
  Who made the eyes but I?

Truth Lord, but I have marred them: let my shame
  Go where it doth deserve.
And know you not, says Love, who bore the blame?
  My dear, then I will serve.
You must sit down, says Love, and taste my meat:
  So I did sit and eat. George Herbert, The Temple (1633)

Watch what happens in it, because it is the whole book compressed into eighteen lines.

The soul is invited and draws back. Not because it doubts the invitation — because it knows what it is. Guilty of dust and sin. And Love does not argue it out of the self-assessment. Love comes nearer.

Then every objection gets answered, and notice the order. I am not worthy to be here — you shall be. I cannot even look at you — who do you think made the eyes? But I have ruined them, let my shame go where it deserves — and here is the hinge of the entire poem, and of this book: and know you not who bore the blame?

The shame does go where it deserves. That is not denied. It goes somewhere, and Love is telling the soul where. This is chapter nine, in a doorway, in 1633.

And then the soul makes its last attempt, and it is the most human line in English poetry, because it is what every single one of us tries. My dear, then I will serve. Fine. If I cannot earn my way in, at least let me be useful once I am here. Let me carry something. Let me not simply receive.

And Love will not have it.

You must sit down, says Love, and taste my meat: so I did sit and eat.

You must sit down. It is the only imperative Love gives in the entire poem, and it is a command to stop working and eat.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 11:28–30 — “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest… my burden is light.” (ESV)

Directly stated. And the word translated burden here is phortion — the same word from chapter fifteen: not a crushing weight, but a proper load, a pack sized to the one carrying it. Whatever you have been under, it was probably not the one he issued.

Sit down and eat

So here is the last thing, and it is the sentence this whole book exists to say.

The motivation to change is not the price of admission to the table. It is born at the table, in the eating.

You do not clean yourself up to earn the meal. You sit, and you are fed, and the feeding is what slowly changes you. That is why chapter two insisted gratitude is the exhaust and not the fuel. That is why chapter thirteen said a love is only ever displaced by a better one. It is all the same shape, and the shape is a table you did not set.

The motivation to change is not the price of admission. It is born at the table, in the eating — which means you have to sit down before it can start.

Case · the guest at the door

“W.” has done everything asked of him. Two years of treatment, faithful attendance, homework completed, medication taken as prescribed. By every external measure he is doing well and his clinician has said so.

He does not believe it. When it is put to him that he has come a long way, he agrees politely and privately discounts it, because the improvement feels like a run of good weather rather than anything he can stand on. He is waiting to be found out. He has been waiting for about thirty years and he has never once been able to say what he is waiting to be found out as.

W. does not have a symptom problem any more. He has a standing problem. He is in the doorway with the light on and the table laid, agreeing that it looks lovely, and not sitting down.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The presentation this chapter is for. Clients who meet their treatment goals and do not experience the improvement as belonging to them — symptom reduction without any corresponding shift in self-concept. The gains are discounted as luck or timing, and the underlying question (am I acceptable) is untouched by any amount of behavioural progress, because it was never a behavioural question.

What is being proposed. That the goal of treatment as usually stated — constructing a life the client judges worth living — leaves the client as both builder and assessor, which for this presentation is precisely the unsustainable position. The chapter substitutes a received rather than constructed standing, appraised externally and in advance.

You do not have to share the metaphysics to use the structure. The clinical target is the same one you are already working: worth that is non-contingent and not self-generated. This client has a framework in which that is already available and has, for whatever reason, never applied it to themselves.

A question that tends to open it: “If you got everything on the treatment plan, what would still be unanswered?” For this presentation the answer usually arrives fast and is not about symptoms.

Practice

Name your project. Finish this without editing it into something presentable: “My life will have been worth it if I ______.” Whatever fills that blank is what you have been building, and it is almost certainly a genuinely good thing sitting in a position too high for it.

Read the fear. What are you most afraid of losing? Put it beside the sentence above. They are usually the same thing, and seeing them next to each other in your own handwriting does something an argument cannot.

Read the poem out loud. All of it, slowly, once a day for a week. Do not analyse it. Notice which line you resist — most people have one, and it is frequently then I will serve, because we would all rather be useful than fed.

Journaling.

  1. Where in your life are you the architect, the labourer, and the inspector? What would it change to give up one of those jobs? Which one could you actually give up?
  2. What would be true about you tomorrow morning if the verdict were already in? Write it in the present tense. Read it back and notice what you do not believe.
  3. What are you still offering to serve instead of sitting down? Everybody has something. Herbert’s soul had one line left in it and used the line on this.

To bring to your counselor. “I’ve met the goals and I still don’t feel like the improvement belongs to me. Can we work on that rather than on more symptoms?”

Memory · Chapter Sixteen

“Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.”

Matthew 11:28 (ESV)

“You must sit down, says Love, and taste my meat: So I did sit and eat.”

George Herbert, Love (III), 1633

That is the end of the book.

I wrote it for myself first, and for a friend, and for whoever is reading over our shoulder. If you have come the whole way through it, you already know it does not promise that the distress lifts. Psalm 88 ends in the dark, Christian lay in the dungeon for four days with the key in his pocket, and Paul asked three times and was told no.

What it promises is that none of that is happening in an empty room, and that the question you have been white-knuckling since you were young — whether you are acceptable, whether your life counts, whether you will finally be found out — was answered a long time ago, in a garden and on a hill, while you were not even looking, by somebody you had done nothing to deserve.

So put the ledger down. It was never going to come out in your favour, and it does not need to.

Sit down, and eat.
