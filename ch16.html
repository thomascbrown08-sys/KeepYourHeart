Part IV · Loving · Chapter Sixteen

Repair

On the act itself, which sits between what happens inside you and what happens inside them.

You have done it. You know you have done it. There is no confusion left about whose fault it was and no version of the story where you come out well.

And you have not gone and said so, because between knowing and saying there is a gap, and the gap is not made of ignorance. You know the words. You have drafted them in the car. What is in the gap is the fact that saying them will cost something, and that you cannot control what happens after.

This book has a chapter for what goes on inside a person who has done wrong, and a chapter for what goes on inside the person it was done to. Between them sits the act, and until now it has had no chapter at all.

In My Toolbox

Adjacent clinical material: Moral injury — which covers harm you have done as well as harm done to you, and behaves differently from fear-based trauma; and Confession and spiritual direction for the practice as a discipline rather than an event.

The altar waits

Jesus says something about this in the Sermon on the Mount that is easy to read past and very hard to live with once you have noticed it.

You are at the altar. You have brought your gift. You are, in other words, in the middle of the most important religious act available to you in that culture — and it is going well.

And you remember that your brother has something against you.

Leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift. Matthew 5:23–24

Three things in that, and each one costs.

The trigger is your memory, not their complaint. Nobody has raised it. They may never raise it. The obligation begins the moment you remember, which removes the most popular excuse there is — that they seem fine, that it has probably blown over, that raising it now would only stir things up.

Worship is interrupted. Not completed and then followed up. Left. Standing there, halfway through, with the gift on the stone.

And the order is stated. First be reconciled. Then come and offer. God, in this instruction, is prepared to wait.

Warrant · Direct

Matthew 5:23–24 — “leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift.” (ESV)

Directly stated, including the sequence and the trigger. Note that the verse describes the offerer remembering a grievance held against him — the obligation is not conditional on being confronted.

Leave the gift at the altar and go. God is prepared to wait. The person you wronged has already been waiting.

What an apology is for

Before the mechanics, the engine — because this is where most sincere apologies fail, and they fail invisibly.

An apology can be aimed at one of two things.

It can be aimed at the other person: at repairing what you damaged in them, at returning something you took, at making it possible for them to be in a room with you without managing a wound you put there.

Or it can be aimed at your own discomfort: at ending the churn, closing the open loop, getting yourself back to a state where you can think about something else.

The words can be identical. The difference is not detectable in the transcript, and it is completely detectable in the room, because the second kind carries a request the first one does not. It needs an answer. It is waiting for something. And the person you injured is now holding a bill they did not issue and cannot refuse without looking cruel.

An apology aimed at making your discomfort stop is not an apology. It is a transaction — and the person you are apologizing to can always feel which one they are in.

This is chapter two arriving at the worst possible moment. If your standing is on trial, then being forgiven is not a gift you are hoping for, it is a verdict you require, and you will go and get it from whoever is holding it. Which is how a person can perform a completely sincere apology that is nevertheless about themselves from beginning to end.

The only thing that frees an apology to be a gift is having somewhere else to stand while it is refused.

The anatomy

Four parts. The first three are mechanical and can be learned in ten minutes. The fourth is the whole thing.

The four parts
  1. Name it, with yourself as the subject. The actual act, in the active voice. “I said X.” “I did not come.” “I read your letter.” Not what happened. What you did.
  2. Attach nothing to it. No but, no because, no if. Full stop, and let the silence be uncomfortable.
  3. Repair what can be repaired, and say what will be different. Concretely. If you do not yet know how to make it different, say that instead of promising — an honest I do not know yet how to make sure this does not happen again, and I am going to find out is worth more than a confident promise you have not thought through.
  4. Stop. Do not ask for anything.

The grammar is the tell

Almost every failed apology fails at the level of syntax, which is good news, because syntax is fixable and character takes longer.

The missing subject. Mistakes were made. Things got out of hand. It came out wrong. The passive voice removes the agent. An apology with no subject in it is not an apology; it is a weather report, and both of you know it.

The conjunction. I'm sorry, but you have to understand… The but deletes everything in front of it. Chapter twenty-three makes the same point about a different pair of sentences, and the fix is identical: full stop, then a new sentence, later, if it is needed at all.

The conditional. I'm sorry if that upset you. This one is worse than saying nothing, because it puts the fact of the injury in question. It says: I am prepared to be sorry in the event that you turn out to have been hurt, which remains to be established.

The relocated object. I'm sorry you felt that way. Now the thing being apologized for is their feeling rather than your act. It sounds like an apology and is structurally an accusation.

An apology with no subject is a weather report. Sorry, but deletes everything in front of it. Sorry if disputes the injury. Sorry you felt apologizes for their reaction. Four sentences that sound like repair and are not.

The forgotten half

Modern apology is almost entirely verbal. Scripture's is heavily material, and the difference is worth sitting with.

Zacchaeus is a tax collector, which in that system means he has been extracting money from his neighbours under legal cover for years. Jesus invites himself to dinner, the crowd objects, and Zacchaeus stands up and says this:

Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold. Luke 19:8

Notice what is not in there. He never says he is sorry. Not once.

What he does instead is name a figure. Half. Fourfold. Numbers you could audit, committed to in public, in front of the people he took it from.

And fourfold is not the going rate. The law required restitution plus a fifth for this kind of fraud — fourfold was the penalty for stealing and slaughtering a man's ox, the aggravated case. He assigns himself the heavier sentence. Nobody made him.

Jesus's response is not that is very generous. It is today salvation has come to this house.

Warrant · Direct

Luke 19:8–9 — Zacchaeus names half his goods and fourfold restitution; “Today salvation has come to this house.” (ESV). With Leviticus 6:1–5 (restitution plus a fifth) and Exodus 22:1 (fourfold for the aggravated case).

Directly stated. That Zacchaeus exceeds the required restitution is an observation from the two Old Testament passages rather than something Luke comments on, but the arithmetic is not in dispute.

Zacchaeus never says sorry. He names a figure — and then assigns himself the heavier penalty. Our apology is almost all words. Scripture's is almost all restitution.

Proverbs puts both halves in one line and refuses to let either stand alone: whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy. Two verbs. Saying it, and stopping doing it. An apology that carries only the first is half an apology, and the person receiving it can count.

Warrant · Direct

Proverbs 28:13 — “Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and the pairing is the point: confession and forsaking are given as a single compound condition, not as alternatives.

Stopping

Now the fourth part, which is where sincere people fail.

You have said it. It has landed. And there is a silence, and the silence is unbearable — because the whole point of the discomfort you have been carrying was that it would end when you did this, and it has not ended, and the only person who can end it is standing right there.

So the sentence arrives almost on its own. Do you forgive me? Are we okay? Please say something.

Look at what that does. You came to return something you took. And in the last four seconds you have handed them a new job: the job of managing your feelings about having hurt them. They are now required to comfort the person who injured them, immediately, on the spot, or else be the one who withheld it.

That is a second injury, and it is delivered inside the apology, which is what makes it so hard to name. Nobody can object to it without sounding ungracious.

Asking to be told it is alright hands the person you injured one more job — the job of managing your feelings about having injured them. It is a second injury, delivered inside the apology.

So: say it, and stop. Let the silence be theirs. They may say nothing. They may say something hard. They may say something generous you do not think you have earned. All three are their business, and none of them changes whether you did the right thing.

What you do not control

Which is the freeing part, and it comes straight out of chapter twenty-five.

Forgiveness is theirs to give. Reconciliation takes two. Trust is earned over time and cannot be requested. You control the naming, the restitution, the change, and the stopping. You control nothing after that, and you were never supposed to.

Which means a good apology can be met with nothing at all and still be a good apology. If you measure it by the response, you have made the response the point, and you are back in the transaction.

Two situations where the right repair is not an apology

Where approaching would harm them again. The desire to apologize is not the same as the right to make contact. For some injuries, and some people, an approach — however well worded — puts them back in a room with you that they have worked to leave. If there are legal restrictions, no-contact conditions, or a clearly expressed wish not to hear from you, those settle it, and nothing in this chapter overrides them.

What remains available is real: confession to God, restitution routed through somebody else where that is possible, changed conduct, and telling a third party the truth about what you did rather than the version that protects you. That is not a consolation prize. It is most of the substance with the part that would cost them removed.

Where the apology has become the compulsion. If you have apologized for the same thing four times, the fifth is not for them. Repeated apology is one of the commonest forms the loop in chapter twenty-four takes, and it is exhausting to be on the receiving end of. Say it once, properly, and then live differently, which is the only version of it they can actually use.

Case · the good apology that took something

“M.” said something cruel to his sister at a family dinner, thought about it for three days, and drove over to apologize. He named the sentence he had said. He did not excuse it. He said he had been drinking and that this was a reason and not an excuse, and that he was going to do something about the drinking.

By any measure it was a good apology, and then he sat in her kitchen for forty minutes waiting. She made him tea. She told him it was fine, twice. She walked him to his car and said again that it was fine, and he drove home considerably lighter than he arrived.

She has not told anyone that she spent that evening upset and could not say so, because the person who upset her was in her kitchen needing to be reassured, and she is the sort of person who does that. The apology was real. The last forty minutes cost her more than the original remark.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The distinction this chapter is built on is between repair directed at the injured party and repair directed at the client's own distress. The behaviours are frequently identical; the discriminator is what happens at the end, where a relief-seeking apology reliably produces a bid for reassurance.

Why it matters clinically. Clients with high guilt sensitivity often apologize frequently and are surprised that it strains their relationships. Framing the reassurance-seeking as a demand placed on the injured party — rather than as excessive conscientiousness — usually lands where "you apologize too much" does not, because it identifies a cost to somebody else rather than asking the client to be easier on themselves, which they will refuse.

Directly usable: the four grammatical failure modes (missing subject, conjunction, conditional, relocated object) work as a written exercise. Having a client draft an apology and then mark which of the four it contains is fast and tends to be uncomfortable in a productive way.

Watch for apology functioning as a compulsion — repeated confession of the same act, with relief decaying on a predictable schedule. The chapter names this and refers back; it is the same mechanism as the scrupulosity material.

Note also that the chapter explicitly forecloses contact where it would harm the other party or breach conditions, and offers substantive alternatives rather than treating those situations as a dead end.

Practice

Draft one and mark it. Write an apology you owe. Then go through it and mark every instance of the four failures: passive voice, but, if, and apologizing for their feeling rather than your act. Most first drafts contain at least two.

Find the ask. Read your draft again and find the place where it requests something — reassurance, understanding, an acknowledgment that you are not that bad. It is usually in the last two sentences. Delete it. Read what is left and notice how much more exposed it feels, which is the correct feeling.

Name a figure. Where restitution is possible — money, time, a correction made to the people you said it to — write the specific amount or action rather than an intention. Zacchaeus said half and fourfold, out loud, in public.

Then check the altar test. Is there something you have remembered while reading this? Not something they have raised — something you remembered. That is the trigger the passage names.

Journaling.

  1. What are you actually afraid will happen if you say it plainly and add nothing? Write the specific fear. It is usually about what their reaction would mean about you.
  2. Think of an apology you received that did not work. What was wrong with it? Most people can identify the mechanism immediately in someone else's and not in their own.
  3. Where has apologizing become a way of keeping something open rather than closing it?

To bring to your counselor. “I think some of my apologies have been about getting relief rather than repairing anything. Can we look at what I do in the silence afterwards?”
