Glossary

Both vocabularies in one place, because this book asks you to hold them at the same time.

Terms are marked clinical or theological, and each says where in the book it does its work.

Affections theological
In Edwards’ usage, the settled inclinations of the will — what you are actually oriented toward — as distinct from passions, which are feelings that sweep over you. The distinction matters because a person can be genuinely oriented toward God on a morning they feel nothing at all. Chapter 13.
Already and not yet theological
The New Testament pattern in which what God has promised is genuinely present now and genuinely incomplete. Collapsing it in either direction produces triumphalism or despair. Chapters 4, 16.
Assurance theological
Confidence that one stands accepted by God. Distinct from salvation itself: the Westminster Confession states explicitly that assurance may be shaken, diminished, and interrupted while the thing it is confidence in is untouched. Chapter 8.
Biosocial theory clinical
DBT’s account of how severe emotional dysregulation develops: a biological predisposition toward intense, fast-arriving, slow-fading emotion, meeting an environment that repeatedly treated the person’s inner experience as wrong or excessive. Chapter 3.
Cognitive defusion clinical
Loosening a thought’s grip by seeing it as a thought rather than a report on reality. Not I am worthless but I am having the thought that I am worthless. Chapter 6.
Common grace theological
God’s goodness given broadly to everyone regardless of belief — rain, harvest, intelligence, skill, and the capacity to discover true things. It saves nobody. It is why a Christian may use a treatment developed outside the faith. Chapter 1.
Contingent self-worth clinical
The degree to which a person’s sense of their own value rises and falls with performance. Where it is high, a lapse functions as a verdict rather than as information, which reliably produces all-or-nothing collapse after small failures. Chapters 2, 14, 16.
Coram Deo theological
Latin, before the face of God. The conviction that every moment is lived in the presence of a God personally attending to it — not logged by a system, looked at by somebody. Chapter 5.
Cornerstone theological
In this book’s warrant system, a passage the argument structurally depends on. Used three times. See How Scripture Is Cited Here.
The cup theological
Settled Old Testament imagery for the wrath of God against sin, handed to the guilty and drained to the bottom. What Jesus asks to be spared in Gethsemane. Chapter 9.
Dialectic clinical
In DBT, mostly a practical stance: the refusal of false either/ors, especially the all-or-nothing ones. Not a theory that truth is relative, though the philosophical tradition it borrows from does carry such a claim. Chapter 4.
Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) clinical
A structured treatment developed by Marsha Linehan in the late 1980s, originally for chronic suicidal behaviour and borderline personality disorder. Teaches skills in attention, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and relationships. Among the most researched psychological treatments in existence. Throughout.
Expulsive power of a new affection theological
Chalmers’ argument that a love is never removed by an argument against it, only displaced by a better love. There is no heart emptied, only a heart re-occupied. The answer to a bad love is never a better no; it is a better yes. Chapters 2, 13.
The Fall theological
The entry of ruin into the world in Genesis 3, and the ultimate explanation for all human suffering. It explains why the world is broken. It does not tell you which broken thing you are looking at. Chapter 3.
Fear of man theological
Proverbs’ term for needing another person’s approval so much that you cannot be honest with them. Called a snare rather than a weakness — a trap you walk into and cannot move in. Chapter 14.
Hāgâ theological
The main Old Testament word for meditation. Its root sense is muttering or growling — a sound, not a silence. To meditate biblically is to chew something over, aloud and repeatedly, until it goes in. Chapter 7.
The heart theological
In Hebrew (lêb, lêbâb), not the emotions as opposed to the intellect — that split is modern. The whole inner governing centre: thinking, choosing, valuing, feeling, deciding. Where you are steered from. Chapter 6; the title of this book.
Immortality project clinical
Becker’s term for the enterprise a person pours a life into so that the life demonstrably counted — a career, a reputation, a cause, or one’s own improvement. Chapter 16.
Infirmity / iniquity / injury theological
This book’s triage for suffering. Iniquity is something you did — repentance. Infirmity is something you carry — treatment and endurance. Injury is something done to you — lament. Categories for reaching, not for filing. Chapter 3.
Ironic process clinical
The finding that deliberately trying not to think of something increases how often you think of it, because part of the mind must keep checking whether you are succeeding. Chapter 7.
Justification theological
God’s declaration that a person stands accepted, on the ground of Christ’s record rather than their own. A verdict, not a process. It does not improve when you do or lapse when you fail. Chapter 2.
Lament theological
Complaint addressed to God. Roughly a third of the Psalms. Distinguished from grumbling by direction rather than content: grumbling talks about God to others, lament talks to him, which makes it an exercise of trust rather than a withdrawal of it. Chapter 10.
Means of grace theological
The ordinary channels through which God works — Scripture, prayer, the sacraments, the church. The doctrine matters here because it establishes that God normally works through means, so a learned skill is not a rival to grace. Chapter 2.
Morbid introspection clinical
Self-examination that has stopped producing anything and become a closed loop, generating distress rather than information. Chapter 8.
Ordo amoris theological
Augustine’s “order of loves.” Virtue is not loving fewer things but having your loves correctly ranked. Almost nothing wrecking you is a love of something evil; it is a genuine good sitting too high. Chapter 13.
Radical acceptance clinical
Fully accepting a reality you cannot change, in order to stop the secondary suffering that fighting it produces. This book distinguishes it from surrender, which has a person on the other end and preserves the wanting. Chapter 9.
Sanctification theological
The lifelong process by which a justified person is actually made different. Unlike justification this is a process, has good weeks and bad ones, and you participate in it strenuously. Chapter 2.
Scrupulosity clinical
Obsessive moral or religious anxiety: persistent fear of unknown sin, compulsive confession or reassurance-seeking, inability to accept forgiveness as settled. Recognized, treatable, and the treatment is not more devotion. Chapters 7, 8.
Simul iustus et peccator theological
“At once righteous and a sinner.” Not partly one and partly the other, and not on a sliding scale — fully both, at once. The Reformers gave it a permanent name because it needed one. Chapter 4.
Warrant theological
In this book, the labelled box showing how much weight a cited passage is carrying. Six tiers, from Direct to Historical. See How Scripture Is Cited Here.

This book is not treatment. It is written to be read alongside care from a qualified professional, not instead of it. If you are in immediate danger, contact your clinician or, in the US, call or text 988.
