Part III · Enduring · Chapter Eleven

Bread and Sleep and Sabbath

On the treatment God prescribed first, before he said anything at all.

There is a scene in 1 Kings that I think is one of the kindest things in the Bible, and it is almost never preached, probably because nothing happens in it.

Elijah has just had the greatest day of his professional life. Mount Carmel: the prophets of Baal, the fire falling, the whole nation on its face saying the Lord, he is God. An unambiguous, public, career-defining victory.

And then a message arrives from a queen who is not impressed, and Elijah runs. A day into the wilderness. He sits down under a solitary broom tree, and he asks God to let him die. He says he has had enough, and that he is no better than his fathers.

Take that seriously for a second before we go on. This is the prophet. He has just watched fire come out of the sky in answer to his own prayer, and roughly forty-eight hours later he is alone under a bush asking to be finished. Whatever else is true about that, it is not a failure of evidence. He had all the evidence anyone has ever had.

What God does next

Here is the part.

God does not rebuke him. God does not remind him about Carmel. God does not say after everything I have done for you. And there is bad theology in the speech — Elijah says he alone is left, and he is demonstrably wrong; there are seven thousand who have not bowed. God corrects him about that eventually, on the mountain, and does not pretend the error does not matter.

He just does not do it first. The correction waits forty days behind two meals and two sleeps, and the order is not an accident.

An angel touches him and says: Arise and eat.

There is bread baked on hot stones and a jar of water. He eats. He lies down again and sleeps. Then the angel comes a second time, touches him again, and says arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you.

Two meals. Two sleeps. And then, forty days later, the conversation on the mountain — the wind and the earthquake and the fire and the low whisper.

God’s first treatment for a prophet who wanted to die was a meal and a nap. Then another meal and another nap. The theology came several weeks later.

Warrant · Direct

1 Kings 19:1–9 — Elijah under the broom tree; the angel’s twofold provision of food and sleep before any word of instruction or correction.

Directly stated, including the sequence, which is the load-bearing part. The narrative gives two full cycles of eating and sleeping before God says anything about the situation at all.

And notice the sentence the angel uses the second time: the journey is too great for you.

That is not a rebuke either. It is God agreeing with him. Elijah has just said he cannot do this any more, and heaven’s reply is: correct, you cannot, not on what you are running on. Here is more bread.

“The journey is too great for you.” That is not a correction. That is God saying yes, you are right, it is — and then handing him breakfast.

A word, because of where this chapter has been

If you have had the thought Elijah had — that you have had enough, that you would rather not continue — you are not disqualified, you are not uniquely faithless, and you are in the company of a prophet. That is genuinely what the text says.

It is also not something to carry by yourself. Please tell your clinician, or your doctor, or one person who loves you, this week. Not because the thought makes you fragile, but because Elijah did not get out of that wilderness alone either — somebody came, and fed him, and sat with him.

Why this is not a lesser kind of care

Something in us wants to file food and sleep under practical and put prayer and Scripture under spiritual, as though the first were the waiting room and the second the actual appointment.

Christianity has no such filing system, and it is one of the sharpest differences between it and nearly every rival religion in the ancient world.

God made matter and called it good, before anything went wrong. He built a rhythm of work and rest into the week and put it in the Ten Commandments, alongside prohibitions on murder. When he came himself, he came with a body, which got tired — the Gospels record him asleep in a boat in a storm, and asking a Samaritan woman for a drink because he was thirsty. He fed people. Repeatedly. When he wanted to establish that he was genuinely raised and not a ghost, his chosen demonstration was to eat a piece of fish in front of them. And what we are finally promised is not escape from the body but its resurrection.

You cannot get a religion less embarrassed about biology than this one.

The risen Christ proved he was not a ghost by eating a piece of fish. Whatever Christianity is, it is not a plan for getting free of your body.

Warrant · Entailed

Genesis 1:31 (creation called very good); Mark 4:38 (Jesus asleep in the boat); John 4:6–7 (wearied from the journey, thirsty); Luke 24:41–43 (eating the broiled fish); 1 Corinthians 15:42–44 (the body raised).

Each verse is directly stated. The conclusion drawn from them — that care of the body is therefore a spiritual act rather than a preliminary to one — is entailed, assembled from the pattern rather than stated in any one place. It is, however, a very consistent pattern.

Sabbath is a command, not a reward

Which brings us to the strangest of the Ten Commandments.

Eight of them tell you not to do things. One tells you to honour your parents. And one tells you to stop working — and it is the longest of the ten, given more words than murder, theft, and adultery.

Two reasons are given, in two different places, and both are worth having.

In Exodus, the reason is creation: God made the world in six days and rested on the seventh, so the rhythm is built into the fabric of things rather than imposed on top of it.

In Deuteronomy, the reason is rescue: remember that you were a slave in Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out. Which is to say — slaves do not get days off. You are not a slave. Stop.

Warrant · Direct

Exodus 20:8–11 (grounded in creation); Deuteronomy 5:12–15 (grounded in deliverance from slavery); Mark 2:27 — “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.”

All directly stated. Christians differ about how the fourth commandment applies now — that is a genuine secondary disagreement and this book takes no side in it. What is not in dispute is the underlying claim: that God legislated rest, and framed it as something done for people rather than extracted from them.

Notice what that does to the way most of us actually treat rest. We treat it as a wage. You may stop when the list is finished; you have earned an evening. And since the list is never finished, you never quite qualify, and you rest badly and guiltily in the gaps.

Sabbath is the opposite arrangement. It comes at a fixed point regardless of what is outstanding. It is not the reward for completion. It is a command to stop before you are done, on the grounds that you are not a slave and the work is not what you are for.

Rest as a wage means you can never afford it, because the list never finishes. Sabbath comes on a schedule instead — you stop before you are done, which is the only way anyone ever stops.

And if you have followed the argument in chapter two, you will see this is the same shape again. Rest that must be earned is the elder brother’s ledger, applied to a calendar. Rest that is commanded is grace, applied to a Tuesday.

The ordinary things, in order of unglamorousness

DBT has a whole skill devoted to reducing your physical vulnerability to emotional collapse — sleep, food, exercise, illness, substances. It is taught with a memory aid, it is the least exciting thing in the curriculum, and clinicians will tell you it is among the most reliably effective.

Everything above is why a Christian should take that seriously rather than treating it as the secular part of the programme.

But there is a failure mode on both sides here and I want to name both.

The first error is dismissing the body. Treating exhaustion as a spiritual problem, telling a person who has not slept in four days that they need more faith. Chapter three dealt with that at length.

The second error is the mirror of it — and it is the one this chapter could produce if read carelessly. It is telling someone with a serious illness that they mainly need a sandwich and a walk. Elijah’s bread did not make him not depressed; it made him able to travel. Food and sleep are the floor, not the building. If you have been doing all of this faithfully and are still drowning, that is information about your illness, not about your discipline, and it belongs with your clinician.

Bread and sleep are the floor, not the building. Get the floor right anyway, because you cannot put anything on top of a floor that is not there.

Case · the good soldier

“N.” is fifty-five, an elder at his church, and has not taken a full day off in about nine years. He would tell you this cheerfully. There is always something, and other people have real problems.

He sleeps around five hours, eats standing up, and has developed a habit of irritability that he confesses regularly and repents of sincerely and cannot shift. He has prayed about it for years. He has never once considered that a man running on five hours of sleep for a decade might be irritable for reasons that are not moral.

His pastor recently suggested he take a Sabbath seriously. N. heard it as one more thing to do well, and has begun to feel guilty about resting badly.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

The obstacle this chapter is clearing. Religious clients frequently under-comply with the physical-vulnerability components of treatment — sleep hygiene, regular eating, scheduled rest — not from carelessness but because they implicitly rank them below spiritual disciplines and above all below service to others. Rest in particular can carry a moral charge: stopping feels like selfishness.

The lever. This chapter reframes rest as commanded rather than permitted, which converts it from an indulgence the client must justify into an obligation they are already failing. For conscientious clients that reversal is unusually effective, because it recruits the same dutifulness that was previously driving the overwork.

Worth using directly: the observation that God’s recorded response to a suicidal prophet was food and sleep, twice, before any discussion. Clients who feel that attending to their body is a distraction from the real work tend to find that sequence difficult to argue with.

Watch for the client converting Sabbath into another performance metric — resting correctly, on schedule, and reporting on it. The chapter warns against this; the case study is exactly that presentation.

Practice

The Elijah audit. Before analysing anything about your spiritual state this week, answer four questions in writing: how much did you sleep, what did you eat, did you move, and are you ill or recovering. If the answers are bad, fix those first and postpone the analysis. That is the order God used.

Take four hours. Not a whole day yet — four hours, once, this week, at a fixed time chosen in advance. No work, no admin, no productive hobby that is secretly work. Choose it before the week starts so it does not have to be justified.

Journaling.

  1. What do you have to have finished before you feel allowed to stop? Write the actual condition. Then ask when it was last met.
  2. Which of the two errors is yours — dismissing the body, or expecting the body to fix everything? Most people lean hard one way and can name it in a sentence.
  3. Read 1 Kings 19:1–9 and write down what God did not say. The list is longer than the list of what he did.

To bring to your counselor. “I think I’ve been treating rest as something I have to earn. Can we set an actual schedule rather than leaving it to when I feel I’ve done enough?”
