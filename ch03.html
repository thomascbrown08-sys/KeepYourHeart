Part I · Foundations · Chapter Three

Why You Hurt

On the difference between a sin, a weakness, and a wound — and why handing someone the wrong one is not a small mistake.

Somebody has said it to you. Kindly, probably. With their whole heart.

Have you prayed about it?

Or one of its relatives. Are you in the Word? You know, joy is a choice. I wonder whether there's some unconfessed sin there. They meant it as help. What it landed as was a diagnosis, and the diagnosis was you.

And here is the part that does the damage: you could not entirely dismiss it. Because you have sinned, and you do know it, and once the suggestion is in the room you cannot get it out. So you went home and searched yourself for the sin that was causing this, and either you found one and it did not help, or you found nothing and concluded you were also blind. Either way the search made everything worse, and now the illness has a second floor: the original suffering, and on top of it the guilt of still suffering.

I want to be slow here, because this is not first of all an intellectual problem and I am not going to treat it like one. If that has happened to you, it was not a small thing and it was not your fault. Let that sit for a second before we go anywhere.

Being told your pain is a sin problem does not make you examine yourself. It makes you a defendant. And a defendant cannot rest.

What the treatment says, and why it lands

DBT’s account of where this kind of suffering comes from is called the biosocial theory, and it is worth stating properly, because when people first hear it something in them exhales.

Term
The biosocial theory
The proposal that severe emotional dysregulation develops from two things meeting: a biological predisposition toward intense, fast-arriving, slow-fading emotion, and an environment that repeatedly treated that person’s inner experience as wrong, excessive, or not really happening. Neither alone is usually enough. Together, over years, they produce someone who feels enormously and does not trust what they feel.

Notice what that account does. It says: you are not defective, you are sensitive, and something happened to you. It takes the accusation off the table.

That is not a small gift, and I am not going to be clever about it. For a person who has spent thirty years believing they were fundamentally and mysteriously broken in a way other people are not, hearing a competent adult say here is the mechanism, and it is not a character flaw can be the first honest breath in a long time.

It is also, as far as it goes, true. Scripture is not remotely embarrassed by the idea that people have bodies that fail and histories that wound them. It is, if anything, more blunt about it than we are.

Where it stops

But an explanation can be true and still be short, and this one stops in two places that matter.

The first is that it explains the mechanism and not the meaning. It can tell you with real precision how you came to be like this. It cannot tell you what it is for, or whether it is going anywhere, because those are not questions a mechanism can answer. That is not a failure of the theory. It is a boundary of the kind of thing a theory is.

The second is subtler, and it costs the sufferer something. Clinical language about an invalidating environment is deliberately non-blaming — it describes a mismatch, not a crime. That is often wise; it keeps a client from a decade of recreational resentment. But it also means that when something was actually done to you — not a mismatch, not a well-meaning parent who ran out of road, but a genuine wrong — the vocabulary has no word for it. And a person who has been wronged and has no word for it will usually do the only thing left available and turn the verdict back on themselves.

Scripture hands you the missing word without hesitation. The psalms are full of men saying plainly that what was done to them was wicked, and saying it to God, out loud, by name. It was not an enemy who did this, one of them says — it was you, my companion, my familiar friend, the man I went to the house of God with.

The Bible will let you say that was wrong without requiring you to say and therefore they are a monster. Most of us can only manage one of those at a time, which is why we end up saying neither.

Three different things wearing the same coat

So where does suffering come from? Ultimately, from the Fall — from the ruin that entered the world in Genesis 3 and has not left it. That is the Christian answer and this book holds it without flinching.

But that answer is often used badly, so let me say precisely what it does and does not settle. The Fall explains why the world is broken. It does not tell you which broken thing you are looking at. And in Scripture there are at least three, they require completely different responses, and almost all the cruelty committed by Christians in this area comes from confusing them.

Iniquity — something I did

Real sin, genuinely mine, genuinely culpable. Scripture never softens this and neither will this book. The response is repentance, and repentance is not self-flagellation; it is turning around and walking toward God, who is already coming down the road.

Infirmity — something I carry

Weakness. Frailty. A body that breaks and a mind that runs on it. This is not sin and God does not treat it as sin. He knows how we are made; he remembers that we are dust — and that verse is not a rebuke, it is the reason given for his compassion. The response here is treatment, patience, and endurance.

Warrant · Direct

Psalm 103:13–14 — “As a father shows compassion to his children, so the Lord shows compassion to those who fear him. For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust.” (ESV)

Directly stated, and note the logic: our frailty is given as the ground of God’s compassion, not as an obstacle to it. He is not disappointed to discover that you are limited. He built you that way and factored it in.

Injury — something done to me

This category gets a chapter of its own in Part VI: What Was Done To You separates forgiveness, reconciliation and trust, which are three things and are usually handed over as one.

Harm inflicted by other people, whether deliberately or through their own ruin. Not your sin. The responses are lament, honest naming, sometimes the pursuit of justice, and eventually — on a timeline nobody else gets to set for you — forgiveness.

Repentance is the right answer to iniquity and the wrong answer to infirmity. Treatment is the right answer to infirmity and no answer at all to iniquity. Handing someone the wrong one is not merely useless. It is cruel.

The guard on this frame — read it, do not skip it

These three are not three zones of the self, and it would be a serious error to use them that way — as though the body were morally neutral territory where grace does not operate and the soul were the only part God is interested in. Scripture knows nothing of that division. The whole person fell, and the whole person is being redeemed, body included, right down to a physical resurrection.

In practice the three interweave constantly. Exhaustion makes sin easier. Sin produces real physical wreckage. Being wounded by someone leaves both a memory and a nervous system. You will rarely find one of these on its own.

These categories are for reaching, not for filing. They exist to tell you which tool to pick up first — not to decide which parts of you God cares about. He cares about all of it. He came and got a body.

Jesus refuses the calculation

The disciples walk past a man blind from birth and ask the question everyone asks: whose fault is this — his, or his parents’? They assume there is a ledger and that the suffering is an entry in it.

Jesus tells them neither. Flatly. He does not soften it, qualify it, or leave the door open for a subtler version of the same accounting.

Warrant · Direct

John 9:1–3 — “It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him.” (ESV)

The most direct refutation in Scripture of the assumption that suffering is a reliable readout of the sufferer’s sin. Jesus is asked to run the calculation and declines to run it.

He does not say suffering is never connected to sin — elsewhere he clearly allows it can be. He says you cannot reason backwards from someone’s pain to their guilt. And it is worth noticing that the entire book of Job is forty chapters of men doing exactly that reasoning, at length, with real eloquence, and being told at the end that they had not spoken rightly.

Job’s friends were not stupid and they were not cruel. They were systematic. They had a theology of suffering that ran clean end to end, and it was wrong, and it took them seven days of silence and forty chapters to find out.

The pastors who already fought this war

If it helps to know that this is not a modern accommodation to psychiatry: English pastors were making this argument in print more than three hundred years ago, and they were not gentle about it.

Timothy Rogers was a Nonconformist minister who lost something like two years of his life and ministry to what his century called melancholy. He could not work. He could not, for long stretches, believe that God loved him, though he could still have explained the doctrine correctly to you. When he eventually came out of it, he wrote a book about it, and a good deal of that book is Rogers turning around and speaking directly to the friends and pastors of sufferers — essentially telling them to stop. Stop telling them to cheer up. Stop treating the disease as the fault. Stop demanding they produce evidence of spiritual improvement while they are drowning.

Richard Baxter, who was chronically ill for most of his long life and knew the territory from the inside, went further and wrote a treatise on melancholy with a title that gives away the whole argument: it proposed to treat the thing by faith and physic — that is, by prayer and by medicine, together, without apology. Baxter also gave a piece of counsel that ought to be startling coming from a Puritan: he told melancholy sufferers to spend less time alone in self-examination, not more. He had noticed that for a certain kind of sick person, introspection is not a cure. It is a symptom.

Warrant · Historical

Timothy Rogers, A Discourse Concerning Trouble of Mind and the Disease of Melancholy (1691); Richard Baxter, The Cure of Melancholy and Overmuch Sorrow, by Faith and Physic; William Bridge, A Lifting Up for the Downcast (1648).

Historical warrant: this is the settled pastoral judgment of a serious strand of the Protestant tradition, not a claim Scripture states in these words. What it establishes is that treating depression as a medical condition requiring treatment is not a concession the church made to modern psychology. It is a position the church held before modern psychology existed.

The teaching that says faith should have fixed it

There is a strand of teaching — you have probably met it — which holds that sufficient faith would have lifted the affliction by now, and that its persistence is therefore a report on your believing.

I want to answer it with a person rather than an argument.

Paul had something. He never says what. He calls it a thorn in the flesh, and he asked the Lord three times to take it away, which is the prayer of a man who is not being casual. The answer was no. Not not yet. No — along with a reason: that grace was sufficient, and that power is perfected in weakness.

So the apostle who wrote a third of the New Testament, who saw the risen Christ, who had faith by anyone’s measure, prayed specifically and repeatedly for relief and did not get it. Whatever your unrelieved affliction proves about you, it cannot prove what that teaching says it proves, because it would have to prove the same thing about Paul.

Warrant · Contested

2 Corinthians 12:7–9 — the thorn in the flesh.

Marked contested deliberately. What the thorn was is genuinely unknown and faithful interpreters have argued for illness, eye trouble, a persecutor, and a besetting temptation. Nobody knows. The point being drawn here does not depend on knowing: whatever it was, it was unrelieved, it was prayed about seriously, and the answer was no. If someone tells you confidently what the thorn was, they are telling you about their commentary, not about the text.

Where this is going — but not yet

Christianity does say something more, and I am going to say it carefully, because said too fast it becomes exactly the thing this chapter has spent its whole length attacking.

Paul writes that the whole creation was subjected to futility — not by its own choice — and that it groans, and that we groan along with it while we wait. Then he says the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed.

Read the order. He does not skip the groaning to get to the glory. He puts the groaning in the text, twice, and gives it the word futility — which is not a comforting word and was not meant to be. The hope is real and it is not offered as a reason to stop groaning.

Warrant · Direct

Romans 8:18–25 — creation subjected to futility, groaning together, awaiting the redemption of our bodies.

Directly stated. Note especially that what is awaited is the redemption of bodies — the Christian hope is not escape from the physical but its repair, which is why nothing in this book can treat your body as the unimportant part.

One distinction this chapter must not flatten

I have spent this whole chapter attacking the idea that your suffering is a penalty being extracted from you, and I stand by every word. But there is a neighbouring idea that is not the same thing and that Scripture plainly teaches, and if I let the first argument swallow the second I will have traded one error for another.

Hebrews says the Lord disciplines those he loves, and treats us as sons. That is real, and it is worth holding precisely because of how carefully the passage frames it — as the mark of belonging, not the mark of disfavour. Note three things about it, because all three get lost.

  • Discipline is not punishment. The punishment was borne elsewhere, once, by someone else. What remains is not a court extracting payment. It is a father training a child he has already claimed.
  • Discipline is formative, not retributive. It looks forward to what you are becoming rather than back at what you did. Hebrews says as much: it is for our good, that we may share his holiness.
  • You cannot use it as a diagnostic. This is the crucial one. That God sometimes disciplines does not license you — or anyone else — to read backwards from a hard season to a specific fault. That is exactly the calculation Jesus refused in John 9 and exactly the reasoning Job’s friends were condemned for. Discipline is something you may recognize afterwards, in hindsight, with gratitude. It is not a tool for auditing yourself on a bad Tuesday.
Warrant · Direct

Hebrews 12:5–11 — the Lord disciplines the one he loves; he treats us as sons; it is for our good.

Directly stated, and stated as evidence of belonging. The passage’s argument is not “this hurts, so examine yourself” but “this is how fathers treat sons, so do not conclude you have been disowned.”

Punishment asks what you owe. Discipline asks what you are becoming. The first was settled at the cross and is not being re-litigated in your living room.

So: your suffering is not meaningless. It is not a penalty being extracted from you. It may or may not be forming you in ways you will only see later, and you are not in a position to audit which. And it is not going to be explained to you on demand. All of that can be true at once. The Christian claim is not that you will be told why. It is that there is a who — that the futility is not the last word, that somebody is underwriting the whole thing, and that he is not indifferent, because he has been in here with us and has the scars to establish it.

A mechanism can tell you how you got here. Only a person can tell you it is going somewhere. And you cannot get that second thing from a better mechanism, no matter how good it gets.

Case · the second floor

“M.” is forty-one and has had depressive episodes since she was nineteen. She loves her church. When the current episode started she told her small group, and they were kind, and they prayed, and over the following weeks several people asked — gently, always gently — whether there was anything she needed to bring into the light.

So she looked. She found the ordinary sins any honest person finds, confessed them, and did not improve, which she took as evidence that she had not looked hard enough. Six weeks later she is doing four hours a week of increasingly frantic self-examination and has stopped telling anyone how she is, because the reporting has become an audit and she is failing it.

Nobody in this story did anything malicious. They gave her a tool. It was the wrong tool, and she used it as hard as she could, on a wound it could not reach.

Composite. Not a real person.

For a counselor

This chapter exists mainly to dismantle a specific harm you may be working against already: religiously framed self-blame, where the client reads symptom persistence as spiritual failure and responds by escalating self-scrutiny. The escalation is not irrational within the client’s framework, which is why arguing them out of it from outside the framework rarely works.

What this chapter does is supply an internal counter-argument — Jesus explicitly refusing the suffering-equals-guilt calculation, Job’s comforters condemned for exactly that reasoning, Paul praying for relief and being refused, and a documented Protestant pastoral tradition insisting since the 1600s that melancholy is an illness requiring treatment. If you need to challenge the belief, it is more effective coming from inside than outside.

A phrase that may be useful: the client can hold “this is not a punishment” and “I do not know why” at the same time. Certainty about the second is not required to keep the first.

Practice

Journaling — the triage. Take a single specific episode from the last month, not your life in general.

  1. Write down what actually happened, plainly, with no interpretation and no adjectives about yourself.
  2. Now sort it into the three, and expect it to be more than one: iniquity (what here, if anything, was genuinely my sin?), infirmity (what here was my body, my sleep, my illness, my limits?), injury (what here was done to me?).
  3. Look at the sizes of the three piles. Most of us have one pile we throw everything into. Which is yours — and what has that habit been costing?
  4. For the iniquity pile only: confess it, receive that it is dealt with, and close it. Do not reopen it tomorrow. If you cannot stop reopening it, that is important and belongs in the next session.

To bring to your counselor. “I think I have been treating a health problem as a moral failure. Can we look at what I do after a bad week?”

To bring to your pastor or a trusted friend, if you can. “When I tell you I’m struggling, I need you to hear it as an illness and not as a confession.”
