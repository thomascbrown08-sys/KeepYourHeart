Part II · Attending · Chapter Eight

Examined but Not Absorbed

On the difference between looking inward and drowning there.

Everything in Part II has pointed here, because everything in Part II can be turned into a weapon and pointed at you.

Notice, name, address. Talk back to yourself. Chew the verse. Every one of those is an inward-facing act, and there is a kind of person for whom inward-facing acts are the last thing on earth they need more of — who has been examining themselves for twenty years with the thoroughness of an auditor and the mercy of one, and who has come out the other side not humbler but hollower.

If that is you, this chapter is the one I most want you to read.

The problem, stated without a resolution

Scripture commands self-examination. Plainly and more than once. Examine yourselves, to see whether you are in the faith. Let a person examine himself. Search me, O God, and know my heart. It is not optional and I am not going to pretend it is.

And self-examination has driven people mad. Not metaphorically. The Puritans, who practised it more rigorously than anyone in the English-speaking world, are also the ones who documented most carefully what it does to certain people when it goes wrong — because they watched it happen in their own congregations, and in some cases in their own studies.

Both of those are true. This chapter is about how to hold them.

Terms
Morbid introspection
Self-examination that has stopped producing anything and become a closed loop — the same ground turned over indefinitely, generating distress rather than information.
Scrupulosity
A pattern in which moral or religious anxiety becomes obsessive: persistent fear of having sinned without knowing it, compulsive confession or reassurance-seeking, and an inability to accept forgiveness as settled. It is a recognized clinical presentation, it is treatable, and the treatment is not more devotion.

The Puritan who told people to stop

Richard Baxter came up briefly in chapter three; he earns his own section here.

Baxter is exactly the man you would expect to prescribe more self-examination. He was relentless — a pastor who visited every family in his parish personally and catechized them, who wrote something like 140 books while chronically ill, who took the state of a human soul as seriously as anyone ever has.

And when he wrote about melancholy, his counsel to sufferers was to examine themselves less. To avoid long solitude. To not spend their hours in the deep interior searching that he otherwise recommended to everybody. He had noticed something a lesser pastor would have missed: that for a certain kind of sick person, introspection is not the cure. It is the illness eating.

That is a remarkable thing for a Puritan to write, and it did not come from a soft view of sin. It came from paying attention.

Warrant · Historical

Richard Baxter, The Cure of Melancholy and Overmuch Sorrow, by Faith and Physic — counsel to melancholy sufferers to avoid prolonged solitary self-examination, alongside physical treatment.

Historical warrant. Not a scriptural command, and not a rule for everyone — Baxter prescribed rigorous self-examination generally and made this a deliberate exception for the ill. Which is the point: he treated introspection as a medicine, with a dose and with contraindications.

Baxter told melancholy sufferers to examine themselves less. Not because he thought sin was small, but because he had noticed that for some people introspection is not the cure. It is the illness feeding.

Two questions that look identical

Here is the distinction that unlocks the whole thing, and once you have it you will see it everywhere.

When you look inward, you can be asking one of two completely different questions.

Question one: Am I acceptable?

Question two: What is going on with me, and what needs attention?

The second question is genuinely answerable by looking inward, and asking it is wisdom. It produces information: I am exhausted. I have been avoiding that phone call for three weeks. I was unkind on Thursday and I knew it at the time.

The first question is not answerable by looking inward at all, and this is the entire problem. Its answer is not located in you. It is a verdict, it was handed down elsewhere, on someone else’s record — and searching your interior for it is like searching your kitchen for a court ruling. You will look for a very long time and find nothing, and the not-finding will feel like evidence.

So the disease is not looking inward. The disease is asking question one with the tools of question two. That search has no floor and no termination condition, which is exactly why it can run for twenty years.

Self-examination is safe in exactly the proportion that the verdict is not at stake in it. The moment you are looking inward to find out whether you are acceptable, you have begun a search that cannot end, because the answer was never filed in there.

God is greater than your heart

Which brings us to what I think is the most pastorally useful verse in the New Testament for people like us, and hardly anyone quotes it.

John has just spent a chapter giving evidences — tests, ways of knowing you belong. And then he anticipates the person for whom the tests come back inconclusive, or worse. He says: by this we shall know that we are of the truth, and reassure our heart before him — for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything.

Read what he does there. He does not say your heart is wrong about the facts. He does not say you are better than you think. He says something far more useful: your heart does not have jurisdiction. It has an opinion, it is delivering that opinion loudly, and it has been outranked. God knows more than your self-accusation knows, including all the things it has on you, and his verdict stands over it.

Warrant · Direct / Entailed · Cornerstone

1 John 3:19–20 — “by this we shall know that we are of the truth and reassure our heart before him; for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything.” (ESV)

The third and last cornerstone in this book — the marking is deliberately rare, and the three are the engine (Philippians 2:12–13), the attending (Psalm 139), and this. Directly stated: God’s knowledge outranks the self-accusing heart, and this is offered as the ground of reassurance. Entailed: that self-examination which ends in self-condemnation has not gone far enough rather than too far — it stopped at the heart’s verdict instead of appealing past it.

And this is where your assurance actually rests, which is worth stating as plainly as possible because it is the load-bearing beam under the whole book.

You do not establish that you are loved by inspecting yourself. The evidence is not in there. It is outside you, in history, in a garden and on a hill, in something that happened whether or not you can currently feel it. If assurance depended on the quality of your inner life, it would rise and fall with your sleep — and any God who arranged things that way would have made the most important thing in the universe contingent on your serotonin.

The old confession is unusually gentle about this, and I think it is worth knowing that the tradition wrote the gentleness in. It says explicitly that true believers may have their assurance shaken, diminished, and interrupted; that they may wait a long time for it; and that they are not therefore without the thing itself. Assurance and salvation are not the same object. You can hold the second without feeling the first.

Warrant · Historical · Confessional

Westminster Confession XVIII.3–4 — that assurance may be shaken, diminished, and intermitted; that believers may wait long for it; and that they are never left utterly without support.

Confessional warrant, which sits above ordinary historical warrant and below Scripture. Included because it is a documented, deliberate provision in the Protestant tradition for exactly the person who cannot presently feel what they believe.

The watchman still has a post

I need to correct something before it hardens, because chapter five put the emphasis hard on the passive — you are observed — and that could be heard as saying attention is entirely received and never assigned.

It is not. Both are true and neither cancels the other.

You are observed, always, by someone kind. And Proverbs assigns you a post: keep your heart with all vigilance. That verb is a guard’s verb; it is what a watchman does on a wall. Being watched over does not relieve you of watching. It changes what the watching is for — you are not standing sentry to determine whether you belong inside the walls. You are standing sentry because you do.

Which is, incidentally, another straight application of chapter four’s test. Both sides get heavier: God’s attention to you is total, and your responsibility for your inner life is real. Discount either one and you get something worse — passivity in one direction, exhaustion in the other.

Four rules that make it safe

So, practically. How do you examine yourself without drowning? Four rules, all of them drawn from how Scripture and the tradition actually do it rather than from how we usually do it.

1. Bounded. Psalm 139 is a prayer, and prayers end. David asks God to search him in two verses, not for an afternoon. Set an actual limit — ten minutes, a walk, one page — and stop when you reach it whether or not you have found anything. Especially if you have not found anything. Baxter would tell you that the not-finding is where the danger begins.

2. Companioned. David does not search himself. He asks God to search him, which is a request made to somebody — and it is the same third beat from chapter five, applied to the hardest material. And bring it to a human being too. Isolation is the accelerant here; almost nobody spirals in company. If you cannot say the thing out loud to one person, that is itself the finding.

3. Terminal. It has to end in something. Confession and receiving forgiveness. A decision. A specific thing you will do or stop. Handing it over. An examination that does not terminate is not an examination; it is a loop with better vocabulary. If nothing ever closes, the process is not working, however sincere it feels.

4. Downstream of the verdict. This one is the load-bearing rule and it is not a technique. You look because you are already safe, not to establish whether you are. Christ’s work is what makes it survivable to see yourself accurately — you can afford the truth about yourself precisely because the truth about yourself was never what was holding the relationship together.

You can afford to see yourself clearly only because what you see was never what was holding you. Take away the verdict and honest self-knowledge becomes unaffordable — which is why people who cannot rest cannot look.

How to tell which one you are doing

Three questions. They take about fifteen seconds and they are reliable.

  • Does it produce action, or paralysis? Examination generates something you can do. Rumination generates weight.
  • Does it end? Examination reaches a conclusion. Rumination circles.
  • Am I finding new things, or re-finding the same thing? The tenth pass over identical ground is not thoroughness. It is the loop.

If you get three noes, stop. Not because the material is unimportant, but because you have stopped being able to work it — and the honest thing at that point is to hand it to someone else rather than keep grinding.

Warrant · Interpretation · commonly weaponized

2 Corinthians 13:5 — “Examine yourselves, to see whether you are in the faith.”

Flagged because it is regularly used to drive exactly the endless search this chapter warns against. In context Paul is writing to a church that has been questioning his legitimacy, and he turns it round: look at yourselves — Christ is in you, isn’t he? The expected answer is yes. It is a confidence-building rhetorical move addressed to a congregation, not an instruction to conduct an open-ended audit of your own sincerity. The command to self-examine is real. This verse is not the whip it gets used as.

Case · the search with no floor

“A.” is sixty-one and has been a Christian since he was nineteen. He prays every morning, and most mornings the prayer becomes an inventory: is he really converted, was his repentance genuine at the time, is the fact that he has to keep asking itself the evidence that the answer is no.

He has asked three pastors. All three reassured him. All three reassurances held for between one and four days. He has concluded from this that they do not know him well enough to answer properly, and he has stopped asking, because asking a fourth time would be shameful.

A. is not lacking information. He has been given the right answer three times. He is asking a question in a place where its answer is not kept, and every failure to find it there confirms the fear that sent him looking.

Composite. Not a real person. This presentation is common enough to have a name, and it belongs with a clinician.

For a counselor

What to listen for. Religious clients who present with what looks like doctrinal doubt are sometimes running a reassurance-seeking loop rather than an intellectual problem. The tell is the durability of relief: an intellectual doubt stays resolved once answered; a compulsive one returns within days and requires re-asking. If reassurance decays on a predictable schedule, you are probably not looking at a question.

Why theological reassurance often fails and can backfire. Supplying the answer again is supplying the compulsion. It relieves acutely and strengthens the loop, and a well-meaning pastor can spend years reinforcing it. This is worth naming with the client’s clergy if the client consents.

What this chapter contributes structurally. It attempts to close the question rather than answer it — arguing that the client’s standing is not located internally, is therefore not discoverable by introspection, and that the search itself is the category error. Whatever you make of the content, the move is to remove the target rather than repeatedly hit it.

The four rules above — bounded, companioned, terminal, and downstream of a settled verdict — are offered as a structure you can hold the client to. The first three are usable in any framework.

Practice

The bounded examen. Once a day, ten minutes, timed — and stop at ten whether or not you are finished. Three questions, in this order, and the order matters:

  1. What am I grateful for today? (Start here. Starting with sin means you never arrive at anything else.)
  2. What went wrong today, and what was mine in it? Name it plainly, once, without adjectives about yourself.
  3. Hand it over and close it. Say it to God, receive that it is dealt with, and stop. Do not reopen it tonight.

Run the three-question test on the next episode of inward searching you catch yourself in. Action or paralysis? Does it end? New material or the same ground?

Journaling.

  1. Which of the two questions have you actually been asking — am I acceptable, or what needs attention? Look at the last month of evidence rather than your intention.
  2. Write down what you would need to find in yourself to feel finally acceptable. Then look at what you wrote and ask whether any human being has ever found that.
  3. Read 1 John 3:19–20 slowly. Whose heart is doing the condemning, and who is greater than it?

To bring to your counselor. “I think my self-examination has become a loop. Can we look at whether reassurance actually holds for me?”

Memory · Chapter Eight

“Whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything.”

1 John 3:20 (ESV)

That closes Part II. The four chapters have been about attention: where to point it, what to say when you get there, what to put in the room, and how to look inward without falling in.

All of it assumes something that has not yet been faced directly — that you can bear what you find. Part III is about the days you cannot: what to do when the distress does not lift and no skill touches it, and what Christianity has to say about the one night when even the Son of God asked to be let off.
